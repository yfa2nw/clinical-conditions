Stroke syndromes: localizing the lesion from the cortex to the spinal cord

Stroke symptoms follow the anatomy of the lesion rather than its cause. Cerebral syndromes reflect the ACA, MCA, PCA, and watershed territories, brainstem syndromes produce crossed signs, and spinal cord lesions separate motor, pain, and proprioceptive pathways. Hemorrhagic strokes add features of raised ICP and meningeal irritation. Here, we review these syndromes and their complications, and consider how to distinguish those that resemble each other.

Key points

  • Brainstem strokes are crossed: cranial nerve signs are ipsilateral, and limb signs are contralateral.
  • ACA favors the leg, whereas MCA favors the face and arm.
  • Dominant MCA lesions cause aphasia; non-dominant lesions cause neglect.
  • Thunderclap headache with meningeal signs suggests SAH.

Cerebral ischemic syndromes

ACA syndrome causes contralateral leg weakness, greater than arm, contralateral leg sensory loss, urinary incontinence, abulia, and a grasp reflex. MCA syndrome causes contralateral face and arm weakness, greater than leg, gaze toward the lesion, contralateral face and arm sensory loss, and contralateral homonymous hemianopia; dominant lesions add aphasia and Gerstmann syndrome, and non-dominant lesions add extinction, hemispatial neglect, and anosognosia. PCA syndrome usually causes no weakness, with contralateral sensory loss if the thalamus is involved, contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing, alexia without agraphia (dominant), and prosopagnosia (non-dominant). MCA/ACA watershed syndrome, from low perfusion or ICA stenosis, causes proximal arm and shoulder weakness sparing the hand and face ("man in a barrel" if bilateral), mild proximal arm sensory loss, and transcortical motor aphasia (dominant). MCA/PCA watershed syndrome usually causes no weakness and mild sensory change, with visual field loss, transcortical sensory aphasia (dominant), neglect (non-dominant), and Balint syndrome if bilateral.

Brainstem strokes

In the midbrain, supplied by P1 paramedian branches and the top of the basilar, Weber syndrome (ventromedial) causes ipsilateral CN III palsy with a dilated pupil and contralateral hemiparesis. Benedikt syndrome (paramedian) causes ipsilateral CN III palsy, contralateral tremor and ataxia, and contralateral vibration and proprioception loss, combining Weber and Claude features. Claude syndrome causes ipsilateral CN III palsy and contralateral ataxia, with no weakness. In the pons, ventromedial pontine syndrome, from basilar paramedian branches, causes contralateral hemiparesis, dysarthria, and ataxic hemiparesis, and its caudal form (Raymond syndrome) adds ipsilateral CN VI palsy. Ventral pontine (Millard-Gubler) syndrome causes contralateral hemiplegia, ipsilateral CN VI and CN VII palsy (forehead included), and horizontal diplopia. Inferior medial pontine (Foville) syndrome causes ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy and CN VII palsy (forehead included), contralateral hemiparesis, contralateral vibration and proprioception loss, and eyes that deviate toward the weak side; both are supplied by basilar paramedian and short circumferential branches. Lateral pontine (AICA) syndrome causes ipsilateral CN VII palsy (forehead included) and ataxia with preserved strength, crossed pain and temperature loss (ipsilateral face and contralateral body), and ipsilateral hearing loss, vertigo, and Horner syndrome. Locked-in syndrome, from bilateral ventral pons injury via the basilar artery, causes quadriplegia and anarthria, usually with preserved sensation, consciousness, vertical eye movements, and blinking. In the medulla, lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome, from the vertebral artery or PICA, causes ipsilateral palate, pharynx, and vocal cord weakness and ataxia with preserved strength, crossed pain and temperature loss, and ipsilateral Horner syndrome, vertigo, dysphagia, and hiccups. Medial medullary (Dejerine) syndrome, from the anterior spinal artery, causes contralateral hemiparesis sparing the face, ipsilateral tongue weakness with deviation toward the lesion, and contralateral vibration and proprioception loss.

Spinal cord lesions

Central cord syndrome, from hyperextension injury and syringomyelia, causes arm weakness greater than leg, with the hands worst, cape-like pain and temperature loss, and sacral sparing. Anterior cord syndrome, from the anterior spinal artery, causes bilateral weakness and pain and temperature loss below the lesion, preserved vibration and proprioception, and bladder and bowel dysfunction. Posterior cord syndrome, from the posterior spinal artery, tabes dorsalis, and B12 deficiency, causes sensory ataxia with preserved strength, bilateral vibration and proprioception loss, and a positive Romberg sign. Brown-Séquard syndrome (cord hemisection), from penetrating trauma and compression, causes ipsilateral weakness and vibration and proprioception loss below the lesion, contralateral pain and temperature loss, and ipsilateral Horner syndrome if at or above T1.

Hemorrhagic strokes

Intracerebral hemorrhage causes headache and cortical focal neurologic deficits. Subarachnoid hemorrhage causes thunderclap headache and meningeal signs: photophobia, neck stiffness, and positive Kernig and Brudzinski signs. Both cause raised ICP, with nausea and vomiting, posturing, and Cushing triad, along with loss of consciousness in intracerebral hemorrhage and CN deficits in subarachnoid hemorrhage. Subarachnoid hemorrhage is complicated by rebleeding, highest early before the aneurysm is secured; vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia on days 3–14; hydrocephalus; hyponatremia from SIADH or cerebral salt wasting; and seizures.

Complications

Stroke complications are cerebral edema (malignant MCA infarct, with herniation within 2–5 days), hemorrhagic transformation, aspiration pneumonia from dysphagia, seizures, DVT and PE from immobility, spasticity and contractures, post-stroke depression, and central post-stroke pain from thalamic infarct.

Acute ischemic stroke: embolic, thrombotic, and low-flow injury and the race to reperfuse

Blocked or inadequate arterial blood flow leads to cerebral ischemia, causing an infarct core surrounded by a salvageable penumbra. Embolus is the most common mechanism; the others are thrombus, global hypoperfusion, vasculitis, and dissection. Management is a race to reperfuse within fixed time windows, followed by prevention tailored to the cause. Here, we review its causes, imaging, acute treatment, and recurrence prevention, and consider how it relates to hemorrhagic stroke.

Key points

  • Glucose and non-contrast CT head come first, to rule out hypoglycemia and hemorrhage.
  • IV alteplase or tenecteplase is given within 4.5 hours of onset.
  • Thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion extends up to 24 hours with penumbra.
  • Prevention of recurrence is chosen by cause.

Pathophysiology

Blocked or inadequate flow causes cerebral ischemia with decreased oxygen delivery. An embolus or thrombus stops flow downstream. In the infarct core, flow is too low for cells to survive, so injury is irreversible, whereas in the penumbra, flow stops function but not survival, so reperfusion can reverse deficits. Failing ion pumps swell cells, causing cerebral edema, with malignant MCA infarct and herniation within 2–5 days, and reperfusion of injured vessels can cause hemorrhagic transformation, with blood within the infarct.

Etiology and mechanisms

Cardiac embolus, an important cause, arises from a left atrial thrombus in A-fib or flutter, mechanical valve, or RHD, or from a left ventricular thrombus in LV aneurysm from MI, anterior or lateral MI, or HFrEF. Infective endocarditis is a separate source of cardiac embolus and a contraindication to thrombolysis. Paradoxical embolus occurs with ASD, patent foramen ovale, and atrial septal aneurysm, where a right-to-left shunt lets a venous thrombus reach arterial circulation. Global hypoperfusion from cardiac arrest, acute respiratory failure, or ICA stenosis with low BP decreases cerebral oxygen delivery, causing watershed infarcts. Large vessel thrombus is atherosclerotic thrombus in the ICA, MCA, and vertebrobasilar arteries. Small vessel thrombus causes lacunar infarcts from the lenticulostriate arteries or small pontine branches. Vasculitis from autoimmune disease, infections, or primary CNS vasculitis causes arterial wall inflammation and can affect any artery. Dissection from trauma, iatrogenic injury, or connective tissue diseases causes an intimal tear with thrombus and embolus.

Diagnosis

Glucose and non-contrast CT head rule out hypoglycemia and hemorrhage. If there is no hemorrhage, CT angiogram of the head and neck looks for large vessel occlusion, and CT perfusion or MRI looks for penumbra if onset is over 6 hours or unknown. Cardiac embolus is sought with ECG, telemetry, and echocardiogram, looking for A-fib and thrombus. Paradoxical embolus is sought with bubble echocardiogram, looking for PFO. Large vessel thrombus is assessed by CT angiogram or carotid duplex for ICA stenosis, small vessel thrombus by MRI for lacunar infarct, vasculitis by ESR, CRP, autoimmune serology, and CSF, dissection by CT or MR angiogram of the neck, and infective endocarditis by blood cultures and echocardiogram. Vascular risk factors are assessed with lipids and HbA1c. NIHSS grades stroke severity.

Stroke mimics

Stroke mimics must be recognized without delaying reperfusion. Hypoglycemia causes deficits that resolve with glucose. A seizure with Todd paralysis is favored by a witnessed seizure, tongue biting, and a post-ictal state. Migraine with aura spreads over minutes, with positive symptoms and past similar attacks. Functional neurologic disorder shows inconsistent signs, a positive Hoover sign, and normal imaging. Brain tumor or abscess has a gradual onset, with fever in an abscess, and is seen on CT or MRI with contrast. Bell palsy causes isolated facial weakness that includes the forehead, peripheral vertigo causes isolated vertigo with a reassuring HINTS examination, and systemic illness such as sepsis, hyponatremia, or drugs can unmask an old deficit.

Management

Reperfusion uses IV alteplase or tenecteplase within 4.5 hours of onset, and mechanical thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion up to 24 hours with penumbra. Aspirin is given within 24–48 hours, waiting 24 hours after thrombolysis. Supportive care comprises a swallow screen before oral intake, treating fever and glucose, and DVT prophylaxis. BP is kept below 185/110 for thrombolysis, and otherwise allowed up to 220/120. Thrombolysis contraindications are hemorrhage on CT, stroke or head trauma within 3 months, intracranial or spinal surgery within 3 months, active bleeding, platelets under 100000/µL, INR over 1.7, and infective endocarditis.

Prevention of recurrence

Cardiac embolus is treated with anticoagulation: DOAC, or warfarin for a mechanical valve. Infective endocarditis is treated with ABX. Paradoxical embolus is treated with antiplatelet therapy, with PFO closure if under 60 with no other cause. Large vessel thrombus is treated with antiplatelet therapy and high-intensity statin, with carotid endarterectomy for symptomatic ICA stenosis of 70–99%. Small vessel thrombus is treated with antiplatelet therapy, statin, and BP control. Global hypoperfusion is treated by treating the cause of low perfusion. Vasculitis is treated with immunosuppression and treating any infection. Dissection is treated with antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulation.

Intracerebral hemorrhage: causes by location, and limiting hematoma growth

Vessel rupture bleeds into the brain parenchyma, forming a hematoma that compresses tissue and raises ICP. Hypertension is the most common cause under 60, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy over 60, with location helping to separate them. Management aims to limit hematoma growth, reverse coagulopathy, and control ICP. Here, we review its causes, workup, and treatment, and consider how it relates to ischemic stroke and subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Key points

  • Hypertension bleeds in deep structures, especially the basal ganglia; amyloid angiopathy bleeds in the cortex.
  • If systolic is 150–220, lower it to a target of 140.
  • Reversal is matched to the drug.
  • Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis is treated with anticoagulation, even with hemorrhage.

Pathophysiology

A weakened vessel ruptures into the brain parenchyma, forming a hematoma. The hematoma presses on brain tissue, causing cortical focal neurologic deficits, and bleeding can continue, causing worsening deficits, which is why BP control and reversal limit it. Injured tissue swells around the hematoma, adding to its mass. This added mass raises ICP, causing headache, nausea and vomiting, loss of consciousness, posturing, and Cushing triad, and blood or mass effect can block CSF flow, causing hydrocephalus that may need an EVD.

Etiology and mechanisms

Hypertension, the most common cause under 60, bleeds in the basal ganglia, pons, and cerebellum, and less commonly the cortex. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy, the most common cause over 60, bleeds in cortical structures and may present with dementia. Coagulopathy follows warfarin, heparin, DOACs, tPA, aspirin, clopidogrel, and liver disease. Hemorrhagic transformation follows ischemic stroke, through conversion from tPA or reperfusion injury. Arteriovenous malformation is a vascular abnormality of young patients. Mycotic aneurysm follows infective endocarditis vegetation deposits. Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis follows factor V Leiden, decreased AT-III, decreased protein C or S, APS, OCP, and pregnancy, with sinus swelling near the cortex. Malignancy, including papillary thyroid cancer, small cell lung carcinoma, RCC, melanoma, and choriocarcinoma, disrupts the BBB. Causes from coagulopathy onward can bleed at any site.

Clinical presentation

Patients have headache and cortical focal neurologic deficits, with raised ICP causing nausea and vomiting, loss of consciousness, posturing, and Cushing triad.

Diagnosis

Non-contrast CT head shows hyperdense blood in the parenchyma, bloods comprise FBC, PT/INR, aPTT, and LFTs, and CT angiogram is obtained if the patient is young, the bleed is lobar, or there is no hypertension. Hypertension shows a deep location on CT. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy is evaluated by MRI with SWI, showing lobar microbleeds. Coagulopathy is evaluated by those bloods. Hemorrhagic transformation is seen on CT head after stroke or thrombolysis as blood within the infarct. Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis is evaluated by CT or MR venography, with the empty delta sign. Malignancy is evaluated by MRI with contrast, showing an enhancing mass. Arteriovenous malformation is evaluated by CT angiogram, with catheter angiography to confirm. Mycotic aneurysm is evaluated by blood cultures, echocardiogram, and CT angiogram. Seizures: lowered thresholds, the patterns they produce, and stepwise control

Excess excitation or lost inhibition lowers the seizure threshold, so cortical neurons fire in excessive synchrony. An etiology raises excitability, and triggers such as stress, sleep deprivation, and bright lights and sounds can then push the brain over the threshold. Seizures are focal or generalized, and their features follow the region involved. Management stabilizes the patient, stops prolonged seizures in timed steps, treats the cause, and selects long-term medication by seizure type. Here, we review these mechanisms, causes, patterns, and treatments, and consider how seizures relate to other neurologic conditions.

Key points

  • Causes follow VITAMIN DEFG, and triggers act on top of an etiology.
  • Focal seizures follow their lobe: motor in the frontal lobe, sensory in the parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes, and autonomic and psychological in the temporal lobe and insula.
  • Reticular formation or thalamic involvement brings LOC, aura, and a post-ictal state.
  • Absence seizures have no post-ictal state, and EEG shows a 3 Hz spike-and-wave pattern.
  • A seizure lasting 5 minutes or more is status epilepticus and is treated in timed steps.

Pathophysiology

Glutamate opens Na+ and Ca2+ channels, causing depolarization, and GABA opens Cl− channels, causing hyperpolarization. When excitation wins, the seizure threshold falls and neurons fire together rather than independently. In a focal seizure, firing starts in one region, so symptoms match that region; in a generalized seizure, firing involves both hemispheres from the start, or spreads to them.

Etiology

Vascular causes are AIS, ICH, SAH, PRES from increased BP, and anoxic brain injury from global hypoxia. Infectious causes are meningitis, encephalitis, and brain abscess, and traumatic causes are epidural and subdural hematomas. Autoimmune causes are SLE and paraneoplastic syndrome, leading to autoimmune encephalitis. Metabolic causes are decreased vitamin B1, hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia, hyponatremia or hypernatremia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia, increased urea and metabolic waste, increased ammonia, and increased thyroid hormones. Iatrogenic causes are opioids, TCAs, isoniazid, salicylate, cocaine, cefepime, amphetamine, metronidazole, penicillin, bupropion, lithium, and benzodiazepine or alcohol withdrawal. Idiopathic causes are epilepsy, with non-adherence to medications, West syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, and juvenile myoclonic epilepsy. Neoplastic causes are glioblastoma, meningioma, and metastatic cancer, and degenerative causes are Alzheimer disease and MS. Eclampsia, fever, and genetic causes complete the list. Triggers are stress, sleep deprivation, and bright lights and sounds.

Clinical presentation

A seizure can pass through an aura, an ictal event, and a post-ictal state. Focal motor seizures arise in the frontal lobe and are atonic, tonic, clonic, or myoclonic, or show a Jacksonian march. Focal autonomic and psychological seizures arise in the temporal lobe and insula and cause increased HR and BP, urinary incontinence, salivation, and sweating; anger and hypersexuality; and déjà vu, depersonalization, and derealization. If the reticular formation or thalamus is involved, there is LOC, aura, and a post-ictal state, and if not, there is none of these. Auras include déjà vu, nausea, and automatisms such as rapid blinking, chewing, and lip smacking.

Generalized seizures can follow a focal seizure. Motor seizures are atonic, tonic, clonic, myoclonic, or tonic-clonic, with LOC and a post-ictal state, and may feature an involuntary cry or scream, upgaze, pooling of oral secretions, tongue biting, urinary or fecal incontinence, and increased HR and BP. Absence seizures are non-motor, with staring, daydreaming, automatisms, and no loss of muscle tone. Environmental factors are EBV, HHV-6, and decreased vitamin D. Genetic and demographic factors are HLA-DR2 and being female and aged 20–40 years.

Clinical presentation

Optic neuritis causes decreased visual acuity, color blindness, and RAPD, and MLF damage causes bilateral INO. Pseudobulbar palsy causes decreased chewing, increased jaw jerk, absent facial expressions, dysphagia, dysphonia, increased gag reflex, dysarthria, and spastic tongue. Cerebellar involvement causes ataxia and intention tremor. Lhermitte sign is an electric shock sensation down the spine on neck flexion, and in Uhthoff phenomenon, symptoms worsen with heat. Dorsal column damage causes loss of fine touch, vibration, and conscious proprioception, and spinothalamic damage causes loss of pain and temperature. Upper motor neuron signs are muscle weakness, increased tone, hyperreflexia, and positive Babinski sign. Bladder dysfunction causes incontinence with SNS damage and retention with PNS damage.

Diagnosis

First-line testing is MRI brain and spine with gadolinium, which shows T2 plaques in periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial, and spinal cord sites, with active plaques enhancing. The McDonald criteria need dissemination in space, with plaques in at least 2 typical CNS sites, and dissemination in time, with new plaques over time, enhancing and non-enhancing plaques together, or CSF oligoclonal bands. Other causes must be excluded first. Second-line testing is lumbar puncture for CSF oligoclonal bands, if MRI is not diagnostic. Extra tests are visual evoked potentials, which show slowed conduction in the optic nerve, and bloods to exclude mimics: B12, TFTs, ANA, syphilis serology, HIV, and AQP4 and MOG antibodies.

MS mimics

NMOSD causes severe optic neuritis and long spinal cord lesions, with AQP4 antibodies, and MOGAD causes bilateral optic neuritis, often with disc swelling, with MOG antibodies. ADEM is one episode after an infection, with encephalopathy, usually in children. B12 deficiency causes dorsal column and corticospinal signs with anemia. SLE is suggested by rash, joint pain, and kidney disease, with ANA, and neurosarcoidosis by lung disease and CN palsies, with CXR and serum ACE. Neurosyphilis, HIV, and Lyme disease are found by serology. Small vessel disease occurs with older age and vascular risk factors, with no oligoclonal bands.

Management

An acute relapse is treated with IV methylprednisolone for 3–5 days, with plasma exchange if refractory. Chronic disease-modifying therapy includes the injectable drugs interferon beta and glatiramer acetate, the oral drugs dimethyl fumarate, teriflunomide, and fingolimod, and the high-efficacy drugs natalizumab, ocrelizumab, and alemtuzumab. Natalizumab can cause PML, so JC virus status is checked first. Symptoms are controlled with baclofen for spasticity, oxybutynin for incontinence, catheterization for retention, gabapentin for neuropathic pain, and avoiding heat in Uhthoff phenomenon.

Relationships to other conditions

. Both cause loss of vibration and proprioception, and B12 deficiency is a cause of posterior cord syndrome, which is why B12 is checked. Low B12 favors deficiency, whereas MRI plaques and CSF oligoclonal bands favor multiple sclerosis.

Alzheimer disease: amyloid plaques, tau tangles, and progressive neuronal loss

Amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles cause gliosis and neuronal loss, leading to progressive dementia. Plaques arise when β-secretase and γ-secretase cleave APP to release Aβ, and tangles arise when hyperphosphorylated tau leaves microtubules and aggregates inside neurons. Age is the main risk factor, and mutations in APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 cause early-onset disease. Loss of neurons in the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and cholinergic and monoaminergic nuclei produces memory loss and later global decline. Diagnosis in life is clinical, with biomarkers, and the ABC score is made after death. Here, we review these mechanisms, the symptoms they produce, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider how Alzheimer disease relates to other neurologic conditions.

Key points

  • α-secretase cuts APP inside the Aβ region, so no Aβ forms, whereas β-secretase followed by γ-secretase releases Aβ.
  • PSEN1 and PSEN2 encode the catalytic component of γ-secretase, and their mutations cause early-onset familial disease.
  • Aβ begins in the neocortex and moves inward, whereas NFTs begin in the entorhinal cortex and move outward.
  • Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors offset the loss of cholinergic neurons from the nucleus basalis of Meynert.
  • The ABC score grades Aβ plaques, NFTs, and neuritic plaques, and is made after death.

Pathophysiology

Aβ is produced from APP, forms plaques outside neurons alongside NFTs inside them, and activates microglia and astrocytes, causing gliosis. Neuronal loss then causes atrophy and dementia.

APP is processed by two pathways. In the non-amyloidogenic pathway, α-secretase cuts inside the Aβ region, so no Aβ forms when γ-secretase then cuts within the membrane. In the amyloidogenic pathway, β-secretase cuts at the start of the Aβ region, and the γ-secretase cut releases Aβ. Aβ aggregates into oligomers and then fibrils, which deposit outside neurons as plaques. The presenilins PSEN1 and PSEN2 are the catalytic component of γ-secretase, and PEN2, APH1, and nicastrin are the other components of the complex.

Tau is a microtubule-associated protein that stabilizes axons. Hyperphosphorylation detaches tau from microtubules, and tau then aggregates inside neurons as NFTs. Axonal transport fails, and the neurons die.

The two lesions spread differently. Aβ follows the Thal phases, from the neocortex to the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, then the basal ganglia and thalamus, the brainstem, and the cerebellum. NFTs follow the Braak stages, from the entorhinal cortex (I–II) to limbic areas (III–IV) and the neocortex (V–VI). NFTs track symptoms better than plaques do, because they follow the circuits that fail.

Etiology

Decreased Aβ clearance is represented by age, the main risk factor, and APOE ε4, which raises the risk of late-onset disease. Increased Aβ production is represented by early-onset mutations in APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2, and by Down syndrome, in which chromosome 21 carries an extra APP gene. Vascular injury from hypertension, diabetes, and smoking adds to neuronal loss.

Clinical presentation

Symptoms follow the structures affected. The entorhinal cortex is the first site of NFTs, causing early memory loss and getting lost, and damage to hippocampus CA1 causes short-term memory loss. Noradrenergic loss in the locus coeruleus changes attention and mood, and serotonergic loss in the raphe nuclei causes depression and sleep changes. Cholinergic neuron loss in the nucleus basalis of Meynert causes memory and attention decline.

Early disease causes short-term memory loss and word-finding difficulty. Middle disease adds disorientation, apraxia, agnosia, and behavior changes, and late disease causes dependence, mutism, and incontinence. Complications are aspiration pneumonia from dysphagia, falls and pressure injuries from immobility, and agitation, wandering, and caregiver strain. Aβ in vessel walls causes cerebral amyloid angiopathy, with lobar ICH.

Diagnosis

Assessment begins with a history that includes a collateral source, followed by cognitive testing with the MMSE or MoCA. Bloods are FBC, TFTs, B12, and electrolytes, and MRI shows medial temporal atrophy. Biomarkers are added if the diagnosis is unclear: CSF shows decreased Aβ42 with increased tau and phosphorylated tau, and amyloid PET shows plaques in life. Genetic testing is considered with early onset and a strong family history.

The ABC score is a neuropathologic score made after death. A grades Aβ plaques by Thal phase, with A1 for phases 1–2, A2 for phase 3, and A3 for phases 4–5. B grades NFTs by Braak stage, with B1 for stages I–II, B2 for III–IV, and B3 for V–VI. C grades neuritic plaques by CERAD score, with C1 sparse, C2 moderate, and C3 frequent. The three grades combine into a level of Alzheimer disease neuropathologic change: not, low, intermediate, or high.

Dementia mimics

Delirium is favored by sudden onset, fluctuating attention, and an acute illness, and prompts a search for the cause. Depression shows low mood, poor effort, and awareness of memory problems. Hypothyroidism causes fatigue, cold intolerance, and weight gain, and is found with TFTs, and B12 deficiency causes anemia and neuropathy. Normal pressure hydrocephalus causes gait disturbance and urinary incontinence early, and is assessed with MRI. Dementia with Lewy bodies is favored by visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, and fluctuations. Vascular dementia shows stepwise decline with vascular risk factors, and frontotemporal dementia occurs at a younger age, with early behavior or language change; both are assessed with MRI.

Management

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors slow ACh breakdown, offsetting cholinergic neuron loss, and give a modest benefit in mild to moderate disease. The drugs are donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine, and they can cause bradycardia and GI upset. Memantine is an NMDA receptor antagonist for moderate to severe disease. The anti-amyloid antibodies lecanemab and donanemab clear plaques in early disease, with monitoring for ARIA.

Supportive care covers safety, routine, and caregiver support. Behavior is managed with non-drug approaches first, vascular risk factors are treated, and advance care planning begins early.

Relationships to other conditions

. Both cause progressive dementia. Visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, and fluctuations favor dementia with Lewy bodies, whereas early short-term memory loss with medial temporal atrophy favors Alzheimer disease.

. Aβ links the two conditions, since Aβ in vessel walls causes cerebral amyloid angiopathy and lobar ICH. Sudden focal deficits with blood on imaging favor hemorrhage, whereas gradual cognitive decline favors Alzheimer disease.

. Alzheimer disease is a degenerative cause of seizures, so treatment targets the underlying disease as well.
