Neurons and action potentials: structure, channels, and conduction

Neurons receive input on dendrites, integrate it at the axon hillock, and conduct all-or-none action potentials to their terminals. The resting potential of about −70 mV reflects potassium leak and the Na/K-ATPase, and voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels drive depolarization and repolarization. Refractory periods limit firing rate and keep conduction one-way, and myelin allows fast saltatory conduction. Demyelination, sodium channel mutations, and sodium channel toxins disrupt this process, and sodium channel blockers exploit it. Here, we review neuron structure, membrane channels, the action potential, and their failures.

Key points

  • The resting potential is about −70 mV, close to the potassium equilibrium potential, because potassium leak channels dominate at rest.
  • At threshold, about −55 mV, voltage-gated sodium channels open, producing an all-or-none spike.
  • Sodium channel inactivation and potassium efflux repolarize the membrane, and inactivation causes the absolute refractory period.
  • Myelin from oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells allows saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.
  • Lidocaine and phenytoin block voltage-gated sodium channels.

Structure

Dendrites receive afferent signals, the soma holds the nucleus, and the axon hillock initiates the action potential, which the axon carries to terminals that release neurotransmitter. Myelin, from oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the periphery, insulates the axon, leaving nodes of Ranvier packed with voltage-gated sodium channels. Most neurons, including motor neurons, are multipolar; some are bipolar; and sensory neurons of the dorsal root ganglia are pseudounipolar.

Channels and the action potential

Potassium leak channels make the resting membrane most permeable to potassium, so the resting potential of about −70 mV lies near the potassium equilibrium potential, and the Na/K-ATPase pumps 3 sodium out and 2 potassium in to maintain the gradients. When summed input reaches threshold, about −55 mV, voltage-gated sodium channels open and sodium rushes in, an all-or-none depolarization. Sodium channels then inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels open, repolarizing the membrane, and slow potassium channel closure causes hyperpolarization. At the terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels trigger transmitter release, and ligand-gated channels generate postsynaptic potentials.

During the absolute refractory period, inactivated sodium channels prevent another spike; during the relative refractory period, a stronger stimulus is needed. These periods set the maximum firing rate and keep conduction one-way. In unmyelinated axons, depolarization spreads to adjacent membrane slowly, whereas in myelinated axons current jumps from node to node, saltatory conduction, more than 10 times faster.

When it fails

Multiple sclerosis destroys oligodendrocytes, and MOGAD targets myelin, slowing or blocking conduction. Tetrodotoxin blocks sodium channels, causing numbness and weakness, and sodium channel mutations such as SCN1A raise excitability and cause epilepsy.

Pharmacology

Local anesthetics such as lidocaine block voltage-gated sodium channels in nerves, so pain signals fail to conduct. Antiseizure drugs such as phenytoin, carbamazepine, and lamotrigine reduce sodium influx in neurons, lowering excitability.

Relationships to other topics

. The action potential ends at the terminal, where calcium entry converts the electrical signal into chemical transmission.

Synapses and neurotransmitters: release, receptors, and clearance

At chemical synapses, an action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium-activated SNARE proteins fuse vesicles to release neurotransmitter. Transmitters act on fast ionotropic or slower metabotropic receptors, producing excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potentials that sum at the axon hillock, and are cleared by reuptake or enzymes. Glutamate is the main excitatory and GABA the main inhibitory transmitter, with acetylcholine, dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin modulating specific systems. Here, we review synaptic transmission, the major transmitters, their failures, and drugs that target them.

Key points

  • Calcium entry activates SNARE proteins, which fuse vesicles and release transmitter.
  • Ionotropic receptors are fast ion channels; metabotropic receptors act through G proteins.
  • Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter, GABA the main inhibitory transmitter in the brain, and glycine inhibitory in the cord.
  • Acetylcholine is cleared by AChE; small-molecule transmitters are cleared by reuptake.
  • Benzodiazepines increase GABA-A channel opening, and cholinesterase inhibitors raise acetylcholine.

Synaptic transmission

An action potential depolarizes the terminal, opening voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium activates SNARE proteins that fuse vesicles with the membrane. Transmitter binds ionotropic receptors, ion channels that act quickly, or metabotropic receptors, G protein-coupled receptors with slower, longer effects. Excitatory postsynaptic potentials depolarize the next neuron and inhibitory potentials hyperpolarize it, as chloride enters through GABA-A receptors, and inputs sum at the axon hillock. Transmitter is cleared by reuptake into the terminal or glia, for small molecules only, or by enzymes such as AChE. Electrical synapses pass current directly through gap junctions with almost no delay.

Neurotransmitters

Glutamate, acting on AMPA and NMDA receptors, is the main excitatory transmitter and underlies plasticity and memory. GABA, acting on GABA-A chloride channels and GABA-B G protein-coupled receptors, is the main inhibitory transmitter in the brain, and glycine is inhibitory mainly in the cord. Acetylcholine acts on nicotinic ion channels and muscarinic M1–M5 receptors at muscle, autonomic synapses, and the basal forebrain. Dopamine from the SNc and ventral tegmental area acts on D1 and D2 families, norepinephrine from the locus coeruleus drives arousal, and serotonin from the raphe nuclei modulates mood, sleep, and gut motility.

When it fails

Loss of cholinergic neurons contributes to memory decline in Alzheimer disease, and death of SNc dopamine neurons leaves too little dopamine in Parkinson disease. Glutamate excitotoxicity contributes to both, seizures arise when excitation outweighs GABA inhibition, and in hepatic encephalopathy, ammonia enhances GABA-A chloride flow.

Pharmacology

Benzodiazepines increase how often GABA-A chloride channels open, causing sedation and seizure control. Cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil block acetylcholine breakdown, botulinum toxin blocks acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, and SSRIs block serotonin reuptake.

Relationships to other topics

. The action potential arriving at the terminal opens the calcium channels that start release.

. Dopamine acts on D1 and D2 striatal neurons to balance the direct and indirect pathways.

Ascending tracts: dorsal columns, spinothalamic tracts, and spinocerebellar tracts

Ascending tracts carry sensation from the body to the thalamus and cortex or to the cerebellum. The dorsal column-medial lemniscus carries fine touch, vibration, and conscious proprioception, ascends on the same side, and crosses in the medulla. The spinothalamic tract carries pain, temperature, and crude touch and crosses in the cord within 1–2 segments of entry. The spinocerebellar tracts carry unconscious proprioception to the cerebellum on the same side. Where each crosses determines the side of deficits after a lesion. Here, we review their routes and failures.

Key points

  • The dorsal columns ascend on the same side and cross in the medulla as the medial lemniscus.
  • Spinothalamic fibers cross in the anterior white commissure 1–2 segments above entry.
  • A cord hemisection causes same-side dorsal column loss and opposite-side pain and temperature loss.
  • Spinocerebellar tracts end in the cerebellum on the same side, so their lesions cause same-side ataxia.

Routes

In the dorsal column-medial lemniscus, large DRG fibers ascend on the same side, gracilis from below T6 and cuneatus from T6 up, to the nucleus gracilis and cuneatus in the medulla. Second-order internal arcuate fibers cross and ascend as the medial lemniscus to the VPL thalamus, which projects to S1. The pathway carries fine touch, vibration, conscious proprioception, and two-point discrimination.

In the spinothalamic tract, thin Aδ and C fibers synapse in the dorsal horn, and second-order fibers cross in the anterior white commissure 1–2 segments above entry. The lateral tract carries sharp pain and temperature, with sacral fibers most lateral, and the anterior tract carries crude touch; both reach the VPL thalamus and S1, insula, and cingulate cortex.

The dorsal spinocerebellar tract arises in the Clarke nucleus (C8–L3) and ascends uncrossed through the inferior cerebellar peduncle, carrying leg and trunk information. The ventral tract arises from spinal border cells, crosses twice, and enters through the superior cerebellar peduncle, so both end on the same side, carrying unconscious proprioception from muscle spindles and tendon organs.

When it fails

A cord hemisection, Brown-Séquard syndrome, causes same-side dorsal column loss and weakness with opposite-side pain and temperature loss. Posterior cord lesions and dorsal column loss from B12 deficiency or tabes dorsalis cause vibration and position loss with a positive Romberg test, and syringomyelia interrupts crossing spinothalamic fibers, causing cape-like pain and temperature loss. Medial medullary syndrome damages the medial lemniscus, causing opposite-side vibration and position loss, and spinocerebellar loss, as in Friedreich ataxia, causes same-side ataxia.

Relationships to other topics

. Lesions often hit both: in a hemisected cord, the corticospinal tract adds same-side weakness to the sensory pattern.

Descending tracts: corticospinal, corticobulbar, and brainstem motor tracts

Descending tracts carry motor commands to cranial nerve nuclei and the spinal cord. The lateral corticospinal tract crosses at the pyramids and drives skilled distal movement, and the corticobulbar tract reaches cranial nerve nuclei mostly bilaterally. Brainstem tracts set posture and tone: the rubrospinal tract favors upper limb flexion, and the vestibulospinal and pontine reticulospinal tracts favor extension, whereas the medullary reticulospinal tract inhibits extensors. Lesions above or below the pyramidal decussation and the red nucleus produce predictable weakness and posturing. Here, we review these tracts and their failures.

Key points

  • About 90% of corticospinal fibers cross at the pyramidal decussation, so lesions above it cause opposite-side weakness.
  • Corticobulbar input is mostly bilateral, and the upper face receives input from both hemispheres.
  • The rubrospinal tract crosses in the midbrain and favors upper limb flexion.
  • Lesions above the red nucleus cause decorticate posturing; lesions below it cause decerebrate posturing.

Corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts

The lateral corticospinal tract arises from M1, the premotor cortex and SMA, and S1, and descends through the posterior limb of the internal capsule, cerebral peduncle, basis pontis, and pyramid; 90% of fibers cross at the pyramidal decussation to reach ventral horn neurons for distal limbs, driving skilled movement. The anterior corticospinal tract, about 10%, stays uncrossed until its segment and serves axial and proximal muscles on both sides. The corticobulbar tract arises from the face area of the motor cortex, passes through the genu of the internal capsule, and reaches the motor nuclei of CN V, VII, and IX–XII, mostly bilaterally; because the upper face receives input from both hemispheres, a cortical lesion spares the forehead.

Brainstem tracts

The rubrospinal tract arises from the red nucleus, which receives cortical and cerebellar input, crosses in the midbrain, and favors upper limb flexion. The lateral vestibulospinal tract, from the lateral vestibular nucleus, and the pontine reticulospinal tract descend uncrossed and excite antigravity extensors, whereas the medullary reticulospinal tract, mainly uncrossed, inhibits extensors, facilitates flexors, and contributes to autonomic and breathing control. A lesion above the red nucleus causes decorticate posturing, with flexed arms and extended legs, and a lesion below it causes decerebrate posturing, with extension of all limbs.

When it fails

Capsular or brainstem lesions above the decussation cause opposite-side spastic hemiparesis, and cord lesions below it cause same-side spasticity, hyperreflexia, and a Babinski sign. Corticobulbar lesions cause supranuclear facial palsy, sparing the forehead, and bilateral lesions cause pseudobulbar palsy, with dysarthria, dysphagia, and a brisk jaw jerk.

. Combined lesions localize precisely: a cord hemisection adds same-side corticospinal weakness to the sensory pattern.

Basal ganglia pathways: direct, indirect, and the role of dopamine

The basal ganglia select and scale movement through two opposing striatal output pathways. In the direct pathway, D1 medium spiny neurons inhibit the GPi and SNr, releasing the thalamus to drive movement; in the indirect pathway, D2 neurons inhibit the GPe, freeing the STN to excite the GPi and restrain movement. Dopamine from the SNc excites D1 and inhibits D2 neurons, so both actions favor movement. Dopamine loss causes parkinsonism, whereas indirect-pathway loss and STN lesions cause chorea and ballismus. Here, we review the circuit, its failures, and drugs that act on it.

Key points

  • The GPi and SNr tonically inhibit the VA and VL thalamus.
  • The direct pathway (D1) reduces that inhibition; the indirect pathway (D2, GPe, STN) increases it.
  • Dopamine excites D1 and inhibits D2 neurons, favoring movement through both pathways.
  • Dopamine loss in Parkinson disease causes bradykinesia; indirect-pathway loss in Huntington disease and STN lesions cause hyperkinesia.

Circuit

The striatum, made of the caudate and putamen, contains GABA medium spiny neurons that receive cortical and dopamine input. The GPi and SNr form the output and tonically inhibit the VA and VL thalamus, which relays to the motor cortex. In the direct pathway, D1 neurons inhibit the GPi and SNr, releasing the thalamus and allowing movement. In the indirect pathway, D2 neurons inhibit the GPe, which then inhibits the STN less, and the STN, the loop's only glutamate nucleus, excites the GPi, restraining movement. Dopamine from the SNc excites D1 and inhibits D2 neurons, so both actions favor movement.

When it fails

In Parkinson disease, loss of SNc dopamine weakens the direct pathway and strengthens the indirect pathway, increasing GPi inhibition and causing bradykinesia, and dopamine-blocking drugs cause parkinsonism by blocking D2 receptors. In Huntington disease, indirect-pathway neurons die first, reducing GPi inhibition and causing chorea, and an STN lesion removes the GPi's excitation, releasing the thalamus and causing hemiballismus.

Pharmacology

Levodopa crosses the BBB and becomes dopamine, with carbidopa blocking its peripheral conversion, and dopamine agonists such as pramipexole stimulate receptors directly. MAO-B and COMT inhibitors slow dopamine and levodopa breakdown. VMAT2 inhibitors such as tetrabenazine block vesicular loading of dopamine, reducing chorea, and D2 antagonists such as haloperidol reduce hyperkinesia but can cause parkinsonism.

Relationships to other topics

. Dopamine acts through D1 and D2 receptors, members of the metabotropic receptor family.

. The basal ganglia act through the thalamus and cortex, which drive movement through the corticospinal tract.

Cranial nerves I, II, and VIII carry smell, sight, hearing, and balance. Olfactory receptor neurons pass through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb and reach cortex without a thalamic relay.   • Smell reaches cortex without a thalamic relay, and olfactory receptor neurons regenerate.
  • Only nasal fibers cross at the chiasm, so chiasmal lesions cause bitemporal hemianopia and tract lesions homonymous hemianopia.
  • CN II is the afferent and CN III the efferent limb of the pupillary light reflex.

Olfactory and optic nerves

Olfactory receptor neurons, which regenerate for life, pass through the cribriform plate to synapse on mitral cells in the olfactory bulb, which project to the piriform cortex, amygdala, and entorhinal cortex without a thalamic relay. Ammonia tests the trigeminal nerve rather than smell. Retinal ganglion cells form the optic nerve, a CNS tract myelinated by oligodendrocytes, which passes through the optic canal to the chiasm, where nasal fibers cross. Each optic tract carries the opposite visual field to the LGN, and radiations reach V1 in the calcarine cortex, the lower field through Meyer loop in the temporal lobe. Optic nerve lesions blind one eye, chiasmal lesions cause bitemporal hemianopia, tract and V1 lesions cause homonymous hemianopia, and Meyer loop lesions cause an upper quadrantanopia. Pupil fibers leave before the LGN for the pretectum, and CN III constricts both pupils, so a damaged optic nerve produces an RAPD.

Vestibulocochlear nerve

Hair cells of the cochlea and vestibular labyrinth synapse on bipolar neurons of the spiral and Scarpa ganglia, whose axons pass through the internal acoustic meatus with CN VII to the cochlear and vestibular nuclei. Hearing becomes bilateral at the trapezoid body and ascends through the lateral lemniscus, inferior colliculus, and MGN to the Heschl gyrus, so one-sided deafness localizes to the ear, nerve, or cochlear nucleus. Vestibular nuclei project through the MLF for the VOR, to the vestibulospinal tracts, and to the cerebellum.

When it fails

Smell is lost early in Parkinson disease and after head injury. Optic neuritis demyelinates CN II, causing painful vision loss with an RAPD, and strokes behind the chiasm cause homonymous hemianopia. Vestibular schwannoma causes one-sided hearing loss and tinnitus, and vestibular neuritis causes vertigo with an abnormal head impulse.

Relationships to other topics

. CN III forms the efferent limb of the pupillary light reflex, and the vestibular nuclei drive eye movements through the MLF.

Oculomotor, trochlear, and abducens nerves: moving the eyes

Cranial nerves III, IV, and VI move the eyes. CN III supplies four extraocular muscles and the levator, and its parasympathetic fibers constrict the pupil; CN IV supplies the superior oblique and is the only nerve to leave the back of the brainstem; CN VI supplies the lateral rectus. Their palsies produce characteristic deviations, and the pattern of pupil involvement in CN III palsy separates compression from ischemia. The MLF links the abducens and oculomotor nuclei for conjugate gaze. Here, we review these nerves and their failures.

Key points

  • CN III palsy turns the eye down and out with ptosis and, if compressive, a dilated pupil.
  • A PComm aneurysm dilates the pupil early, whereas a diabetic infarct spares it.
  • CN IV palsy causes hypertropia and extorsion, with a head tilt away from the weak side.
  • CN VI palsy causes esotropia and is a false localizing sign of raised ICP.
  • An MLF lesion causes INO, as in MS.

Eye muscles

The lateral rectus (VI) abducts, the medial rectus (III) adducts, the superior rectus (III) elevates, and the inferior rectus (III) depresses. The superior oblique (IV) depresses and intorts, especially when adducted, the inferior oblique (III) elevates and extorts when adducted, and the levator (III) lifts the lid.

The three nerves

CN III arises with the Edinger-Westphal nucleus at the superior colliculus level, leaves through the interpeduncular fossa beside the PComm, runs in the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus, and enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure, its parasympathetic fibers synapsing in the ciliary ganglion. Its palsy turns the eye down and out with ptosis and a dilated pupil; because pupil fibers ride on the surface, a PComm aneurysm dilates the pupil early, whereas a diabetic infarct spares it. Every CN IV fiber crosses before leaving the back of the midbrain, and its palsy causes hypertropia and extorsion with a head tilt away from the weak side. CN VI arises under the facial colliculus, climbs the clivus, and runs free in the cavernous sinus; its palsy causes esotropia and can be a false localizing sign of raised ICP, and its nucleus is the horizontal gaze center. The MLF links the VI nucleus to the opposite III nucleus, and its lesion causes INO.

When it fails

A PComm aneurysm compresses CN III and blows the pupil, and Weber syndrome combines a same-side CN III palsy with opposite hemiparesis. MS demyelinates the MLF, causing INO, and progressive supranuclear palsy causes a supranuclear vertical gaze palsy with intact nerves.

Relationships to other topics

. CN II and III form the pupillary light reflex, and vestibular input drives the eyes through the MLF.

Trigeminal and facial nerves: face sensation, mastication, and expression

The trigeminal nerve carries sensation from the face through three divisions and supplies the muscles of mastication, with four nuclei extending from the midbrain to C2. The facial nerve supplies facial expression and the stapedius, carries parasympathetic fibers to the lacrimal and salivary glands, and carries taste from the front of the tongue. Together they form the corneal reflex. Upper and lower motor neuron facial palsies differ by forehead involvement, and trigeminal nuclear lesions cause dissociated facial sensory loss. Here, we review these nerves and their failures.

Key points

  • CN V has mesencephalic, principal sensory, spinal, and motor nuclei; the spinal nucleus carries facial pain and temperature to C2.
  • V1, V2, and V3 leave through the superior orbital fissure, foramen rotundum, and foramen ovale.

  • The facial nerve passes through the parotid without supplying it.

Trigeminal nerve

CN V has a mesencephalic nucleus for jaw proprioception, a principal sensory nucleus for touch, a spinal nucleus for pain and temperature extending to C2, and a motor nucleus. Its ophthalmic division, through the superior orbital fissure, supplies the forehead and cornea; its maxillary division, through the foramen rotundum, the cheek and upper jaw; and its mandibular division, through the foramen ovale, the lower jaw and front of the tongue, plus the masseter, temporalis, and pterygoids. Facial nerve

The facial nucleus lies in the pons, and its fibers loop over the VI nucleus before leaving at the cerebellopontine angle with CN VIII. At the geniculate ganglion, the greater petrosal nerve leaves for the lacrimal gland; in the facial canal, branches supply the stapedius and form the chorda tympani, which carries taste from the front two-thirds of the tongue and fibers to the submandibular and sublingual glands. Only motor fibers leave the stylomastoid foramen, and the nerve divides into 5 branches within the parotid without supplying it. The corneal reflex runs V1 in and VII out, and loss of the stapedius causes hyperacusis.

When it fails

A UMN lesion weakens the lower face and spares the forehead, whereas Bell palsy, an LMN lesion, weakens the whole half of the face with hyperacusis and taste loss. Lateral medullary syndrome damages the spinal trigeminal nucleus, causing same-side facial pain and temperature loss, and vestibular schwannoma can stretch CN VII at the cerebellopontine angle.

Relationships to other topics

. Corticobulbar input to the upper facial nucleus is bilateral, which is why UMN lesions spare the forehead.

Glossopharyngeal, vagus, accessory, and hypoglossal nerves: the lower cranial nerves

The lower cranial nerves leave the medulla and upper cervical cord. CN IX supplies the stylopharyngeus, the parotid, the carotid sinus and body, and the back of the tongue, forming the afferent limb of the gag reflex. CN X supplies the palate, pharynx, and larynx and carries parasympathetic fibers to the heart, lungs, and gut. CN XI supplies the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius, and CN XII the tongue. Their lesions cause dysphagia, hoarseness, weakness of head turning, and tongue deviation, and they localize medullary strokes. Here, we review these nerves and their failures.

Key points

  • CN IX is the afferent and CN X the efferent limb of the gag reflex.
  • The left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under the aortic arch.
  • The uvula deviates away from a vagal lesion, and the tongue toward a hypoglossal lesion.
  • The right sternocleidomastoid turns the head to the left.
  • Lateral medullary syndrome affects IX and X; medial medullary syndrome affects XII.

The four nerves

CN IX supplies its only muscle, the stylopharyngeus, sends parasympathetic fibers to the parotid through the otic ganglion, carries pressure and oxygen signals from the carotid sinus and body that slow the heart, and carries taste and touch from the back third of the tongue, forming the afferent limb of the gag reflex. CN X, from the nucleus ambiguus, supplies the palate, pharynx, and larynx and forms the efferent limb of the gag, and from the dorsal motor nucleus sends parasympathetic fibers to the heart, lungs, and gut to the splenic flexure; the left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under the aortic arch, and the uvula deviates away from a vagal lesion. CN XI arises from C1–C5, rises through the foramen magnum, and leaves through the jugular foramen to supply the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius, and can be injured in posterior triangle node biopsy. CN XII arises in the floor of the fourth ventricle, leaves between the pyramid and olive through the hypoglossal canal, and supplies all tongue muscles except palatoglossus; its lesion deviates the tongue toward the lesion, with wasting and fasciculations.

When it fails

Lateral medullary syndrome damages the nucleus ambiguus, causing dysphagia, hoarseness, and a reduced gag, and medial medullary syndrome damages CN XII, deviating the tongue toward the lesion with opposite hemiparesis. Bilateral UMN lesions cause pseudobulbar palsy, with dysarthria and dysphagia but no wasting, and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury causes hoarseness.

Relationships to other topics

. Corticobulbar fibers reach these motor nuclei, so bilateral UMN lesions cause pseudobulbar palsy.
