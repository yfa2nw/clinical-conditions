Neurons and action potentials: structure, channels, and conduction

Neurons receive input on dendrites, integrate it at the axon hillock, and conduct all-or-none action potentials to their terminals. The resting potential of about −70 mV reflects potassium leak and the Na/K-ATPase, and voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels drive depolarization and repolarization. Refractory periods limit firing rate and keep conduction one-way, and myelin allows fast saltatory conduction. Demyelination, sodium channel mutations, and sodium channel toxins disrupt this process, and sodium channel blockers exploit it. Here, we review neuron structure, membrane channels, the action potential, and their failures.

Key points

  • The resting potential is about −70 mV, close to the potassium equilibrium potential, because potassium leak channels dominate at rest.
  • At threshold, about −55 mV, voltage-gated sodium channels open, producing an all-or-none spike.
  • Sodium channel inactivation and potassium efflux repolarize the membrane, and inactivation causes the absolute refractory period.
  • Myelin from oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells allows saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.
  • Lidocaine and phenytoin block voltage-gated sodium channels.

Structure

Dendrites receive afferent signals, the soma holds the nucleus, and the axon hillock initiates the action potential, which the axon carries to terminals that release neurotransmitter. Myelin, from oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the periphery, insulates the axon, leaving nodes of Ranvier packed with voltage-gated sodium channels. Most neurons, including motor neurons, are multipolar; some are bipolar; and sensory neurons of the dorsal root ganglia are pseudounipolar.

Glial cells support neurons. Astrocytes buffer potassium, take up neurotransmitter, and with their foot processes help form the BBB; microglia, of monocyte origin, are the immune cells of the CNS; oligodendrocytes myelinate many CNS axons each, whereas each Schwann cell myelinates one PNS axon; and ependymal cells line the ventricles, with choroid epithelium regulating CSF. The BBB consists of capillary endothelial cells joined by tight junctions, supported by pericytes, astrocyte foot processes, and the basal lamina.

Channels and the action potential

Potassium leak channels make the resting membrane most permeable to potassium, so the resting potential of about −70 mV lies near the potassium equilibrium potential, and the Na/K-ATPase pumps 3 sodium out and 2 potassium in to maintain the gradients. When summed input reaches threshold, about −55 mV, voltage-gated sodium channels open and sodium rushes in, an all-or-none depolarization. Sodium channels then inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels open, repolarizing the membrane, and slow potassium channel closure causes hyperpolarization. At the terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels trigger transmitter release, and ligand-gated channels generate postsynaptic potentials.

During the absolute refractory period, inactivated sodium channels prevent another spike; during the relative refractory period, a stronger stimulus is needed. These periods set the maximum firing rate and keep conduction one-way. In unmyelinated axons, depolarization spreads to adjacent membrane slowly, whereas in myelinated axons current jumps from node to node, saltatory conduction, more than 10 times faster.

When it fails

Multiple sclerosis destroys oligodendrocytes, MOGAD targets myelin, and NMOSD destroys astrocytes through antibodies against AQP4, with secondary demyelination, slowing or blocking conduction. Tetrodotoxin blocks sodium channels, causing numbness and weakness, and sodium channel mutations such as SCN1A raise excitability and cause epilepsy. After an axon is cut, Wallerian degeneration breaks down the axon and myelin distal to the cut, starting within hours, and regrowth, at about 1 mm/day, begins only after it is complete; within hours, chromatolysis swells the cell body and nucleus and breaks down Nissl bodies.

Pharmacology

Local anesthetics such as lidocaine block voltage-gated sodium channels in nerves, so pain signals fail to conduct. Antiseizure drugs such as phenytoin, carbamazepine, and lamotrigine reduce sodium influx in neurons, lowering excitability.

Relationships to other topics

. The action potential ends at the terminal, where calcium entry converts the electrical signal into chemical transmission.

Synapses and neurotransmitters: release, receptors, and clearance

At chemical synapses, an action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium-activated SNARE proteins fuse vesicles to release neurotransmitter. Transmitters act on fast ionotropic or slower metabotropic receptors, producing excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potentials that sum at the axon hillock, and are cleared by reuptake or enzymes. Glutamate is the main excitatory and GABA the main inhibitory transmitter, with acetylcholine, dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin modulating specific systems. Here, we review synaptic transmission, the major transmitters, their failures, and drugs that target them.

Key points

  • Calcium entry activates SNARE proteins, which fuse vesicles and release transmitter.
  • Ionotropic receptors are fast ion channels; metabotropic receptors act through G proteins.
  • Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter, GABA the main inhibitory transmitter in the brain, and glycine inhibitory in the cord.
  • Acetylcholine is cleared by AChE; small-molecule transmitters are cleared by reuptake.
  • Benzodiazepines increase GABA-A channel opening, and cholinesterase inhibitors raise acetylcholine.

Synaptic transmission

An action potential depolarizes the terminal, opening voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium activates SNARE proteins that fuse vesicles with the membrane. Transmitter binds ionotropic receptors, ion channels that act quickly, or metabotropic receptors, G protein-coupled receptors with slower, longer effects. Excitatory postsynaptic potentials depolarize the next neuron and inhibitory potentials hyperpolarize it, as chloride enters through GABA-A receptors, and inputs sum at the axon hillock. Transmitter is cleared by reuptake into the terminal or glia, for small molecules only, or by enzymes such as AChE. Electrical synapses pass current directly through gap junctions with almost no delay.

Neurotransmitters

Glutamate, acting on AMPA and NMDA receptors, is the main excitatory transmitter and underlies plasticity and memory. GABA, acting on GABA-A chloride channels and GABA-B G protein-coupled receptors, is the main inhibitory transmitter in the brain, and glycine is inhibitory mainly in the cord. Acetylcholine acts on nicotinic ion channels and muscarinic M1–M5 receptors at muscle, autonomic synapses, and the basal forebrain. Dopamine from the SNc and ventral tegmental area acts on D1 and D2 families, norepinephrine from the locus coeruleus drives arousal, and serotonin from the raphe nuclei modulates mood, sleep, and gut motility.

When it fails

Loss of cholinergic neurons contributes to memory decline in Alzheimer disease, and death of SNc dopamine neurons leaves too little dopamine in Parkinson disease. Low serotonin, with low plasma levels of its precursor tryptophan, is linked to depression. Glutamate excitotoxicity contributes to both, seizures arise when excitation outweighs GABA inhibition, and in hepatic encephalopathy, ammonia enhances GABA-A chloride flow. Increased presynaptic striatal dopamine function is the final common pathway to psychosis in schizophrenia.

At the neuromuscular junction, myasthenia gravis targets postsynaptic nicotinic AChR or MuSK, causing fatigable weakness that worsens through the day, with ptosis and diplopia in 85% and thymoma in 10%; pyridostigmine, a cholinesterase inhibitor, is followed by immunosuppression. Lambert-Eaton syndrome targets presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing ACh release, and causes proximal weakness, autonomic dysfunction, and reduced reflexes that improve with repeated contraction, often with small cell lung cancer. In Huntington disease, striatal GABA neurons die.

Pharmacology

Benzodiazepines increase how often GABA-A chloride channels open, causing sedation and seizure control. Cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil block acetylcholine breakdown, botulinum toxin blocks acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, and SSRIs block serotonin reuptake.

Relationships to other topics

. The action potential arriving at the terminal opens the calcium channels that start release.

. Dopamine acts on D1 and D2 striatal neurons to balance the direct and indirect pathways.

Ascending tracts: dorsal columns, spinothalamic tracts, and spinocerebellar tracts

Ascending tracts carry sensation from the body to the thalamus and cortex or to the cerebellum. The dorsal column-medial lemniscus carries fine touch, vibration, and conscious proprioception, ascends on the same side, and crosses in the medulla. The spinothalamic tract carries pain, temperature, and crude touch and crosses in the cord within 1–2 segments of entry. The spinocerebellar tracts carry unconscious proprioception to the cerebellum on the same side. Where each crosses determines the side of deficits after a lesion. Here, we review their routes and failures.

Key points

  • The dorsal columns ascend on the same side and cross in the medulla as the medial lemniscus.
  • Spinothalamic fibers cross in the anterior white commissure 1–2 segments above entry.
  • A cord hemisection causes same-side dorsal column loss and opposite-side pain and temperature loss.
  • Spinocerebellar tracts end in the cerebellum on the same side, so their lesions cause same-side ataxia.

Routes

In the dorsal column-medial lemniscus, large DRG fibers ascend on the same side, gracilis from below T6 and cuneatus from T6 up, to the nucleus gracilis and cuneatus in the medulla. Second-order internal arcuate fibers cross and ascend as the medial lemniscus to the VPL thalamus, which projects to S1. The pathway carries fine touch, vibration, conscious proprioception, and two-point discrimination.

In the spinothalamic tract, thin Aδ and C fibers synapse in the dorsal horn, and second-order fibers cross in the anterior white commissure 1–2 segments above entry. The lateral tract carries sharp pain and temperature, with sacral fibers most lateral, and the anterior tract carries crude touch; both reach the VPL thalamus and S1, insula, and cingulate cortex.

The dorsal spinocerebellar tract arises in the Clarke nucleus (C8–L3) and ascends uncrossed through the inferior cerebellar peduncle, carrying leg and trunk information. The ventral tract arises from spinal border cells, crosses twice, and enters through the superior cerebellar peduncle, so both end on the same side, carrying unconscious proprioception from muscle spindles and tendon organs.

Dermatome landmarks localize sensory levels: C6 supplies the thumb, C7 the middle finger, T4 the nipple, T10 the umbilicus, L4 the anterior knee and medial malleolus, and S1 the lateral malleolus.

When it fails

A cord hemisection, Brown-Séquard syndrome, causes same-side dorsal column loss and weakness with opposite-side pain and temperature loss. Posterior cord lesions and dorsal column loss from B12 deficiency or tabes dorsalis cause vibration and position loss with a positive Romberg test, and syringomyelia interrupts crossing spinothalamic fibers, causing cape-like pain and temperature loss. Medial medullary syndrome damages the medial lemniscus, causing opposite-side vibration and position loss, and spinocerebellar loss, as in Friedreich ataxia, causes same-side ataxia.

Relationships to other topics

. Lesions often hit both: in a hemisected cord, the corticospinal tract adds same-side weakness to the sensory pattern.

Descending tracts: corticospinal, corticobulbar, and brainstem motor tracts

Descending tracts carry motor commands to cranial nerve nuclei and the spinal cord. The lateral corticospinal tract crosses at the pyramids and drives skilled distal movement, and the corticobulbar tract reaches cranial nerve nuclei mostly bilaterally. Brainstem tracts set posture and tone: the rubrospinal tract favors upper limb flexion, and the vestibulospinal and pontine reticulospinal tracts favor extension, whereas the medullary reticulospinal tract inhibits extensors. Lesions above or below the pyramidal decussation and the red nucleus produce predictable weakness and posturing. Here, we review these tracts and their failures.

Key points

  • About 90% of corticospinal fibers cross at the pyramidal decussation, so lesions above it cause opposite-side weakness.
  • Corticobulbar input is mostly bilateral, and the upper face receives input from both hemispheres.
  • The rubrospinal tract crosses in the midbrain and favors upper limb flexion.
  • Lesions above the red nucleus cause decorticate posturing; lesions below it cause decerebrate posturing.

Corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts

The lateral corticospinal tract arises from M1, the premotor cortex and SMA, and S1, and descends through the posterior limb of the internal capsule, cerebral peduncle, basis pontis, and pyramid; 90% of fibers cross at the pyramidal decussation to reach ventral horn neurons for distal limbs, driving skilled movement. The anterior corticospinal tract, about 10%, stays uncrossed until its segment and serves axial and proximal muscles on both sides. The corticobulbar tract arises from the face area of the motor cortex, passes through the genu of the internal capsule, and reaches the motor nuclei of CN V, VII, and IX–XII, mostly bilaterally; because the upper face receives input from both hemispheres, a cortical lesion spares the forehead.

Brainstem tracts

The rubrospinal tract arises from the red nucleus, which receives cortical and cerebellar input, crosses in the midbrain, and favors upper limb flexion. The lateral vestibulospinal tract, from the lateral vestibular nucleus, and the pontine reticulospinal tract descend uncrossed and excite antigravity extensors, whereas the medullary reticulospinal tract, mainly uncrossed, inhibits extensors, facilitates flexors, and contributes to autonomic and breathing control. The basal ganglia select and scale movement through two opposing striatal output pathways.   • The GPi and SNr tonically inhibit the VA and VL thalamus.
  • The direct pathway (D1) reduces that inhibition; the indirect pathway (D2, GPe, STN) increases it.

The striatum, made of the caudate and putamen, contains GABA medium spiny neurons that receive cortical and dopamine input. Pain pathways and descending modulation: from nociceptor to cortex and back

Pain begins when noxious stimuli open TRP channels on the free endings of Aδ and C fibers, which synapse in laminae I and II of the dorsal horn. The lateral spinothalamic tract crosses in the cord and relays through the VPL to S1, conveying where and how intense a stimulus is, while medial pathways reach the reticular formation, PAG, intralaminar thalamus, cingulate, and insula to carry its emotional and visceral weight. The dorsal horn is a gate: touch input and descending signals from the PAG, RVM, and locus coeruleus inhibit transmission through enkephalin interneurons and presynaptic opioid receptors. Here, we review nociceptors, the ascending and descending pathways, their failures, and the analgesics that act on them.

Key points

  • Aδ fibers carry fast, well-localized first pain; C fibers carry slow, poorly localized pain.
  • The lateral spinothalamic tract crosses in the anterior white commissure and relays in the VPL to S1.
  • The PAG drives serotonergic raphe neurons in the RVM and noradrenergic locus coeruleus neurons, which inhibit the dorsal horn.
  • Opioid receptors are Gi-coupled: they open potassium channels and close N-type calcium channels, cutting substance P release.
  • Opioids, paracetamol, and SNRIs strengthen descending inhibition; NSAIDs, gabapentinoids, and ketamine act at the periphery and dorsal horn.

Nociceptors

Nociceptors are free nerve endings whose cell bodies sit in the DRG, or the trigeminal ganglion for the face. Lightly myelinated Aδ fibers, with small receptive fields, carry the fast, sharp, well-localized first pain and release glutamate, while unmyelinated C fibers, with large receptive fields, carry slow, poorly localized pain that conveys intensity and release neuropeptides such as substance P. They are activated by histamine, NGF, substance P, CGRP, potassium, serotonin, acetylcholine, ATP, lactic acid, low pH, and arachidonic acid, and by temperature extremes, high pressure, and tissue damage. Silent nociceptors respond only after inflammation sensitizes them and cause rest pain in arthritis. The brain has no nociceptors, so awake craniotomy is painless, and because nociception is detection and pain is experience, either can occur without the other.

Somatic pain from skin is sharp or burning and well localized, while pain from joints, tendons, and bone is aching and less localized. Visceral pain is carried by C fibers and poorly localized. Referred pain arises when visceral and somatic afferents converge on the same spinal segments, as when MI pain spreads to the neck, shoulder, and back. Neuropathic pain follows nerve damage, with spontaneous firing or altered conduction; allodynia is pain from a normally harmless stimulus, and hyperalgesia is an exaggerated response to a painful one.

Ascending pathways

Aδ fibers end in lamina I and C fibers in lamina II, the substantia gelatinosa. The lateral, neospinothalamic tract crosses in the anterior white commissure and relays in the VPL to S1, conveying location and intensity. The medial paleospinothalamic and archispinothalamic pathways project bilaterally to the reticular formation, PAG, and intralaminar thalamus, then to the cingulate, insula, and hypothalamus, carrying emotional and visceral responses. From the face, the trigeminal ganglion projects to the spinal trigeminal nucleus in the medulla, which relays through the VPM to S1.

Descending modulation

In the gate control theory of Melzack and Wall (1965), the substantia gelatinosa acts as a gate. Aβ and Aδ input excites inhibitory interneurons that damp dorsal horn projection neurons, which is why rubbing a wound dulls sharp pain, and cortical centers also control the gate, which depression holds open more often.

Ascending signals and opioids activate the PAG, which drives raphe neurons of the RVM with glutamate. These serotonergic neurons, the main endogenous modulator and the main target of supraspinal opioids, activate enkephalin interneurons in the dorsal horn, whose opioid receptors on nociceptive terminals open potassium channels and block calcium entry, reducing substance P release and suppressing C fiber input. A parallel route from the PAG to the locus coeruleus releases norepinephrine onto presynaptic α2 receptors. Opioids in the PAG inhibit local GABA interneurons, releasing the descending pathway, and the PAG can also facilitate pain, contributing to chronic pain.

μ receptors bind β-endorphin and endomorphins and mediate analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, constipation, and dependence; κ receptors bind dynorphins and mediate spinal analgesia, diuresis, and dysphoria; and δ receptors bind enkephalins and mediate analgesia and decreased gastric motility. All three are Gi-coupled, opening potassium channels and closing N-type calcium channels.

When it fails

Lateral medullary syndrome damages the spinal trigeminal nucleus and spinothalamic tract, causing crossed pain and temperature loss on the same side of the face and the opposite side of the body. Because spinothalamic fibers have already crossed, cord hemisection causes pain and temperature loss on the opposite side below the lesion, and spinothalamic damage in multiple sclerosis also causes this loss.

Chronic pain, including neuropathic pain and painful attacks, affects 80–100% of patients with NMOSD. CRPS follows minor injury or surgery with severe limb pain, allodynia, and changes in sweating and blood flow, as inflammation sensitizes nociceptive neurons and sympathetic drive sustains the pain. In central sensitization, peripheral and dorsal horn changes lower the threshold and amplify pain, so chronic pain becomes a disease in itself.

Pharmacology

Opioids such as morphine and fentanyl act on μ receptors in the PAG, RVM, dorsal horn, and peripheral afferents, opening potassium channels and closing N-type calcium channels, and in the PAG they inhibit GABA interneurons to release descending inhibition. Paracetamol inhibits central COX and strengthens descending serotonergic inhibition, giving analgesia and antipyresis with little anti-inflammatory effect. SNRIs and TCAs, such as duloxetine and amitriptyline, block serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake in the spinal cord, strengthening descending inhibition and relieving neuropathic pain, and duloxetine also treats fibromyalgia.

NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, aspirin, and celecoxib decrease prostaglandin synthesis by inhibiting COX-1 and COX-2, with aspirin binding irreversibly and celecoxib selecting COX-2. Gabapentin and pregabalin bind the α2δ-1 subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing excitatory transmitter release and relieving neuropathic pain, and ketamine blocks NMDA receptors noncompetitively, giving analgesia and dissociative anesthesia.

The respiratory center: rhythm, chemical control, and failures of drive

Breathing is generated by brainstem neurons and tuned to metabolic demand. The dorsal respiratory group in the NTS is mainly inspiratory, the ventral respiratory group is mainly expiratory and contains the preBötzinger complex that generates the rhythm, and pontine centers limit or prolong inspiration. Central chemoreceptors on the ventral medulla sense CSF pH, which follows PaCO2, and supply most of the drive, while carotid and aortic bodies respond mainly to hypoxemia. Here, we review the brainstem groups, chemical and mechanical control, breathing in sleep, and the patterns and failures of drive.

Key points

  • PaCO2, sensed as CSF pH by central chemoreceptors, is the main drive to breathe.
  • Peripheral chemoreceptors respond mainly to low PaO2; the carotid bodies supply about 15% of drive.
  • The preBötzinger complex generates the rhythm and is where opioids depress breathing.
  • Cheyne-Stokes breathing follows heart failure and stroke, apneustic breathing pontine lesions, and Biot breathing medullary injury.
  • Opioids shift the CO2 response curve right; benzodiazepines and propofol flatten it.

Brainstem groups

The dorsal respiratory group lies in the NTS and is mainly inspiratory. The ventral respiratory group is mainly expiratory, and its rostral part, including the preBötzinger complex, generates the rhythm. In the pons, the pneumotaxic center limits inspiration and the apneustic center prolongs it. Inspiration follows contraction of the diaphragm and external intercostals, which lowers pleural and alveolar pressure, and expiration at rest is passive, from the elastic recoil of the lungs. The resulting rate is about 12 breaths a minute at rest.

Chemical control

Central chemoreceptors on the ventral surface of the medulla and in the retrotrapezoid nucleus sense CSF pH, which falls as CO2 crosses the blood-brain barrier, and supply most of the drive over minutes. Peripheral chemoreceptors respond within seconds, mainly to low PaO2 but also to CO2 and H+; the carotid bodies signal through CN IX and supply about 15% of drive, and the aortic bodies signal through CN X. PaCO2 is the main drive, and oxygen drives the center only in severe hypoxemia; when the receptors disagree, as in hypoxic hypocapnia, the hypoxic response wins.

Between a PaCO2 of 45 and 80 mmHg, ventilation rises 2–5 L/min for each mmHg. Hypoxemia below 60 mmHg and metabolic acidemia shift this curve left, opioids shift it right, and benzodiazepines, propofol, volatile anesthetics, age, and fitness flatten it. Hypoxia causes rapid, shallow breaths, which avoid the work of high volumes, and hypercapnia causes deep, slow breaths, which limit dead space.

Reflexes and sleep

Slowly adapting stretch receptors mediate the Hering-Breuer reflex, in which inflation inhibits inspiration; rapidly adapting irritant receptors cause cough and faster breathing; and juxtacapillary receptors respond to congestion and interstitial edema with faster breathing, all through CN X. In REM sleep, breathing becomes irregular, accessory muscles are paralyzed, upper airway dilators lose tone, and drive falls and responds less to PaO2 and PaCO2.

When it fails

Cheyne-Stokes breathing alternates crescendo-decrescendo ventilation with central apnea in cycles of 45–90 seconds and occurs in heart failure, where slow circulation delays the chemoreceptor signal, and in 20–40% of patients acutely after stroke. Biot, or ataxic, breathing has irregular clusters with unpredictable apnea from medullary injury and raised ICP; apneustic breathing has prolonged inspiratory pauses from pontine stroke or TBI; and Kussmaul breathing is deep, rapid, and continuous in metabolic acidosis such as DKA and renal failure. In the Cushing triad, raised ICP compresses the brainstem and causes irregular respirations with widened pulse pressure and bradycardia, as in intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage.

In opioid overdose, μ receptors at the preBötzinger complex blunt the response to CO2 and hypoxia, causing pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression, and decreased consciousness, and benzodiazepines, inhaled anesthetics, and alcohol also depress drive. In congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, the center ignores PaCO2, so breathing fails in sleep. Central sleep apnea arises from high loop gain in heart failure or low drive from opioids, brainstem stroke, or cervical cord injury and is treated with CPAP or BPAP, and in obesity hypoventilation, retained bicarbonate blunts the central chemoreceptors. The cardiovascular center: the baroreflex and its failures

Arterial pressure is buffered beat to beat by the baroreflex. Stretch of the carotid sinus and aortic arch opens PIEZO channels in baroreceptor endings, whose signals travel in CN IX and CN X to the NTS. The NTS excites the CVLM, whose GABA neurons inhibit the RVLM, the source of sympathetic drive, and excites cardiac vagal neurons in the nucleus ambiguus, so rising pressure slows the heart and dilates vessels while falling pressure does the reverse. Here, we review the reflex, its other inputs and outputs, and the syndromes of too much reflex, too little reflex, and a medulla cut off or compressed.

Key points

  • Baroreceptor afferents from the carotid sinus run in CN IX and from the aortic arch in CN X, ending in the NTS.
  • The NTS inhibits sympathetic output through the CVLM and RVLM and drives vagal output through the nucleus ambiguus.
  • The baroreflex resets with sustained change, so it buffers only short-term fluctuations.
  • Vasovagal syncope and carotid sinus hypersensitivity are excessive reflexes; orthostatic hypotension reflects too little.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia follows spinal cord injury at or above T6, and the Cushing reflex follows raised ICP.

The baroreflex

Wall stretch opens PIEZO1 and PIEZO2 channels in baroreceptor endings, letting sodium and calcium in; myelinated A fibers track fast, pulsatile change and unmyelinated C fibers report sustained pressure. Carotid sinus afferents travel in the carotid sinus nerve, a branch of CN IX, with cell bodies in the petrosal ganglion, and aortic afferents in CN X, with cell bodies in the nodose ganglion. They release glutamate in the NTS, which also receives chemoreceptor and lung stretch afferents. The NTS excites the CVLM, whose GABA neurons inhibit the RVLM, which drives sympathetic preganglionic neurons and maintains baseline pressure, and it excites cardiac vagal neurons in the nucleus ambiguus and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus. The ventral respiratory column sits within the RVLM.

When BP rises, baroreceptor firing increases, vagal output rises and sympathetic output falls, and HR and contractility fall while vessels dilate; when BP falls, the reverse occurs. With sustained change, the operating point shifts toward the new pressure, so the baroreflex buffers only short-term change.

Other inputs and outputs

Cardiopulmonary receptors in the atria, ventricles, and pulmonary vessels sense volume: stretch lowers renal sympathetic tone and releases ANP, causing natriuresis, while low volume raises sympathetic tone, RAAS activity, and vasopressin. The area postrema mediates angiotensin II, which raises BP without much change in HR, and the hypothalamus and insular cortex modulate long-term control. Sympathetic preganglionic neurons arise from the lateral gray column from T1 to L2 and release ACh onto nicotinic receptors, and postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine, with adrenaline from the adrenal medulla. Parasympathetic fibers leave in CN III, VII, IX, and X and from S2–S4, synapse near their targets, and release ACh onto muscarinic receptors.

When it fails

Vasovagal syncope, about 50% of syncope, follows standing, heat, pain, or stress, with sympathetic withdrawal causing vasodilation and vagal activity causing bradycardia. Carotid sinus hypersensitivity affects older adults, triggered by tight collars or head turning, and is cardioinhibitory, with asystole of 3 seconds or more, in 70%, vasodepressor, with a systolic fall of 50 mmHg or more, in 10%, and mixed in 20%; monitored carotid sinus massage confirms it, and pacing treats the cardioinhibitory type. Situational syncope follows coughing, micturition, defecation, and swallowing, and a trigger, lightheadedness, brief jerks, and rapid recovery favor syncope over a seizure.

Orthostatic hypotension is a sustained fall of 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing, as 300–800 mL pools in the legs and splanchnic veins. It occurs in Parkinson disease, early in multiple system atrophy and dementia with Lewy bodies, and in pure autonomic failure, diabetes, B12 deficiency, and with α- and β-blockers. In POTS, HR rises 30 a minute or more within 10 minutes of standing, or 40 at ages 12–19, without orthostatic hypotension, and afferent baroreflex failure after neck radiation or surgery causes swings between hypertension and hypotension.

In autonomic dysreflexia, after SCI at or above T6, a stimulus below the lesion, usually a full bladder (85%), drives splanchnic vasoconstriction, raising systolic pressure by 20 mmHg or more with headache, while vagal bradycardia and flushing occur only above the lesion. In the Cushing reflex, raised ICP lowers CPP, the difference between MAP and ICP, causing a sympathetic surge with widened pulse pressure, then bradycardia and irregular respirations, a late sign of herniation in intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage.
