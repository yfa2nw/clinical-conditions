Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

Pathophysiology

IBS is a type of DGBI that stems from visceral hypersensitivity, altered microbiome, and hypermotility.

Note: There is a lack of structural damage.

Etiology

  • 1Visceral hypersensitivityPsychiatric disorders, fibromyalgia, depression, and anxiety → increased CNS amplification of stretch receptor stimulation from osmotic effects of carbohydrates → abdominal pain
  • 2Altered microbiomeGut microbiome metabolizes carbohydrates into gas → stimulates stretch receptors → reflex contraction → abdominal pain
  • 3HypermotilityIncreased gut motility → diarrhea

Symptoms and complications

Predominant bowel pattern:

  • Abdominal pain
  • Diarrhea
  • Constipation
  • Mixed

Diagnosis

Rome V criteria

  1. Abdominal pain or discomfortRecurrent but not continuous, for at least 3 days per month
  2. And 2 of the followingRelated to defecation, changes in stool frequency, and changes in stool appearance

If there is

  • PerforationAbdominal x-ray and CT AP
  • DiarrheaFecal calprotectin, ESR/CRP, O&P, C. diff, and colonoscopy with biopsy

Treatment

  • Relieve abdominal painTCAs and peppermint oil
  • Relieve constipationLaxatives
  • Relieve diarrheaLoperamide

Hepatocellular Injury: Vascular Causes

Causes of hepatocellular injury VITAMIN + other

  1. VVascular

Pathophysiology

Decreased blood flow into (arterial) or out of (venous) the liver leads to hypoxia of zone 3 (centrilobular) hepatocytes, causing hepatocellular injury.

Etiology

  • Arterial
  • 1Ischemic hepatitis ("shock liver")Shock (cardiogenic, hypovolemic, septic) or cardiac arrest → decreased hepatic perfusion → zone 3 hypoxia → centrilobular necrosis → increased AST/ALT, often over 1000 U/L
  • Venous
  • 2Hepatic vein congestion (congestive hepatopathy)RHF, also tricuspid regurgitation and constrictive pericarditis → increased CVP and hepatic venous pressure → sinusoidal congestion, zone 3 atrophy, and necrosis → nutmeg liver and cardiac cirrhosis if chronic
  • 3Hepatic vein clot (Budd-Chiari syndrome)Myeloproliferative neoplasms (polycythemia vera, JAK2) Most common, pregnancy, OCPs, PNH, and malignancy (e.g. HCC) → thrombosis of hepatic veins or IVC → blocked hepatic outflow and increased sinusoidal pressure → hepatomegaly, ascites, and RUQ pain

Symptoms and complications

Each cause has a distinct pattern:

Onset Acute, 1–3 days after hypotension Usually chronic Acute or chronic
Key features Often no symptoms; nausea, RUQ pain RUQ discomfort, hepatomegaly, peripheral edema Triad of RUQ pain, hepatomegaly, and ascites

Symptoms

  • RUQ pain
  • Hepatomegaly
  • Ascites
  • Jaundice
  • Nausea

Complications

  • Acute liver failure coagulopathy (increased INR), hepatic encephalopathy, hypoglycemia
  • Cardiac cirrhosis long-standing congestion
  • Portal hypertension ascites, varices, splenomegaly

Diagnosis

  • +Any vascular causeLFTs and INR to assess liver function
  • 1Ischemic hepatitisHistory of hypotension; serial LFTs: rapid rise, then rapid fall; LDH: markedly increased
  • 2Congestive hepatopathyEchocardiogram and BNP
  • 3Budd-Chiari syndromeDoppler ultrasound of the hepatic veins First line; CT or MR venography to confirm; hypercoagulability workup (JAK2 V617F)

Check for JVD: present in heart failure, absent in Budd-Chiari syndrome.

Treatment

  • 1Ischemic hepatitisRestore perfusion and treat the cause of shock (fluids, vasopressors, cardiac support)
  • 2Congestive hepatopathyTreat heart failure (e.g. diuretics)
  • 3Budd-Chiari syndromeAnticoagulation and treat the underlying cause, then step up as below

"The vowels hit your bowels": hepatitis A and E are fecal-oral, acute only, and naked.

HDV requires HBV

HDV needs HBsAg from HBV as its envelope, so it only infects people who also have HBV.

Coinfection

  • HBV and HDV acquired at the same time
  • Usually acute and self-limited
  • Both viruses usually cleared

Superinfection

  • HDV acquired by someone with chronic HBV
  • More severe; risk of fulminant hepatitis
  • Usually becomes chronic, with faster progression to cirrhosis

Viral life cycles

+ssRNA viruses HAV, HCV, HEV

  • Attachment and entry into the hepatocyte
  • Uncoating releases +ssRNA into the cytoplasm
  • +ssRNA acts directly as mRNA
  • Translated into a polyprotein, which viral proteases cleave
  • Viral RdRp copies a −RNA template
  • The template makes new +ssRNA genomes
  • Assembly and release

Replicates in the cytoplasm; HCV drugs target NS3/4A, NS5A, and NS5B.

pdsDNA virus HBV

  • Attachment and entry via the NTCP receptor
  • Nucleocapsid carries pdsDNA into the nucleus
  • pdsDNA is repaired into cccDNA
  • Host RNA polymerase II makes mRNAs and pregenomic RNA
  • Viral reverse transcriptase converts pregenomic RNA into new pdsDNA
  • Assembly with an HBsAg envelope and release

cccDNA persists, so HBV is hard to cure; tenofovir and entecavir block reverse transcriptase.

HDV (circular −ssRNA) replicates in the nucleus using host RNA polymerase.

Biliary Tract and Periampullary Malignancies

Biliary tree diseases Steps 1–4 follow a stone down the tree

  6. 6Malignancies

Pathophysiology

Tumors in or around the bile ducts gradually obstruct bile flow, causing painless obstructive jaundice.

Etiology

  • 1Pancreatic head adenocarcinomaMost common periampullary cancer ; smoking, chronic pancreatitis, and diabetes
  • 2Ampullary adenocarcinomaBlocks bile early, so found earlier and has a better prognosis; may cause intermittent jaundice; FAP
  • 3Duodenal adenocarcinomaRare; may also cause GI bleeding or obstruction; FAP, Lynch syndrome, and celiac disease
  • 4Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumorsFrom islet cells; may secrete hormones (insulinoma, gastrinoma, vipoma, glucagonoma); MEN1
  • 5Bile duct adenocarcinoma (cholangiocarcinoma)Intrahepatic, perihilar, or distal; perihilar (Klatskin) most common; , liver flukes (Clonorchis), and choledochal cysts
  • 6Gallbladder carcinomaOften found late, or by chance after cholecystectomy; older women; gallstones (especially large), porcelain gallbladder, polyps over 1 cm, and PSC

Symptoms and complications

Symptoms

  • Painless jaundice
  • Pruritus
  • Dark urine, pale stools
  • Weight loss
  • Palpable, nontender gallbladder

Courvoisier law

  • Malignant obstructionTumor below the cystic duct (pancreatic head, ampulla, distal CBD) → slow, steady blockage; gallbladder wall is healthy and stretchy → enlarged, palpable, nontender gallbladder with painless jaundice
  • Gallstone obstructionStone in the CBD → repeated inflammation has already scarred the gallbladder → shrunken gallbladder that cannot distend, usually with pain

Courvoisier law: painless jaundice with a palpable gallbladder is unlikely to be caused by gallstones, so suspect malignancy.

Complications

  • Ascending cholangitis
  • Liver metastases
  • Gastric outlet obstruction

Diagnosis

  • CholestasisLFTs
  • Tumor markersCA 19-9 and CEA: not for screening
  • ImagingUltrasound, then CT or MRCP: double duct sign
  • Tissue diagnosisERCP brushings or endoscopic ultrasound biopsy

Treatment

  • 1–3Periampullary tumorsPancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure) if resectable
  • 5CholangiocarcinomaResection (bile duct, often with part of the liver); transplant in selected cases
  • 6Gallbladder carcinomaCholecystectomy with liver resection and lymph node removal
  • +Unresectable diseaseBiliary stent to relieve jaundice; chemotherapy

Acute Pancreatitis

Pathophysiology

Premature activation of trypsin inside the pancreas leads to autodigestion, causing local and systemic inflammation.

Etiology

Symptoms and complications

Symptoms

  • Severe epigastric pain radiating to the back
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Fever, tachycardia
  • Epigastric tenderness, guarding

Acute process

  1. Trypsin activated inside the pancreas
  2. Pancreatic autodigestionEpigastric pain; increased IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α
  3. SIRS and local pancreatic complications

Systemic complications (SIRS)

  • Leaky capillariesIncreased capillary permeability → third spacing → hypovolemia, fluid in the lungs, and fluid in the abdomen → shock, AKI, ARDS, exudative ascites, and abdominal compartment syndrome
  • ClottingIncreased tissue factor → increased clotting throughout small vessels → platelets and clotting factors used up, and red cells sheared (schistocytes) → DIC
  • MetabolicLipase digests surrounding fat → fatty acids bind calcium (fat saponification) → hypocalcemia; hyperglycemia from islet damage

Local pancreatic complications

  • Interstitial edematous pancreatitisPancreatic edema → fluid collects around the pancreas → pseudocyst (after 4 weeks; no epithelium) → infection, rupture, and compression: small bowel or gastric outlet obstruction
  • Necrotizing pancreatitisTissue necrosis → acute necrotic collection and enzymes erode blood vessels → walled-off necrosis (after 4 weeks) and retroperitoneal bleeding (Cullen and Grey Turner signs)

Repeated attacks can lead to chronic pancreatitis.

Diagnosis

Any 2 of 3 criteria

  • PainAcute, severe epigastric pain, often radiating to the back
  • EnzymesLipase (or amylase) at least 3 times the upper limit of normal; lipase is more specific
  • ImagingTypical findings on contrast CT abdomen (or MRI, ultrasound)

Find the cause and judge severity

  • CauseRUQ ultrasound for gallstones in every patient; LFTs (ALT over 150 suggests gallstones); triglycerides, calcium, alcohol history, and medications
  • ComplicationsContrast CT if the diagnosis is unclear, or after 48–72 hours if the patient is not improving; CT too early can miss necrosis
  • SeverityFBC, creatinine, BUN, oxygen saturation, and scores such as BISAP

Severity (revised Atlanta classification)

  • MildNo organ failure and no local complications
  • Moderately severeOrgan failure lasting under 48 hours, or local complications
  • SevereOrgan failure lasting over 48 hours

Treatment

  • Supportive careIV fluids (goal-directed, e.g. Hartmann solution), pain relief, and early oral feeding; enteral feeding if severe
  • Treat the causeGallstones: cholecystectomy during the same admission
    Alcohol: stop drinking
    Hypertriglyceridemia: insulin infusion
    Drugs: stop the offending drug
  • Infected necrosisABX (e.g. carbapenem); delayed drainage or necrosectomy, ideally after 4 weeks; no prophylactic ABX
  • PseudocystObserve; drain only if symptomatic or complicated
