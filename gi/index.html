Esophagitis: routes of mucosal injury and relationships to other foregut disease

Esophagitis is inflammation of the esophagus that follows mucosal damage from diverse causes. Injury may be ingested, infectious, or refluxed, and the route largely determines management. Presentation is dominated by dysphagia, odynophagia, heartburn, and chest pain, and the principal threats are bleeding, perforation, and progression through Barrett esophagus to adenocarcinoma. Here, we review the etiology, complications, and management of esophagitis, and consider how it relates to GERD, esophageal motility disorders, gastritis, and PUD.

Key points

  • Esophagitis is a common endpoint of mucosal injury, and identifying the cause determines treatment.
  • Causes are best grouped by route: ingested, infectious, or refluxed.
  • Investigation is directed by complication, with EGD for bleeding and contrast esophagram for perforation.
  • PPIs are given in all cases, while caustic ingestion requires a distinct approach.
  • Odynophagia favors esophagitis over GERD and motility disorders.

Etiology and mechanisms

Esophagitis results when mucosal damage provokes inflammation of the esophagus, and its causes are best grouped by how injury reaches the mucosa. Ingested agents include pills (NSAIDs, KCl, doxycycline, and bisphosphonates), caustics (strong acid or strong base), and food allergens, which drive eosinophilic disease in patients with asthma, allergic rhinitis, or atopic dermatitis. Infection occurs in immunocompromised patients and is most commonly due to Candida, with CMV and HSV also implicated. These are distinguished endoscopically and histologically: Candida by white plaques, pseudohyphae, and budding yeast; CMV by large, shallow, linear ulcers with "owl's eye" inclusions; and HSV by small, punched-out ulcers with multinucleated giant cells. Reflux-associated disease arises from GERD, including conditions nonresponsive to PPIs.

Clinical presentation and complications

Patients present with dysphagia, odynophagia, heartburn, and chest pain. Complications include bleeding (hematemesis, melena), perforation (pneumomediastinum, mediastinitis), strictures and ulcers, obstruction, and aspiration pneumonia. Barrett esophagus may also develop, in which intestinal metaplasia progresses through dysplasia to esophageal adenocarcinoma.

Diagnosis

Investigation is directed by complication. Bleeding is evaluated by EGD with biopsy, and perforation by contrast esophagram and CXR, followed by surgery.

Management

PPIs are given in all cases to prevent further mucosal damage, followed by treatment of the cause: offending drugs are stopped, food allergens are avoided in eosinophilic disease, antimicrobials are prescribed for infection, and GERD is treated. Caustic ingestion requires a distinct approach, comprising securing the airway, NPO, early EGD to grade the injury, avoidance of induced vomiting, and surgery for perforation.

Relationships to other conditions

Reflux is one cause of esophagitis, and the two share dysphagia, heartburn, chest pain, bleeding, perforation, aspiration, and Barrett esophagus. Odynophagia favors esophagitis and dyspepsia favors GERD, whereas rings, furrows, atopy, and food impaction favor eosinophilic esophagitis.

These share dysphagia and aspiration but are neuromuscular rather than mucosal. Dysphagia to solids and liquids from onset favors a motility disorder, for which manometry is the gold standard. Scleroderma links the two, as a weak LES promotes reflux.

Esophageal motility disorders: neuromuscular failure of the LES and esophageal body

Esophageal motility disorders are neuromuscular dysfunctions of the esophagus that cause dysphagia. Achalasia, diffuse esophageal spasm, and esophageal scleroderma are separated by the state of the LES and the pattern of mid-distal motility. Complications include aspiration pneumonia and cancer. Here, we review their mechanisms, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider how they relate to esophagitis and GERD.

Key points

  • LES tone separates the disorders: increased in achalasia and decreased in esophageal scleroderma.
  • Manometry is the gold standard, alongside barium swallow and EGD with biopsy.
  • Achalasia is treated by disrupting the LES, whereas scleroderma is treated with PPIs.

Etiology and mechanisms

Achalasia is idiopathic or due to Trypanosoma cruzi. Decreased myenteric plexus activity lowers NO and VIP neurotransmitter activity, which increases LES tone and decreases mid-distal esophageal motility. In diffuse esophageal spasm, a trigger produces disorganized mid-distal esophageal motility. In esophageal scleroderma, atrophy and fibrosis decrease LES tone and mid-distal esophageal motility.

Clinical presentation and complications

The presenting symptom is dysphagia. Complications are aspiration pneumonia and cancer.

Diagnosis

Diagnostic tools are barium swallow, EGD with biopsy, and manometry, which is the gold standard.

Management

Achalasia is treated with pneumatic dilation or myotomy (Heller or POEM), with CCB, nitrates, or botulinum injection if patients are unfit for these. Diffuse esophageal spasm is treated with CCB or nitrates, with pneumatic dilation or botulinum injection as other options. Esophageal scleroderma is treated with PPIs.

Relationships to other conditions

. Both cause dysphagia and aspiration, but motility disorders are neuromuscular rather than mucosal. Dysphagia to solids and liquids from onset favors a motility disorder, and odynophagia favors esophagitis.

. Achalasia and scleroderma are opposites at the LES, and scleroderma behaves like severe GERD because low LES tone permits reflux. Treatments are therefore opposed: Heller myotomy lowers LES pressure and Nissen fundoplication tightens it, so each would worsen the other condition.

Gastroesophageal reflux disease: failure of the antireflux barrier and its consequences

GERD occurs when HCl enters the esophagus and causes mucosal damage. Reflux is favored by decreased LES tone, hiatal hernia, increased HCl production, and increased intragastric pressure. Symptoms are dysphagia, dyspepsia, heartburn, and chest pain, and the main long-term concern is Barrett esophagus. Here, we review its causes, stepwise diagnosis, and treatment, and consider how it relates to esophagitis, motility disorders, and PUD.

Key points

  • Four mechanisms drive reflux: decreased LES tone, hiatal hernia, increased HCl production, and increased intragastric pressure.
  • Chest pain requires an ECG before a PPI trial.
  • Failure of a PPI trial leads to EGD with biopsy.
  • Barrett esophagus requires EGD every 3–5 years.

Etiology and mechanisms

LES tone is decreased by smoking, alcohol, and caffeine. Hiatal hernias, particularly sliding hernias, also promote reflux. HCl production is increased by smoking, alcohol, NSAIDs, and Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. Intragastric pressure is increased by obesity, gastroparesis, and pregnancy.

Clinical presentation and complications

Patients present with dysphagia, dyspepsia, heartburn, and chest pain. Complications are bleeding (hematemesis, melena), perforation (pneumomediastinum, mediastinitis), obstruction, aspiration pneumonia, and Barrett esophagus, in which intestinal metaplasia progresses through dysplasia to esophageal adenocarcinoma.

Diagnosis

Chest pain is first evaluated with an ECG. A PPI trial is then initiated, and EGD with biopsy follows if there is no improvement. Bleeding is evaluated by EGD with biopsy, and perforation by contrast esophagram and CXR, followed by surgery.

Management

PPIs or H2R antagonists are always prescribed. Patients on NSAIDs stop the offending drug, refractory disease is treated with Nissen fundoplication, and cancer surveillance is by EGD q3–5y.

Relationships to other conditions

. GERD is one cause of esophagitis, and the two share most symptoms and complications. Odynophagia favors esophagitis, and dyspepsia favors GERD.

. Scleroderma produces reflux through low LES tone, whereas achalasia has high LES tone. Manometry confirms normal peristalsis before Nissen fundoplication.

. Zollinger-Ellison syndrome and NSAIDs link the two through increased HCl production, and PPIs treat both.

Cardiac disease. Chest pain is presumed cardiac until proven otherwise, particularly with cardiovascular risk factors.

Gastritis: superficial mucosal injury and the boundary with peptic ulcer disease

Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach's mucosal lining following damage to the stomach, and is associated with the antrum, shallow erosions, and shallow mucosal layers. Causes include NSAIDs and alcohol, H. pylori, Curling and Cushing ulcers, and autoimmune gastritis. Here, we review these mechanisms and consider how gastritis relates to PUD.

Key points

  • NSAIDs and alcohol act through COX inhibition, and H. pylori through increased gastrin.
  • Curling ulcers follow severe burns, and Cushing ulcers follow increased ICP.
  • Autoimmune gastritis decreases HCl and IF, producing fundal atrophy rather than inflammation or erosions.

Etiology and mechanisms

NSAIDs and alcohol cause COX inhibition, which decreases PG, increasing HCl production and decreasing mucus production. H. pylori infection increases gastrin production and therefore HCl production. In Curling ulcers, severe burns cause hypovolemia and mucosal ischemia. In Cushing ulcers, increased ICP increases CN X activity and submucosal activity, which increases HCl production. In autoimmune gastritis, autoantibodies attack parietal cells, decreasing HCl and IF production and causing fundal atrophy rather than inflammation or erosions.

A note on terminology

Curling and Cushing ulcers are not true ulcers because they do not erode past the muscularis mucosae.

Relationships to other conditions

. Depth is the boundary: gastritis stays superficial, whereas PUD extends beyond the muscularis mucosae. NSAIDs, alcohol, and H. pylori cause both.

Autoimmune vs H. pylori gastritis. Autoimmune gastritis targets the body and fundus, where parietal cells live, and causes low acid with high gastrin. H. pylori gastritis typically starts in the antrum. Autoimmune disease also leads to B12 deficiency and raises the risk of gastric carcinoid tumors.

. Autoimmune gastritis can accompany conditions such as Hashimoto thyroiditis and vitiligo.

Peptic ulcer disease: H. pylori, NSAIDs, and the balance of acid and mucosal defense

PUD is ulceration of the mucosal lining that extends beyond the muscularis mucosae. H. pylori is the most common cause, followed by NSAIDs and alcohol, and gastrinomas are a less common cause. Gastric and duodenal ulcers have opposite pain patterns. Bleeding, perforation, and gastric outlet obstruction are the major complications. Here, we review its etiology, diagnosis, and management, and consider its relationship to gastritis, GERD, and cancer.

Key points

  • H. pylori is the most common cause, and first-line eradication is triple therapy.
  • Gastric ulcer pain worsens with eating; duodenal ulcer pain improves with eating and worsens with fasting.
  • Stop PPIs 2 weeks before H. pylori testing, and ABX and bismuth 4 weeks before.
  • If H. pylori is absent, check gastrin and then a secretin stimulation test.

Etiology and mechanisms

H. pylori is gram-negative and urease-positive, has multiple flagella, and lives in the gastric mucosa. Its virulence factors are ureases, cytotoxins (VacA, CagA), mucinases, phospholipases, and PAF. Chronic infection leads to gastritis, then atrophy and intestinal metaplasia, then dysplasia and gastric cancer, and to gastric MALT lymphoma. NSAIDs and alcohol, the second most common cause, act through COX inhibition, decreasing PG, increasing HCl production, and decreasing mucus production. Gastrinomas, as in Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, increase gastrin production and therefore HCl production.

Clinical presentation and complications

Pain is epigastric, dull, and burning in both types; gastric ulcer pain is worse with eating and better with fasting, and duodenal ulcer pain is the reverse. Other symptoms are nausea, vomiting, heartburn, bloating, fullness, belching, and fatigue. Complications are bleeding (hematemesis, melena), perforation (pneumoperitoneum, peritonitis), gastric outlet obstruction (vomiting, pain, malnutrition, succussion splash), intestinal metaplasia, gastric cancer, and gastric MALT lymphoma.

Diagnosis

Perforation is evaluated with an upright CXR, and bleeding with EGD with biopsy. H. pylori is detected by urea breath test, stool antigen test, rapid urease test, or histology. PPIs are stopped 2 weeks before testing, and ABX and bismuth 4 weeks before testing. If no H. pylori is detected, gastrin levels are checked, followed by a secretin stimulation test for Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.

Management

Perforation is treated with IV fluids, IV PPI, IV ABX, and surgery, and bleeding with IV fluids, IV PPI, EGD, and surgery. Acid is suppressed with antacids, H2R antagonists, and PPIs, and the mucosa is protected with misoprostol, sucralfate, and bismuth. First-line H. pylori eradication is triple therapy, bd for 7–14 days: PPI, amoxicillin (metronidazole if allergic), and clarithromycin. Alternative regimens are PPI, amoxicillin, and levofloxacin; PPI, metronidazole, tetracycline, and bismuth; and PPI, amoxicillin, and rifabutin.

Malabsorption: separating mucosal injury from failed digestion

Malabsorption syndromes divide into true malabsorption, with mucosal injury and normal enzyme function, and maldigestion, with altered enzyme function and no mucosal injury. Celiac disease, Whipple disease, and tropical sprue injure the mucosa, whereas pancreatic insufficiency, bile salt deficiency, and lactose intolerance impair digestion. Here, we review their mechanisms, nutrient-specific consequences, and treatment, and consider how they relate to diarrhea and IBD.

Key points

  • The D-xylose test differentiates malabsorption from maldigestion.
  • Celiac disease is confirmed by EGD with biopsy after serology.
  • Consequences follow the lost nutrient: carbohydrate, protein, fat, or micronutrient.
  • Micronutrient deficiency is treated by replacement and IV fluids.

Etiology and mechanisms

Celiac disease is associated with HLA DQ2/8 and triggered by gliadin from gluten in wheat, rye, and barley. Gliadin binds antigen-presenting cells, which activate T helper cells and then B cells, producing IgA antibodies: tTG, DGP, and EMA. Whipple disease is Tropheryma whipplei infection with mucosal damage. Tropical sprue is E. coli and Klebsiella overgrowth in tropical areas with mucosal injury. Pancreatic insufficiency follows acute or chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, and pancreatic cancer or surgery. Bile salt deficiency, a partial maldigestion, follows cirrhosis, hepatocellular injury, biliary tree obstruction, and impaired enterohepatic circulation. Lactose intolerance, also a partial maldigestion, follows genetic mutations and gastroenteritis.

Clinical presentation and complications

Carbohydrate malabsorption causes osmotic diarrhea, bowel distention, and increased gas production from bacterial digestion, with abdominal pain and flatulence. Protein malabsorption causes muscle atrophy and weight loss. Fat malabsorption causes steatorrhea and fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies: vitamin A (night blindness), vitamin D (hypocalcemia, osteomalacia, osteoporosis), vitamin E (neuropathy, hemolytic anemia), and vitamin K (coagulopathy). Micronutrient malabsorption causes iron, B12, and folate deficiency, leading to anemia.

Diagnosis

The D-xylose test first differentiates malabsorption from maldigestion. Celiac disease is evaluated by serology (tTG, DGP, EMA, HLA), infectious causes by history taking, and confirmation is by EGD with biopsy. Pancreatic function is assessed by fecal elastase, lactose intolerance by hydrogen breath test, and bile salt deficiency by LFTs or biopsy.

Management

Micronutrient deficiency is treated by replacing the micronutrients and giving IV fluids. Celiac disease is treated with a gluten-free diet; Whipple disease and tropical sprue with ABX; pancreatic insufficiency with enzyme replacement; bile salt deficiency by treating the liver disease or cholestasis; and lactose intolerance with lactase replacement or a lactose-free diet.

Relationships to other conditions

. Every cause here produces chronic osmotic diarrhea, which has a high osmotic gap and stops with fasting, unlike secretory diarrhea.

. Ileal Crohn disease reproduces malabsorption: fat, B12, and bile salts are lost, with oxalate stones and gallstones as consequences.

. PBC and PSC cause fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies through the same bile salt mechanism.

. Repeated attacks link pancreatitis to pancreatic insufficiency, which fecal elastase identifies.

Diverticular diseases: from pressure-driven outpouching to inflammation

Increased sigmoid colon intraluminal pressure pushes the mucosa and submucosa out through the muscularis externa, forming diverticula. Diverticulitis follows when a fecalith obstructs a diverticulum. LLQ pain is the main symptom, and complications include LGIB, abscess, enterovesical fistula, and perforation. Here, we review their mechanisms and management, and consider how they relate to ischemic colitis, IBD, and IBS.

Key points

  • Low-fiber diet, constipation, obesity, and older age raise sigmoid pressure.
  • Suspected diverticulitis is evaluated with CT AP with contrast.
  • Avoid colonoscopy during acute diverticulitis; perform it 6–8 weeks after recovery.
  • Treatment follows the complication, and severity is monitored for potential colectomy.

Etiology and mechanisms

Diverticulosis follows a low-fiber diet, constipation, obesity, and older age, which increase sigmoid colon intraluminal pressure and form diverticula. In diverticulitis, a fecalith obstructs a diverticulum, causing increased back pressure and bacterial colonization, with LLQ pain and inflammation.

Clinical presentation and complications

The main symptom is LLQ pain. Complications are LGIB (hematochezia), abscess (persistent pain and fever refractory to treatment), enterovesical fistula (pneumaturia, fecaluria, UTI), and perforation (pneumoperitoneum, peritonitis).

Diagnosis

Perforation is evaluated by abdominal x-ray and CT AP, bleeding by colonoscopy, and suspected diverticulitis by CT AP with contrast. Colonoscopy is avoided during acute diverticulitis and performed 6–8 weeks after recovery.

Management

Perforation is treated by laparotomy, bleeding by colonoscopy, abscess by ABX, percutaneous drainage, and surgery, fistula by surgery, and infection by ABX. Otherwise, fiber intake is increased. Severity is monitored for potential colectomy.

Relationships to other conditions

. Both cause LLQ pain and hematochezia, but diverticular bleeding is typically painless, whereas ischemic colitis causes pain with bleeding after hypoperfusion.

. Enterovesical fistula with pneumaturia, fecaluria, and UTI occurs in both.

. The mechanisms mirror each other: a fecalith obstructs a blind pouch, and pressure and infection follow. Diverticulitis causes LLQ pain, and appendicitis RLQ pain.

. Both can cause pain and altered bowel habit, but IBS has no structural damage.

Budd-Chiari syndrome is thrombosis of the hepatic veins or IVC, most commonly from myeloproliferative neoplasms (polycythemia vera, JAK2), and also from pregnancy, OCPs, PNH, and malignancy (e.g. HCC). Infectious liver injury: the hepatitis viruses and their clinical divergence

Viral infection of hepatocytes triggers a cytotoxic T-cell response, which destroys infected cells and causes hepatic inflammation. Hepatitis A and E are fecal-oral and acute, whereas hepatitis B, C, and D are bloodborne and can become chronic, leading to cirrhosis and HCC. CMV, HSV, and EBV also cause hepatitis. Here, we review their virology, phases, serology, and treatment, and consider how they relate to other liver injuries.

Key points

  • "The vowels hit your bowels": hepatitis A and E are fecal-oral, acute only, and naked.
  • HDV needs HBsAg from HBV as its envelope.
  • In the window period, IgM anti-HBc is the only positive marker, and anti-HBc never comes from vaccination.
  • Anti-HCV stays positive for life, so HCV RNA confirms current infection.
  • Screen for HCC every 6 months in chronic HBV, or HCV with cirrhosis.

Virology

HAV is a naked +ssRNA picornavirus spread by the fecal-oral route, with 2–6 weeks of incubation; it is acute only, has no carrier state, and has an inactivated vaccine. HBV is an enveloped, partially double-stranded, circular DNA (pdsDNA) hepadnavirus spread by sex, blood, and perinatally, with 1–6 months of incubation; it becomes chronic in ~90% of infants and under 5% of adults, integrates into host DNA so can cause HCC without cirrhosis, and has a recombinant HBsAg vaccine. HCV is an enveloped +ssRNA flavivirus spread by blood, mainly IV drug use, with 2 weeks–6 months of incubation; it becomes chronic in 75–85%, and high antigenic variation means no vaccine and frequent chronicity. HDV is a circular −ssRNA deltavirus with an HBsAg envelope borrowed from HBV, spread by sex, blood, and perinatally, with incubation similar to HBV; the HBV vaccine protects. HEV is a naked +ssRNA hepevirus spread by the fecal-oral route through contaminated water and undercooked pork, with 2–9 weeks of incubation; it is acute, chronic only if immunocompromised, rarely chronic overall, has no widely available vaccine, and causes fulminant hepatitis in pregnancy with high mortality.

Coinfection and superinfection

HDV only infects people who also have HBV. In coinfection, HBV and HDV are acquired at the same time; disease is usually acute and self-limited, and both viruses are usually cleared. In superinfection, HDV is acquired by someone with chronic HBV; disease is more severe, with a risk of fulminant hepatitis, and usually becomes chronic with faster progression to cirrhosis.

Viral life cycles

+ssRNA viruses (HAV, HCV, HEV) attach, enter the hepatocyte, and uncoat; +ssRNA acts directly as mRNA, is translated into a polyprotein that viral proteases cleave, and viral RdRp copies a −RNA template that makes new genomes, followed by assembly and release. They replicate in the cytoplasm, and HCV drugs target NS3/4A, NS5A, and NS5B. HBV attaches and enters via the NTCP receptor, its nucleocapsid carries pdsDNA into the nucleus, pdsDNA is repaired into cccDNA, host RNA polymerase II makes mRNAs and pregenomic RNA, viral reverse transcriptase converts pregenomic RNA into new pdsDNA, and assembly with an HBsAg envelope is followed by release. cccDNA persists, so HBV is hard to cure, and tenofovir and entecavir block reverse transcriptase. HDV replicates in the nucleus using host RNA polymerase.

Other viruses

CMV affects immunocompromised patients (transplant, AIDS), with a mono-like illness, a negative heterophile test, and usually mild hepatitis. HSV affects pregnancy (third trimester), neonates, and immunocompromised patients; it is rare, and can be fulminant and anicteric. EBV causes infectious mononucleosis in adolescents and young adults, with common mild transaminase rise and hepatosplenomegaly.

Clinical presentation and complications

Acute viral hepatitis passes through an asymptomatic incubation phase; a prodromal phase with flu-like illness, RUQ pain, arthralgia, and increased ALT more than AST; an icteric phase with jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, and increased bilirubin; and a convalescent phase resolving over weeks to months. Many infections are anicteric, especially HAV in young children and acute HCV. Outcomes are recovery (HAV, HEV, most adult HBV), fulminant hepatitis (HBV, HDV, HEV in pregnancy), and chronic infection (HBV, HCV, HDV), in which ongoing inflammation causes fibrosis, cirrhosis, HCC, and liver failure; in chronic HBV, DNA integrates into the host genome and causes HCC even without cirrhosis. Extrahepatic manifestations, mostly from immune complexes (HBV) and cryoglobulins (HCV), are arthritis and arthralgia (serum sickness-like in HBV); polyarteritis nodosa (HBV) and mixed cryoglobulinemia vasculitis (HCV); rare myocarditis and pericarditis; glomerulonephritis, including membranous nephropathy (HBV) and MPGN (HCV and HBV); thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, and neutropenia; B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (HCV); and porphyria cutanea tarda and lichen planus (HCV).

Diagnosis

IgM means current or recent infection, and IgG means past infection or immunity. Acute HAV shows IgM anti-HAV, and IgG anti-HAV is also positive after vaccination. Acute HDV shows IgM anti-HDV or HDV RNA with positive HBsAg, and past infection shows IgG anti-HDV; IgM anti-HBc is positive in coinfection and negative in superinfection. Acute HEV shows IgM anti-HEV, or HEV RNA if immunocompromised, and past infection shows IgG anti-HEV; it should be considered in pregnant patients with acute hepatitis. For HBV, acute infection shows positive HBsAg, HBeAg, and HBV DNA, with IgM anti-HBc. The window period shows negative HBsAg, HBeAg, HBV DNA, and anti-HBs, anti-HBe that may be positive, and IgM anti-HBc. Recovery shows positive anti-HBs and anti-HBe, with IgG anti-HBc. Immunized patients have only anti-HBs. Chronic, replicative infection (high infectivity) shows positive HBsAg, HBeAg, and HBV DNA with IgG anti-HBc, and chronic, non-replicative infection (low infectivity) shows positive HBsAg and anti-HBe, low or undetectable HBV DNA, and IgG anti-HBc. HEV is treated supportively, with ribavirin if chronic. HCC screening with ultrasound every 6 months applies in chronic HBV, or HCV with cirrhosis.

Key points

  • A cholestatic pattern is first evaluated with RUQ ultrasound, then serology.
  • PSC needs surveillance for cholangiocarcinoma, gallbladder, and colorectal cancer.

Etiology and mechanisms

In PBC, AMA is positive in over 90%; T cells destroy small intrahepatic bile ducts, with granulomas around the ducts ("florid duct lesion"), causing loss of bile ducts (ductopenia) and cholestasis. It affects middle-aged women and is associated with other autoimmune diseases: scleroderma (CREST), Hashimoto thyroiditis, rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren syndrome. Cancer risk is HCC once cirrhotic. Cholesterol stones are most common (~80%), arise from cholesterol supersaturation, nucleation, and gallbladder hypomotility, form in the gallbladder, and are usually radiolucent. Black pigment stones are less common, arise from chronic hemolysis with increased unconjugated bilirubin (e.g. sickle cell, spherocytosis), form in the gallbladder, and are often radiopaque (calcium bilirubinate). Brown pigment stones are less common, though more common in East Asia, arise from bile duct infection with bacteria or parasites (Clonorchis), form in the bile ducts, and are radiolucent. The cholesterol triad comprises supersaturation (increased cholesterol secretion into bile, and decreased bile salts or phospholipids to dissolve it), accelerated nucleation (excess nucleation factors such as mucin, and no nucleation inhibitors to stop crystals forming), and gallbladder hypomotility (stasis, giving crystals time to grow into stones). Risk factors are the 6 Fs: female (estrogen raises cholesterol secretion), fat (obesity), fertile (pregnancy, multiparity), forty (age 40 or older), fair (white or Indigenous American ancestry), and family history. Others are rapid weight loss, bariatric surgery, oral contraceptives, estrogen therapy, fibrates, octreotide, fasting, TPN (hypomotility), Crohn disease, and ileal resection (bile salt loss).

Clinical presentation and complications

Cholelithiasis is mostly asymptomatic and often found by chance on imaging. In biliary colic, a heavy, fatty meal triggers CCK release, the gallbladder contracts against an impacted stone, and epigastric or RUQ pain radiates to the back, resolving within 6 hours as the stone dislodges. Stones cause disease by location: in the gallbladder, cholelithiasis; in the cystic duct, biliary colic with a brief block or cholecystitis with a persistent block; in the common bile duct, choledocholithiasis, which can lead to cholangitis; and at the ampulla, gallstone pancreatitis.

Diagnosis

Gallstones are evaluated by RUQ ultrasound, which is first line and shows mobile, shadowing stones. LFTs and FBC assess complications and are normal in biliary colic.

Management

Asymptomatic gallstones need no treatment. Biliary colic is treated with pain relief (e.g. NSAIDs), then elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Patients not fit for surgery can have ursodeoxycholic acid, for small cholesterol stones only.

A stone in the CBD blocks bile flow from the liver, causing cholestasis, and a stone at the ampulla blocks both bile and pancreatic ducts, causing gallstone pancreatitis. Workup depends on the likelihood of a stone, and treatment aims to clear the duct and remove the gallbladder. Choledocholithiasis causes RUQ pain, jaundice, pruritus, dark urine, and pale stools, with a cholestatic pattern and normal lipase. The cholestatic LFT pattern is increased ALP and GGT, increased conjugated bilirubin, and AST/ALT that can spike early, then fall. Tumors in or around the bile ducts gradually obstruct bile flow, causing painless obstructive jaundice. Pancreatic head adenocarcinoma is the most common periampullary cancer, and cholangiocarcinoma and gallbladder carcinoma arise within the biliary tree. Courvoisier law separates malignant from gallstone obstruction. Here, we review their features, workup, and treatment, and consider how they relate to gallstone disease and PSC.

Key points

  • Painless jaundice with a palpable gallbladder suggests malignancy.
  • The double duct sign supports a periampullary tumor.
  • CA 19-9 and CEA are not for screening.
  • Resectable periampullary tumors are treated by pancreaticoduodenectomy.

Etiology

Pancreatic head adenocarcinoma is the most common periampullary cancer, with risk factors of smoking, chronic pancreatitis, and diabetes. Ampullary adenocarcinoma blocks bile early, so it is found earlier and has a better prognosis, may cause intermittent jaundice, and is linked to FAP. Duodenal adenocarcinoma is rare, may also cause GI bleeding or obstruction, and is linked to FAP, Lynch syndrome, and celiac disease. Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors arise from islet cells and may secrete hormones (insulinoma, gastrinoma, vipoma, glucagonoma), with MEN1 as a risk factor. Bile duct adenocarcinoma (cholangiocarcinoma) is intrahepatic, perihilar, or distal, with perihilar (Klatskin) most common, and risk factors of PSC, liver flukes (Clonorchis), and choledochal cysts. Symptoms are painless jaundice, pruritus, dark urine, pale stools, weight loss, and a palpable, nontender gallbladder. Acute pancreatitis: autodigestion and its local and systemic consequences

Premature activation of trypsin inside the pancreas leads to autodigestion, causing local and systemic inflammation. Gallstones and ethanol are the leading causes in the I GET SMASHED mnemonic. Diagnosis requires 2 of 3 criteria, and severity is graded by organ failure and local complications. Here, we review its causes, complications, and management, and consider how it relates to biliary disease and malabsorption.

Key points

  • Gallstones are the most common cause, and ethanol the second.
  • Diagnosis requires any 2 of pain, enzymes, and imaging.
  • RUQ ultrasound is obtained in every patient.
  • CT too early can miss necrosis.
  • Prophylactic ABX are not given.

Etiology and mechanisms

I GET SMASHED covers idiopathic disease, with no cause found in about 10–20%; gallstones, the most common cause, in which a stone blocks the ampulla; ethanol, the second most common, through direct acinar toxicity and protein plugs in ducts; trauma, such as blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar injury; steroids; mumps and other infections, such as coxsackievirus and CMV; autoimmune causes, including IgG4-related autoimmune pancreatitis, SLE, and polyarteritis nodosa; scorpion sting (Tityus trinitatis, Trinidad); hypercalcemia, hypertriglyceridemia (usually over 1000 mg/dL), and hypothermia; ERCP, of which it is the most common complication; and drugs, including azathioprine, valproate, thiazides, furosemide, sulfonamides, and didanosine.

Clinical presentation and complications

Patients have severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, nausea and vomiting, fever, tachycardia, and epigastric tenderness and guarding. Trypsin activated inside the pancreas causes autodigestion, with epigastric pain and increased IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, leading to SIRS and local complications. Systemically, increased capillary permeability causes third spacing, with hypovolemia and fluid in the lungs and abdomen, leading to shock, AKI, ARDS, exudative ascites, and abdominal compartment syndrome. Increased tissue factor causes clotting throughout small vessels, consuming platelets and clotting factors and shearing red cells (schistocytes), leading to DIC. Lipase digests surrounding fat, and fatty acids bind calcium (fat saponification), causing hypocalcemia; islet damage causes hyperglycemia. Locally, interstitial edematous pancreatitis causes pancreatic edema and fluid around the pancreas, forming a pseudocyst after 4 weeks, with no epithelium, which can cause infection, rupture, and compression with small bowel or gastric outlet obstruction. Necrotizing pancreatitis causes tissue necrosis and an acute necrotic collection, becoming walled-off necrosis after 4 weeks; enzymes erode blood vessels, causing retroperitoneal bleeding (Cullen and Grey Turner signs). Repeated attacks can lead to chronic pancreatitis.

Diagnosis

Any 2 of 3 criteria are required: acute, severe epigastric pain, often radiating to the back; lipase (or amylase) at least 3 times the upper limit of normal, with lipase more specific; and typical findings on contrast CT abdomen, MRI, or ultrasound. The cause is sought with RUQ ultrasound for gallstones in every patient, LFTs (ALT over 150 suggests gallstones), triglycerides, calcium, alcohol history, and medications. Contrast CT is obtained if the diagnosis is unclear, or after 48–72 hours if the patient is not improving, as CT too early can miss necrosis. Severity is assessed with FBC, creatinine, BUN, oxygen saturation, and scores such as BISAP. By the revised Atlanta classification, mild disease has no organ failure and no local complications, moderately severe disease has organ failure lasting under 48 hours or local complications, and severe disease has organ failure lasting over 48 hours.

Management

Supportive care comprises goal-directed IV fluids (e.g. Hartmann solution), pain relief, and early oral feeding, with enteral feeding if severe. The cause is treated: cholecystectomy during the same admission for gallstones, stopping drinking for alcohol, insulin infusion for hypertriglyceridemia, and stopping the offending drug. Infected necrosis is treated with ABX (e.g. carbapenem) and delayed drainage or necrosectomy, ideally after 4 weeks, with no prophylactic ABX. Pseudocysts are observed, and drained only if symptomatic or complicated.

Relationships to other conditions

. A stone at the ampulla blocks both bile and pancreatic ducts. A cholestatic LFT pattern and ALT over 150 favor a gallstone cause.

. Repeated attacks lead to chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic insufficiency.

. Epigastric pain overlaps; radiation to the back and increased lipase favor pancreatitis, and pain that changes with meals favors PUD.

. Autoimmune pancreatitis can occur with IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis.

. Painless jaundice favors a pancreatic head tumor over pancreatitis.

Appendicitis: luminal obstruction, migrating pain, and perforation

Obstruction of the appendix lumen leads to increased pressure, infection, and ischemia, causing inflammation and eventually perforation. Pain classically migrates from the periumbilical region to the RLQ as inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum. Here, we review its causes, examination signs, age-specific imaging, and treatment, and consider how it relates to diverticulitis and other causes of abdominal pain.

Key points

  • Fecalith is the most common cause in adults, and lymphoid hyperplasia in children.
  • Pain migrates from visceral (T10) to parietal over 12–24 hours.
  • Imaging depends on the patient: CT AP in adults, and ultrasound first in children and pregnancy.
  • Diagnosis is often clinical.

Etiology and mechanisms

Obstruction arises from a fecalith, most common in adults; lymphoid hyperplasia, in children and after viral infection; or a neoplasm, such as carcinoid, in older adults. Increased intraluminal pressure causes distention and infection, then ischemia and infarction, then perforation and abscess.

Clinical presentation and complications

Periumbilical visceral pain (T10) moves to sharp, localized RLQ pain as inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum over 12–24 hours, with anorexia, nausea and vomiting after the pain, and low-grade fever. McBurney point is tenderness one-third of the way from the right ASIS to the umbilicus and suggests typical appendix position. Rovsing sign is pain in the RLQ on pressing the LLQ, suggesting peritoneal irritation in the RLQ. Psoas sign is pain on extending the right hip with the patient lying on the left side, suggesting a retrocecal appendix irritating the iliopsoas. Obturator sign is pain on internally rotating the flexed right hip, suggesting a pelvic appendix irritating the obturator internus. Rebound tenderness and guarding, with pain when pressure is released and a tense abdominal wall, suggest peritonitis. Complications are perforation after 48–72 hours, particularly in young children and older adults; abscess or phlegmon; and generalized peritonitis and sepsis.

Diagnosis

Bloods show leukocytosis with left shift and CRP, β-hCG is obtained in anyone who could be pregnant to rule out ectopic pregnancy, and urinalysis is performed. Adults have CT AP with contrast, showing an appendix over 6 mm and fat stranding. Children and pregnant patients have ultrasound first to avoid radiation, with MRI if unclear in pregnancy. Diagnosis is often clinical (e.g. Alvarado score).

Management

Uncomplicated appendicitis is treated by laparoscopic appendectomy with perioperative ABX, or ABX alone in selected cases. Perforation with generalized peritonitis requires emergency appendectomy with IV ABX. Perforation with a contained abscess is treated with IV ABX and percutaneous drainage, with appendectomy later if needed.

Relationships to other conditions

. Both are blind pouches obstructed by a fecalith, with abscess and perforation as complications. Appendicitis causes RLQ pain, and diverticulitis LLQ pain.

. RLQ pain is shared; a longer history with diarrhea and weight loss favors Crohn disease.

. Carcinoid is a cause of appendiceal obstruction in older adults, and can cause secretory diarrhea.

Ectopic pregnancy. This is the mimic to exclude, so β-hCG is obtained in anyone who could be pregnant.
