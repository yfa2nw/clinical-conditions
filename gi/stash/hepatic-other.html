Pathophysiology

Four liver diseases are unique to pregnancy and appear at characteristic times: vomiting in hyperemesis gravidarum, altered bile transport in intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, endothelial injury in HELLP syndrome, and fetal fatty acids overwhelming the maternal liver in acute fatty liver of pregnancy; other liver diseases can also occur in pregnancy or predate it.

Review article

Liver disease in pregnancy: four diseases unique to pregnancy, and the rest

Four liver diseases are unique to pregnancy and appear at characteristic times: hyperemesis gravidarum in the first trimester, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy from the second, and HELLP syndrome and acute fatty liver of pregnancy in the third. They range from benign itch to maternal liver failure, and the timing, pruritus, hemolysis, hypoglycemia, and coagulopathy separate them; delivery cures the most dangerous. Pregnancy also alters the course of viral hepatitis, thrombosis, gallstones, and preexisting liver disease, and normal pregnancy changes some liver tests. Here, we review the mechanisms, recognition, and management of these conditions.

Key points

  • In normal pregnancy, ALP rises up to 4 times from the placenta and albumin falls from hemodilution, but any rise in ALT, AST, or bilirubin needs evaluation.
  • Hyperemesis gravidarum causes intractable vomiting with weight loss over 5% and ketonuria, with a high ALT, and needs thiamine when prolonged.
  • Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy causes nocturnal itch of the palms and soles with high serum bile acids, is treated with ursodeoxycholic acid, and threatens the fetus more than the mother.
  • HELLP syndrome is a severe variant of preeclampsia with hemolysis, high liver enzymes, and low platelets, treated with magnesium, antihypertensives, and early delivery.
  • AFLP follows fetal LCHAD deficiency; hypoglycemia, coagulopathy, and conjugated bilirubin separate it from HELLP, and prompt delivery is the only cure.
  • Hepatitis E in the third trimester can cause fulminant liver failure, with maternal mortality up to 30%.

Pathophysiology

In normal pregnancy, ALP rises up to 4 times normal in the third trimester from placental ALP, cholesterol, triglycerides, fibrinogen, and ceruloplasmin rise, albumin falls from hemodilution, and GGT falls slightly, so a GGT rising with ALP, or any rise in ALT, AST, or bilirubin, needs evaluation; spider angiomata and palmar erythema can be normal, and the gallbladder is larger and empties more slowly. Hyperemesis gravidarum is driven by intractable vomiting with hormonal and motility changes, and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy by estrogen and genetic changes in bile transport. In HELLP syndrome, abnormal placental vessels and maternal endothelial injury cause vasospasm and increased vascular resistance, and platelets and fibrin deposit in the sinusoids, causing hemolysis, low platelets, and periportal (zone 1) necrosis and hemorrhage.

In fetal LCHAD deficiency, fetal fatty acids reach the maternal liver and overwhelm its mitochondria, leaving tiny fat droplets in hepatocytes as microvesicular steatosis. Failing hepatocytes cause hypoglycemia, decreased clotting factor synthesis causes increased INR and DIC, and increased ammonia causes encephalopathy, as acute liver failure develops.

Etiology and mechanisms

Hyperemesis gravidarum affects up to 1.5% of pregnancies and is linked to age under 20, obesity, diabetes, nulliparity, H. pylori, hormonal change, and abnormal gut motility. Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy affects up to 1.5% of pregnancies in the US; high estrogen, including high-dose oral contraceptives, and genetic variants of bile transport such as MDR3 and ABCB4 alter canalicular transport, and it is rare in Asian or Black women. HELLP syndrome is a severe variant of preeclampsia, which is hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria or end-organ dysfunction.

Fetal LCHAD deficiency, with the mother as a carrier, allows fetal fatty acids to reach the mother. Maternal liver mitochondria are overwhelmed, causing microvesicular steatosis and acute liver failure. Risk factors are the third trimester (usually 30–38 weeks), first pregnancy, twin pregnancy, male fetus, and pre-eclampsia.

Other liver diseases can occur in pregnancy. Hepatitis A, B, and C behave as outside pregnancy, but hepatitis E in the third trimester causes fulminant liver failure with maternal mortality up to 30%. Pregnancy raises procoagulant and lowers anticoagulant factors, so Budd-Chiari syndrome and portal vein thrombosis occur, usually with another thrombotic risk such as protein S deficiency or factor V Leiden, and slower gallbladder motility favors gallstones and sludge, which usually resolve after delivery. Of preexisting disease, cirrhosis is rare because most patients are anovulatory, and the larger circulating volume worsens portal hypertension; autoimmune hepatitis is common in young women and usually quietens in pregnancy; and untreated Wilson disease causes miscarriage, stillbirth, and prematurity.

Clinical presentation and complications

Hyperemesis gravidarum causes intractable nausea and vomiting, weight loss, and ketonuria, with transient hyperthyroidism in up to 60% from hCG, and rarely Wernicke encephalopathy, esophageal rupture, pneumomediastinum, or retinal hemorrhage. Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy causes pruritus of the palms and soles, worse at night, with jaundice rarely, after about 2 weeks of itch, and steatorrhea; vitamin K malabsorption prolongs the PT, and the fetus is at risk of prematurity, respiratory distress, and intrauterine death. Encephalopathy or abdominal pain is not part of it and prompts a search for another cause. HELLP syndrome is often asymptomatic or causes RUQ pain, hepatomegaly, nausea, vomiting, and confusion, with rare hemolytic jaundice, and carries maternal mortality up to 5% and fetal mortality up to 30%.

Symptoms are nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, malaise, jaundice, polyuria, and polydipsia. Complications are acute liver failure (hypoglycemia, coagulopathy with DIC, encephalopathy), acute kidney injury, and fetal distress and death.

Diagnosis

Hyperemesis gravidarum is diagnosed clinically, with high ALT and AST, ALT usually under 250 U/L but up to 1000. Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy is a diagnosis of exclusion, marked by high total and fasting serum bile acids, sometimes the only abnormality, with ALT rarely over 250 U/L and high ALP and bilirubin; ultrasound excludes biliary obstruction and viral hepatitis is excluded. HELLP syndrome shows intravascular hemolysis with schistocytes and unconjugated bilirubin, AST and ALT over 10 times normal, and low platelets, and is graded by the Tennessee or Mississippi criteria.

Key findings are increased bilirubin, increased AST/ALT (usually under 1000 U/L), decreased glucose, increased INR, decreased fibrinogen, increased ammonia, uric acid, and creatinine, and leukocytosis. Diagnosis is clinical (Swansea criteria), and biopsy is rarely needed. Bilirubin is over 90% conjugated, unlike the hemolytic rise of HELLP, fibrinogen is low and bleeding frequent but DIC is present in only about 10%, and imaging may show fatty liver, although a normal scan does not exclude it.

Management

Hyperemesis gravidarum is treated with bowel rest, antiemetics, IV fluids, correction of electrolytes, and thiamine when vomiting is prolonged. Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy is treated with ursodeoxycholic acid, which relieves itch and improves fetal outcome, with cholestyramine or hydroxyzine if it fails, and vitamin K at least 6 hours before delivery, which is planned at 38 weeks, or 36 if severe; it resolves 4–6 weeks after delivery and recurs in later pregnancies. HELLP syndrome is treated with antihypertensives and magnesium sulfate, with early delivery as the cornerstone and corticosteroids for 24–48 hours for fetal lungs before 34 weeks.

In AFLP, prompt delivery, whatever the gestation and after correcting hypoglycemia and coagulopathy, is the only cure. Support comprises IV glucose, blood products (FFP, cryoprecipitate), and ICU care. Follow-up includes testing the newborn for LCHAD deficiency and counseling about recurrence.

Hepatitis B is treated with tenofovir from 28–30 weeks if the viral load is over 200 000 IU/mL, with immunoprophylaxis for the newborn, and Budd-Chiari syndrome with low-molecular-weight heparin, since oral anticoagulants are teratogenic. Cholecystitis is treated with ABX and IV fluids, with cholecystectomy after delivery unless conservative treatment fails, and ERCP is possible from the second trimester. In cirrhosis, EGD before or during the second trimester assesses varices, treated with nonselective β-blockers or banding; autoimmune hepatitis is treated with prednisone and lower-dose azathioprine; and chelation for Wilson disease continues without interruption, zinc being the safest.

Relationships to other conditions

AFLP and HELLP syndrome. Hypoglycemia is common in AFLP and rare in HELLP; coagulopathy is common in AFLP and uncommon in HELLP; hemolysis is uncommon in AFLP and present in HELLP; platelets are normal or mildly low in AFLP and low in HELLP; and hypertension is sometimes present in AFLP and usually present in HELLP. Both are treated with delivery.
