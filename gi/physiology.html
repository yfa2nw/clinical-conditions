Embryology of the gut and its defects: separation, rotation, migration, and closure

The gut tube forms in week 3 from endoderm, which lines it and buds the liver, gallbladder, and pancreas, wrapped in mesoderm, which forms its wall and muscle, and colonized by neural crest, which forms the ENS. The foregut, midgut, and hindgut take their blood from the celiac artery, SMA, and IMA. The midgut herniates, rotates 270° around the SMA, and returns by week 10. Most congenital defects follow one failed step: separation of trachea and esophagus, recanalization of the duodenum, rotation of the midgut or ventral pancreas, obliteration of the vitelline duct, migration of neural crest, or closure of the abdominal wall. Here, we review these steps and their defects.

Key points

  • Endoderm forms the epithelium, mesoderm the wall and muscle, and neural crest the ENS.
  • The midgut rotates 270° counterclockwise around the SMA while herniated in weeks 6–10.
  • Duodenal atresia follows failed recanalization, shows a double bubble, and occurs with Down syndrome in 30–40%.
  • Pyloric stenosis causes projectile, nonbilious vomiting at 3–6 weeks with hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.
  • Gastroschisis is uncovered and beside the umbilicus; omphalocele is covered by a sac and linked to trisomies.

Germ layers and regions

Endoderm forms the epithelial lining of the gut, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas, mesoderm forms the wall, connective tissue, and smooth muscle, and neural crest from the ectoderm forms the enteric neurons. The foregut runs from the mouth to the upper duodenum and is supplied by the celiac artery, the midgut from the mid-duodenum to the proximal two-thirds of the transverse colon by the SMA, and the hindgut from the distal third of the transverse colon to the upper anus by the IMA. The gut tube forms in week 3, the buccopharyngeal membrane resorbs in week 4, the vitelline duct closes in week 7, the cloacal membrane opens and villi begin in week 9, muscle layers appear by week 11 and crypts by week 12, and absorption begins by week 24 and reaches adult levels by week 32. Organs form in weeks 3–8, when teratogens matter most.

Rotation and the pancreas

Between weeks 6 and 10, the midgut herniates through the umbilical ring, rotates 270° counterclockwise around the SMA, and returns, after which the cecum descends to the right lower quadrant. The ventral pancreatic bud forms the lower head and uncinate process and its duct becomes the main duct of Wirsung, while the dorsal bud forms the body and tail and its duct becomes the accessory duct of Santorini; the buds rotate and fuse in weeks 4–8. A fetus that cannot swallow amniotic fluid past an obstruction develops polyhydramnios, an amniotic fluid index over 25 cm, and GI obstruction can be diagnosed by ultrasound in the late second to early third trimester.

When it fails

Failed lateral septation of the foregut causes esophageal atresia and TEF, most often type C, a blind upper pouch with a distal fistula, in 84%, with choking and respiratory distress, polyhydramnios, a nasogastric tube that will not pass, and cardiac defects. Pyloric stenosis, acquired after birth, causes projectile, nonbilious vomiting at 3–6 weeks, an olive in 60–80%, and hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis, with a wall of 3 mm or more on ultrasound, and is treated by pyloromyotomy. Duodenal atresia follows failed recanalization below the ampulla, causes vomiting within 24–38 hours of the first feed and a double bubble, and occurs with Down syndrome in 30–40%. Annular pancreas, a ventral bud that fails to rotate and encircles the duodenum, causes 30% of congenital duodenal obstruction, and pancreas divisum, in about 10%, is the most common pancreatic malformation. Biliary atresia causes jaundice beyond 2 weeks, acholic stools, dark urine, and hepatomegaly, and needs a Kasai portoenterostomy before 60 days, though many need a transplant.

Malrotation leaves fibrous bands and a mesentery that can twist into volvulus, and bilious vomiting, usually the first sign, needs urgent evaluation. Incomplete obliteration of the vitelline duct leaves a Meckel diverticulum, with painless bleeding from ectopic gastric mucosa and the rule of 2s, while a fully patent duct drains meconium from the umbilicus. Jejunal and ileal atresia follow mesenteric vessel disruption, meconium ileus calls for testing for CF, Hirschsprung disease follows failed neural crest migration, affecting the rectosigmoid in about 80%, and anorectal malformations affect 1 in 5000 births, about 70% of affected boys having a recto-urethral fistula. Gastroschisis is an uncovered defect beside, usually right of, the umbilicus in young mothers, with survival over 90%, whereas omphalocele herniates through the cord base inside a peritoneal sac and is linked with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome and trisomies 13, 18, and 21.

Gastric secretion: neural, endocrine, and local control of acid and its protection

Food triggers gastric secretion in three overlapping phases, which start it, sustain it, and then slow it down. The cephalic phase contributes about 30% of HCl, the gastric phase about 60%, and the intestinal phase about 10%. Secretion depends on seven gastric cell types and on signals that converge on the parietal cell, while a two-layer mucosal barrier protects the mucosa and D cells apply negative feedback. Here, we review this control, how it fails, and how drugs suppress acid or protect the mucosa, and consider how it relates to acid-related conditions.

Key points

  • Higher-order neurons drive the DMV, and efferent CN X stimulates the stomach; thoracolumbar sympathetic fibers inhibit it.
  • Gastrin promotes ECL, parietal, and chief cells, and histamine from ECL cells promotes parietal cells.
  • Excess protons drive D cells, whose somatostatin inhibits G, ECL, parietal, and chief cells.
  • Low pH is the brake on gastrin, so losing HCl raises gastrin.
  • The intestinal phase mostly slows secretion, giving the duodenum time to neutralize and digest chyme.

Cephalic phase

Sight, smell, taste, and thought of food act through higher-order neurons on the DMV, also called DMX. Efferent CN X synapses at the stomach and stimulates it, so secretion starts before food arrives. Afferent CN X carries vagovagal feedback from the stomach to the brainstem, whereas sympathetic fibers from the thoracolumbar outflow inhibit stomach activity. This phase depends on CN X, so vagotomy abolishes it.

Gastric cells and the mucosal barrier

Foveolar cells, at the surface and in gastric pits, and mucous neck cells, in the gland neck, secrete mucus. Parietal cells in the body and fundus secrete protons and IF, and chief cells at the gland base secrete pepsinogen and gastric lipase. ECL cells in the body and fundus secrete histamine, G cells in the antrum secrete gastrin, and D cells in the body, fundus, and antrum secrete somatostatin. Foveolar and mucous neck cells form the mucosal barrier, which has a top layer of 95% water, phospholipids, electrolytes, and mucin proteins, and a bottom layer of bicarbonate.

Gastric phase

Local and vagal stimulation act on G cells. Gastrin then promotes ECL, parietal, and chief cells, and histamine from ECL cells promotes parietal cells, which secrete protons through the H+/K+ ATPase in exchange for K+. Vagal ACh promotes chief cells, and the pepsinogen they secrete is activated to pepsin at pH under 3.5. D cells are negative regulators: excess protons lead to somatostatin, which inhibits G, ECL, parietal, and chief cells. Inside the parietal cell, carbonic anhydrase makes H+ and HCO3−; the H+ is pumped into the lumen by the H+/K+ ATPase, while the HCO3− leaves into the blood through the basolateral Cl−/HCO3− exchanger (AE2), so blood HCO3− rises briefly after meals, the alkaline tide. Vomiting or NG suction loses HCl and leaves its HCO3− in the blood, causing hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, and the volume loss activates RAAS, causing hypokalemia.

Receptors and intracellular pathways

On parietal cells, ACh acts through M3 and gastrin through CCK-2, both using Gq and increased Ca2+, whereas histamine acts through H2, using Gs and increased cAMP. Somatostatin through SSTR2 and PGE2 through EP3 inhibit parietal cells, using Gi and decreased cAMP. On ECL cells, gastrin stimulates through CCK-2, and somatostatin inhibits through SSTR2. On chief cells, ACh through M3 and gastrin through CCK-2 stimulate, and somatostatin through SSTR2 inhibits. On G cells, GRP through BB2 and amino acids through CaSR stimulate, using Gq and increased Ca2+, and somatostatin through SSTR2 inhibits.

Intestinal phase

Chyme in the duodenum mostly slows secretion. Peptides and amino acids release gastrin from G cells, causing a brief rise in parietal cell activity, and entero-oxyntin, which stimulates parietal cells. Chyme and distension release 5-HT from enterochromaffin cells, increasing motility and secretion. Acid releases secretin from S cells, which decreases gastrin and HCl and increases pancreatic HCO3−. Fat and amino acids release CCK from I cells, which decreases HCl, slows gastric emptying, and causes gallbladder contraction and pancreatic enzyme release. Fat and glucose release GIP from K cells, which decreases HCl and increases insulin. Chyme reaching the ileum releases PYY and GLP-1 from L cells, which inhibit parietal cells and slow gastric emptying. Distension or hyperosmolar chyme triggers the enterogastric reflex, which decreases HCl and slows gastric emptying.

Pharmacology

Antacids (calcium carbonate, sodium bicarbonate, magnesium salts, and aluminum hydroxide) neutralize HCl in the lumen, increasing gastric pH and giving fast, short-acting relief. H2R antagonists (famotidine and cimetidine) competitively block histamine at H2 receptors on parietal cells, decreasing cAMP and H+/K+ ATPase activity, and so decrease HCl, especially basal and nocturnal secretion. PPIs (omeprazole and lansoprazole) are prodrugs activated in parietal cell canaliculi that irreversibly bind the pump, causing a profound decrease in basal and stimulated HCl. Misoprostol is a PG analog acting on EP receptors on parietal cells; it decreases HCl and increases mucus and HCO3−, giving gastroprotection in NSAID users. Sucralfate polymerizes at pH under 4 into a paste that binds ulcer base proteins and increases local PG and HCO3−, forming a barrier against acid, pepsin, and bile; because it needs acid, it works poorly with acid-suppressing drugs. Bismuth subcitrate coats the ulcer base, kills H. pylori, and increases PG and HCO3−.

Relationships to other conditions

Peptic ulcer disease. H. pylori increases gastrin and so HCl, and NSAIDs and alcohol inhibit COX, decreasing PG, which increases HCl and decreases mucus. Both therefore damage the balance between acid and the mucosal barrier.

Zollinger-Ellison syndrome and autoimmune gastritis. Both raise gastrin, but for opposite reasons. In Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, gastrin from a tumor ignores feedback, so HCl is high; in autoimmune gastritis, parietal cell loss decreases HCl and IF, so feedback is lost. High HCl favors the former, and low HCl with B12 deficiency favors the latter. Secretin normally inhibits gastrin release but paradoxically increases it from gastrinoma cells.

Cushing ulcers and NSAID injury. Both increase HCl. In Cushing ulcers, increased ICP increases CN X activity, whereas NSAIDs act through COX inhibition and also decrease mucus.

GERD. Here the problem is the location of HCl, which enters the esophagus, so the drugs that suppress acid apply here as well.

. Secretion links directly to digestion: HCl converts pepsinogen to pepsin, IF is needed for B12 absorption, and the intestinal phase slows the stomach so that the duodenum can neutralize and digest chyme.

The emetic center: inputs, the vomiting reflex, and antiemetic targets

Vomiting is coordinated not by a single center but by loosely organized neuron pools in the medulla, which a central pattern generator fires in sequence. Four inputs converge on the NTS, the start of the final common pathway: the CTZ in the area postrema, which samples blood and CSF through leaky capillaries; vagal afferents from the gut; the vestibular system; and the cortex and thalamus. Each input carries characteristic receptors, D2, 5-HT3, NK1, μ-opioid, H1, and muscarinic, which explain both the triggers of vomiting and the targets of antiemetics. Here, we review the inputs, the reflex, its failures, and the drugs that block it.

Key points

  • The CTZ lies in the area postrema, outside the BBB, and carries D2, 5-HT3, NK1, and μ-opioid receptors.
  • All inputs converge on the NTS, which drives a central pattern generator in the medullary reticular formation.
  • Chemotherapy releases 5-HT from the gut wall onto 5-HT3 receptors; delayed CINV on days 2–5 depends on substance P.
  • Motion sickness comes from vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive mismatch and is blocked by H1 antagonists and scopolamine.
  • 5-HT3 antagonists prevent acute CINV, NK1 antagonists delayed CINV, and D2 antagonists act on the CTZ.

Inputs

The CTZ lies in the area postrema on the floor of the fourth ventricle, a circumventricular organ whose capillaries lack tight junctions, so it detects toxins in both blood and CSF. Its receptors include D2, 5-HT3, NK1, and μ-opioid receptors, which respond to chemotherapy, opioids, and antiparkinsonian drugs. Gastric irritation releases 5-HT from the gut wall onto 5-HT3 receptors on vagal afferents, which end in the NTS. The vestibular system, with H1 and muscarinic receptors, responds to motion that the vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive systems report differently, and higher centers in the cortex and thalamus form the fourth input.

The area postrema, NTS, and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus form the dorsal vagal complex. The area postrema receives afferents from CN IX and CN X and the hypothalamus and projects to the NTS, which carries NK1 and μ-opioid receptors and begins the final common pathway. Lesioning the area postrema stops vomiting from most emetic drugs but not from motion or vagal afferents. The NTS drives the central pattern generator in the retrofacial nucleus of the reticular formation, which sequences the respiratory and abdominal muscles, so there is no single vomiting center.

The vomiting reflex

Outputs from the NTS coordinate swallowing, salivation, breathing, the circulation, and the gut. The diaphragm and abdominal muscles contract, the external intercostals contract while the internal intercostals relax, the esophagus contracts while the LES relaxes, and the stomach and duodenum show gastric dysrhythmia and duodenal retroperistalsis. Repeated vomiting loses HCl, leaving its HCO3− in the blood and causing hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis.

When it fails

Chemotherapy and radiation release 5-HT from the gut wall onto 5-HT3 receptors, causing acute CINV within 24 hours, while delayed CINV on days 2–5 depends on substance P. Opioids stimulate D2 and μ receptors in the CTZ, but high doses cross the BBB to μ receptors in the NTS, which suppress vomiting. PONV is most likely in young to middle-aged nonsmoking women with previous PONV or motion sickness and rises with volatile anesthetics, nitrous oxide, long surgery, and postoperative opioids. Antiparkinsonian drugs act on the D2-dense area postrema and cause nausea.

Motion sickness arises from vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive mismatch, and people with total loss of labyrinthine function are immune. In NMOSD, attacks on the AQP4-rich area postrema cause intractable nausea, vomiting, and hiccups. Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome follows years of cannabis use, with cyclical vomiting relieved by hot showers that resolves after stopping. Cyclic vomiting syndrome causes stereotyped episodes separated by baseline health, is linked with migraine, and is diagnosed on Rome criteria after excluding mimics. Hyperemesis gravidarum starts at 4–6 weeks with weight loss of 5% or more, ketonuria, and electrolyte imbalance, and thiamine prevents Wernicke encephalopathy. On the condition pages, appendicitis causes periumbilical pain moving to the right lower quadrant followed by anorexia and vomiting, acute pancreatitis causes severe epigastric pain radiating to the back with nausea and vomiting, and Bouveret syndrome, a gallstone causing gastric outlet obstruction, causes vomiting and epigastric pain.

Pharmacology

D2 antagonists, metoclopramide and prochlorperazine, block D2 receptors in the CTZ, and metoclopramide also stimulates 5-HT4 receptors, releasing ACh in the gut and speeding gastric emptying. 5-HT3 antagonists such as ondansetron and granisetron block 5-HT on vagal afferents and in the CTZ, for acute CINV, radiation, and PONV. NK1 antagonists such as aprepitant and netupitant block substance P in the area postrema and NTS, preventing delayed CINV.

H1 antagonists such as promethazine, dimenhydrinate, and diphenhydramine block histamine and acetylcholine in the brain, reducing vestibular input, especially in motion sickness, while second-generation antihistamines, which act peripherally, do not prevent it. Scopolamine, given as a transdermal patch, blocks muscarinic receptors and inhibits input to the vestibular nuclei, preventing motion sickness.

Liver function: structure, metabolism, bile, and detoxification

The liver is organized around portal triads and central veins, with blood flowing through sinusoids from zone 1 to zone 3 and bile flowing the opposite way. Its hepatocytes run the core pathways of carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism, synthesize most plasma proteins, and store vitamins. They also make bile salts, conjugate bilirubin, metabolize xenobiotics in three phases, and move molecules through dedicated channels. Here, we review these functions, how they fail, and the drugs that target hepatitis viruses and bile salts, and consider how they relate to clinical conditions.

Key points

  • Blood reaches zone 3 last, so zone 3 is first to suffer from low perfusion.
  • During tissue damage, stellate cells become myofibroblasts that secrete collagen I, causing fibrosis.
  • CYP7A1 is the key first step of bile salt synthesis, and CYP8B1 decides between cholic and chenodeoxycholic acid.
  • Only conjugated bilirubin reaches the urine, so dark urine points to conjugated hyperbilirubinemia.
  • Phase I modifies a molecule, phase II conjugates it, and phase III pumps it out.

Anatomy and histology

Each portal triad holds a hepatic artery carrying oxygenated blood, a portal vein carrying blood from the gut, and a bile duct, and the central vein drains the sinusoids of each lobule. Sinusoidal capillaries carry mixed arterial and portal blood past hepatocytes, the main functional cells. Kupffer cells sense gram-negative LPS, parasites, and bacteria, produce cytokines, and convert old RBCs into heme and globin. Stellate cells store vitamin A during quiescence and, during tissue damage, become myofibroblasts that secrete collagen I. The space of Disse lies between sinusoids and hepatocytes, and bile canaliculi collect bile between hepatocytes. Bile contains bile salts, bilirubin, phospholipid, cholesterol, water, and electrolytes. In the acinus, periportal zone 1 receives the most oxygen and handles gluconeogenesis, beta oxidation, and urea synthesis; zone 2 is midzonal; and centrilobular zone 3 receives the least oxygen and handles glycolysis, lipogenesis, and CYP metabolism.

Carbohydrate metabolism

In glycolysis, glucokinase forms glucose 6-P, PFK-1 forms fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, and pyruvate kinase forms pyruvate. In gluconeogenesis, pyruvate carboxylase forms oxaloacetate, PEPCK forms PEP, fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase forms fructose 6-P, and glucose 6-phosphatase forms glucose. In glycogenesis, glucose 6-P forms glucose 1-P, UDP-glucose, and then glycogen through glycogen synthase. In glycogenolysis, glycogen phosphorylase forms glucose 1-P, which becomes glucose 6-P and then glucose. Glucose 6-phosphatase lets the liver release free glucose into the blood.

Amino acids and protein synthesis

ALT and AST perform transamination, converting an amino acid and α-ketoglutarate to a keto acid and glutamate, and glutamate dehydrogenase performs deamination, releasing ammonia. In the urea cycle, carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I forms carbamoyl phosphate from ammonia and CO2, ornithine transcarbamylase forms citrulline, argininosuccinate synthetase adds aspartate, argininosuccinate lyase forms arginine and releases fumarate, and arginase forms urea and releases ornithine. The liver makes albumin; the alpha and beta globulins transferrin, ceruloplasmin, haptoglobin, retinol-binding protein, transcortin, and thyroxine-binding protein; factors I, II, V, VII, IX, and X; AT-III and proteins C and S; complement proteins; and TPO, IGF-1, angiotensinogen, and 25-OH cholecalciferol.

Lipids and vitamins

In lipogenesis, acetyl-CoA from excess glucose forms malonyl-CoA through acetyl-CoA carboxylase, fatty acids through fatty acid synthase, and triglycerides with glycerol 3-phosphate. Fatty acids enter mitochondria through CPT1 for beta oxidation to acetyl-CoA, which can form HMG-CoA through HMG-CoA synthase and then the ketone bodies acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate, used by other tissues. Hepatocytes package triglycerides with ApoB-100 as VLDL, which loses triglycerides in the blood to form IDL and LDL, and the liver makes HDL, which returns cholesterol to it. HMG-CoA reductase forms mevalonate, the rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis, and statins block it; cholesterol leaves in VLDL, into bile, or as bile salts. Vitamin A is stored in stellate cells and vitamin E in the liver. Cholecalciferol from the skin is stored and converted to 25-OH cholecalciferol in the liver, then to calcitriol in the kidney. Reduced vitamin K is needed for gamma-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX, and X and proteins C and S, which forms vitamin K epoxide; VKOR restores it, and warfarin blocks VKOR.

Bile salt synthesis

Cholesterol passes through CYP7A1, the key first step, and forms cholic acid with CYP8B1 and chenodeoxycholic acid without it. The liver conjugates these with glycine or taurine, which makes them more water soluble, forming glycocholic, taurocholic, glycochenodeoxycholic, and taurochenodeoxycholic acid. Micelles digest fat in the small intestine, and most bile salts are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum. In the colon, bacteria deconjugate the rest and form the secondary bile salts deoxycholic and lithocholic acid, some of which are absorbed and return through the portal vein, where the liver reconjugates them.

Bilirubin conjugation

Kupffer cells form heme and globin from old RBCs, and globin is broken down to amino acids. Heme oxygenase forms iron and biliverdin, and biliverdin reductase forms unconjugated bilirubin, which albumin carries to the liver. UGT forms conjugated bilirubin in hepatocytes. Gut bacteria form urobilinogen; reabsorbed urobilinogen reaches the kidney and colors urine yellow as urobilin, and urobilinogen that stays in the gut forms stercobilinogen, then stercobilin, which colors stool brown. Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia leaves urine and stool normal in color, because bilirubin is water insoluble and bound to albumin, whereas conjugated hyperbilirubinemia causes dark urine, and pale stool if bile is obstructed.

Xenobiotic metabolism

In phase I, CYP enzymes oxidize, reduce, or hydrolyze; in phase II, conjugation makes the molecule water soluble; and in phase III, channels pump it into bile or blood. UGT adds glucuronic acid to bilirubin and paracetamol, GST adds glutathione to NAPQI, SULT adds sulfate to paracetamol, and NAT adds an acetyl group to isoniazid. CYP3A4 handles statins, calcium channel blockers, benzodiazepines, and tacrolimus; CYP2D6 handles codeine, tramadol, metoprolol, and tamoxifen; CYP2C9 handles warfarin, phenytoin, and NSAIDs; CYP2C19 handles clopidogrel, PPIs, and diazepam; CYP1A2 handles caffeine, theophylline, and clozapine; and CYP2E1 handles alcohol, paracetamol, and isoniazid. In CYP2D6 poor metabolizers, active drugs build up, so the dose is lowered, and prodrugs such as codeine work poorly; in ultrarapid metabolizers, active drugs clear fast, so the dose is raised, and codeine forms too much morphine. Alcohol is metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase in the cytosol, the main pathway, by CYP2E1, which chronic drinking induces, and by catalase in peroxisomes. Acetaldehyde then forms acetate through aldehyde dehydrogenase, and the raised NADH:NAD+ ratio increases fatty acid synthesis and decreases fatty acid breakdown, causing fatty liver. GSH neutralizes peroxides through glutathione peroxidase, forming GSSG, which glutathione reductase and NADPH restore, and GST joins GSH to toxins such as NAPQI. NAC replenishes glutathione.

Channels

NTCP brings conjugated bile salts into the liver with Na+, OATP brings unconjugated bile salts and drugs such as statins, OCT1 brings organic cations such as metformin, and a bilirubin transporter brings unconjugated bilirubin. Into bile, BSEP sends bile salts, ABCG5/8 cholesterol, MDR3 phospholipid, MRP2 conjugated bilirubin and other conjugates, MDR1 cationic drugs, and BCRP drugs and sulfate conjugates. MRP4/6 send bile salts and other conjugates into blood.

Pharmacology

Tenofovir and entecavir are nucleotide analogs that compete at HBV reverse transcriptase, blocking reverse transcription of pregenomic RNA and suppressing HBV DNA, although cccDNA persists, so HBV is hard to cure. Peginterferon-α acts on interferon receptors, inducing antiviral proteins and boosting the immune response. In HCV, NS3/4A inhibitors such as glecaprevir and grazoprevir block cleavage of the viral polyprotein; NS5A inhibitors such as ledipasvir, velpatasvir, and pibrentasvir bind NS5A, impairing RNA replication and virion assembly; and sofosbuvir, an NS5B inhibitor, is a nucleotide prodrug incorporated into HCV RNA that ends the chain. Combinations cure over 95% of HCV infections. Cholestyramine is a non-absorbed resin that binds bile salts in the intestine and blocks their reabsorption, so the liver converts more cholesterol to bile salts; it decreases cholestatic pruritus, bile acid diarrhea, and LDL, and it binds other drugs too, so it is given apart from them, including ursodeoxycholic acid. Ursodeoxycholic acid is a hydrophilic bile salt that displaces toxic hydrophobic bile salts, increases bile flow, and decreases biliary cholesterol saturation, reducing bile salt toxicity, improving LFTs, slowing progression in PBC, and dissolving cholesterol gallstones.

Relationships to other conditions

Acute liver failure and cirrhosis. Lost synthesis and clearance explain the features: decreased albumin causes ascites and edema, decreased clotting factors cause coagulopathy with increased INR, decreased ammonia clearance causes hepatic encephalopathy, and decreased gluconeogenesis causes hypoglycemia. In cirrhosis, stellate cells drive the fibrosis.

Ischemic hepatitis and paracetamol overdose. Both cause zone 3 necrosis. Decreased perfusion causes zone 3 hypoxia in ischemic hepatitis, whereas in paracetamol overdose, increased NAPQI uses up glutathione. Shock favors the former, and a paracetamol history favors the latter.

Unconjugated and conjugated hyperbilirubinemia. Normal urine color favors an unconjugated cause, such as hemolysis, whereas dark urine and pale stools favor conjugated hyperbilirubinemia from obstruction, as in choledocholithiasis.

Inherited hyperbilirubinemias. Gilbert syndrome and Crigler-Najjar syndrome are both unconjugated, from decreased UGT activity. Gilbert syndrome is benign, with mild jaundice with fasting, stress, or illness; Crigler-Najjar syndrome type I has absent activity, with severe neonatal jaundice and kernicterus; and type II is milder and responds to phenobarbital. Dubin-Johnson syndrome and Rotor syndrome are both conjugated and benign: defective MRP2 with a black liver favors Dubin-Johnson syndrome, and defective OATP reuptake with no black liver favors Rotor syndrome.

PBC and PSC. Cholestasis increases bile salts in blood and conjugated bilirubin, which is why cholestyramine and ursodeoxycholic acid both apply.

Gallstones. Cholesterol stones follow increased cholesterol, or decreased bile salts or phospholipids, whereas black pigment stones follow chronic hemolysis and increased unconjugated bilirubin.

Digestion and absorption: enzyme activation, nutrient transport, and the sites that fail

Enzymes break food down in the lumen and at the brush border, and enterocytes absorb the products, mainly in the duodenum and jejunum. Five sites contribute: the mouth, stomach, pancreas, bile, and brush border. Many enzymes are secreted active, whereas pepsin and the pancreatic proteases are secreted inactive, so they cannot digest the cells that make them. Carbohydrates and proteins reach the portal vein, most fats leave in chylomicrons, and each micronutrient has its own site and route. Here, we review enzyme activation, how each nutrient is digested and absorbed, and how these steps fail, and consider how they relate to clinical conditions.

Key points

  • Enterokinase activates trypsinogen, and trypsin then activates the other pancreatic zymogens and more trypsinogen.
  • Pepsinogen is activated by HCl at pH under 3.5.
  • Glucose and galactose enter through SGLT1 with Na+, fructose through GLUT5, and all three leave through GLUT2.
  • Bile salts emulsify fat and form micelles, and chylomicrons leave into a lacteal.
  • Iron, calcium, and copper are absorbed in the duodenum, folate in the jejunum, and B12 in the terminal ileum.

Enzyme activation

Salivary amylase, lingual lipase, gastric lipase, pancreatic amylase, pancreatic lipase, and cholesterol esterase are secreted active, although pancreatic lipase needs colipase and bile salts, and cholesterol esterase needs bile salts, to work. Brush border enterokinase, disaccharidases, and peptidases are active and stay on the enterocyte. Pepsin is secreted as pepsinogen, which HCl activates at pH under 3.5. Trypsin is secreted as trypsinogen, which enterokinase activates, and trypsin then activates more trypsinogen. Chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidases, prophospholipase A2, and procolipase are all activated by trypsin. Enterokinase sits in the duodenum, so trypsin normally stays inactive until it leaves the pancreas.

Carbohydrates

Salivary amylase starts on starch until stomach acid stops it. Pancreatic amylase forms maltose, maltotriose, and α-limit dextrins, and brush border disaccharidases form monosaccharides: lactase converts lactose to glucose and galactose, sucrase converts sucrose to glucose and fructose, and maltase converts maltose to glucose. Glucose and galactose enter enterocytes through SGLT1 with Na+, fructose enters through GLUT5, and all three leave through GLUT2 into the portal vein. Fiber is not digested, so colonic bacteria ferment it.

Proteins

In the stomach, HCl converts pepsinogen to pepsin, which forms peptides. Trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, and carboxypeptidases form oligopeptides and amino acids, and brush border peptidases form amino acids, dipeptides, and tripeptides. Amino acids enter enterocytes with Na+, and dipeptides and tripeptides enter through PepT1 with H+, after which peptidases form amino acids that leave into the portal vein.

Fats

Lingual and gastric lipase have a minor role in adults. Bile salts emulsify fat into small droplets, and lipase and colipase form monoglycerides and fatty acids, with cholesterol esterase and phospholipase A2 also acting. Micelles of bile salts deliver the products to enterocytes, where triglycerides are rebuilt in the ER, ApoB-48 is added, and chylomicrons leave into a lacteal. Short- and medium-chain fatty acids skip chylomicrons and enter the portal vein directly. Bile salts in micelles in the jejunum are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and return to the liver through the portal vein.

Micronutrients

Iron, calcium, and copper are absorbed in the duodenum, folate and most vitamins in the jejunum, and B12 and bile salts in the terminal ileum. Iron is reduced from Fe3+ to Fe2+, helped by HCl and vitamin C, enters through DMT1, and reaches the blood through ferroportin, carried by transferrin. Hepcidin is the brake on iron: it binds ferroportin and causes it to be degraded. B12 binds haptocorrin from saliva in the stomach, proteases free it in the duodenum, it binds IF from parietal cells, the complex is absorbed in the terminal ileum, and transcobalamin carries it in blood. Copper enters through CTR1, which zinc blocks, reaches the blood through ATP7A, and in the liver ATP7B moves it into bile and onto ceruloplasmin. Vitamins A, D, E, and K travel in micelles and leave in chylomicrons, water-soluble vitamins are absorbed by transporters, mostly in the jejunum, and vitamin D increases calcium absorption. In SIBO, bacteria deconjugate bile salts and cause fat malabsorption, and B12 falls while folate stays normal or high. In abetalipoproteinemia, no apo B lipoproteins form, so enterocytes cannot export chylomicrons, and fat and vitamin E stay behind.

Relationships to other conditions

Maldigestion and malabsorption. Maldigestion leaves food undigested, as in lactose intolerance, pancreatic insufficiency, and bile salt deficiency. Malabsorption reflects mucosal injury, as in celiac disease, where blunted villi decrease absorption, with iron and folate deficiency. Normal enzymes with injured mucosa favor malabsorption, and altered enzyme function without mucosal injury favors maldigestion.

Acute pancreatitis and pancreatic insufficiency. Both involve pancreatic enzymes in opposite ways. In acute pancreatitis, trypsin is activated inside the pancreas, causing autodigestion, whereas in pancreatic insufficiency, decreased enzymes leave fat, protein, and carbohydrate undigested.

Autoimmune gastritis and ileal Crohn disease. Both cause B12 deficiency. Autoimmune gastritis decreases IF, whereas ileal Crohn disease decreases ileal absorption and also causes bile salt loss.

Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. Excess acid inactivates pancreatic enzymes, causing diarrhea, which links gastric secretion to maldigestion.

Hemochromatosis and Wilson disease. Both are metal overload states. In hemochromatosis, decreased hepcidin increases iron absorption, whereas in Wilson disease, decreased ATP7B function decreases copper excretion into bile, so copper builds up in the liver.

The immune system of the gut: sampling, secretory IgA, and tolerance

GALT must defend a vast surface exposed to pathogens while tolerating food and normal flora. M cells over Peyer patches sample luminal antigens and pass them to dendritic cells and macrophages, which prime lymphocytes that travel to the mesenteric lymph nodes. B cells switch to IgA-secreting plasma cells, and enterocytes carry dimeric IgA into the lumen on pIgR as SIgA, the first-line mucosal barrier. Here, we review this system, its tolerance of food, the pathogens and diseases that exploit or misdirect it, and the drugs that suppress it.

Key points

  • Goblet cells make mucus, Paneth cells secrete defensins and lysozyme, and tuft cells start type 2 immunity against helminths through IL-25.
  • M cells over Peyer patches, almost half of them in the distal ileum, carry antigen to dendritic cells and macrophages by transcytosis.
  • Dimeric IgA crosses enterocytes on pIgR and enters the lumen as SIgA, binding antigens before they reach the submucosa.
  • Peyer patch T cells secrete TGF-β and IL-10 toward food; loss of tolerance causes food allergy.
  • Selective IgA deficiency, about 1 in 700, causes sinopulmonary infections and giardiasis and is linked to celiac disease.
  • Vedolizumab blocks gut-homing lymphocytes; anti-TNF, IL-12/23, and JAK drugs block cytokines.

Epithelial cells

Stem cells, LGR5-positive and at the base of the crypts, give rise to every epithelial cell, which matures as it moves up the villus. Enterocytes, about 70% of cells in the ileum, absorb nutrients. Goblet cells secrete mucin into a mucus layer that, in the colon, keeps bacteria off the epithelium, help keep tight junctions, and can import antigen. Enteroendocrine cells sense luminal contents and release incretins such as GLP-1 and GLP-2, which tune absorption, and GLP-1 strengthens the barrier. Paneth cells, at the base of small intestinal crypts beside the stem cells, secrete α-defensins 5 and 6 and lysozyme, keeping the crypt sterile, and support the stem cells with Wnt3, EGF, and Notch signals. Tuft cells sense parasites and release IL-25, which activates type 2 innate lymphoid cells to release IL-13; this promotes more tuft and goblet cells, more mucus, and helminth expulsion, the "weep and sweep" response.

Peyer patches

Peyer patches are organized lymphoid follicles in the lamina propria and submucosa of the jejunum and ileum, and sometimes the duodenum, with almost 50% in the distal ileum; they are most numerous at 15–25 years. They contain mainly B cells, with T cells, plasma cells, mast cells, eosinophils, macrophages, and basophils. The follicle-associated epithelium over each dome has few villi, thin mucus, and M cells, which have few microvilli and a thin glycocalyx and carry antigen across by transcytosis to dendritic cells and macrophages waiting in a basolateral pocket. B cells mature in the germinal center, T cells sit in the parafollicular zone, and activated lymphocytes travel to the mesenteric lymph nodes, where the response is amplified.

Secretory IgA

IgA is made in greater amounts than any other antibody. Plasma cells in the lamina propria secrete monomeric IgA, a J chain joins two monomers, and the dimer binds pIgR at the basolateral surface of enterocytes, crosses the cell, and is released when pIgR is cleaved at the apical surface, leaving the secretory component. SIgA binds antigens in the lumen and keeps them out of the submucosa and circulation, and it is also anti-inflammatory, helping induce tolerance through regulatory T cells. IgA1, about 80% of the total, lines mucosal surfaces and IgA2 is found mainly in the colon; class switching is promoted by IL-5, IL-6, and retinoic acid from vitamin A. IgG crosses the placenta but IgA does not, and IgA in breast milk protects the newborn from enteric infection.

Tolerance and allergy

Food proteins and normal flora are tolerated, and Peyer patch T cells secrete TGF-β and IL-10 toward common food allergens. Cesarean delivery, early antibiotics, and changes in the gut microbiome affect tolerance and barrier integrity. When tolerance is lost, food allergy follows, either IgE-mediated or not; in IgE-mediated allergy, dendritic cells present food antigen to Th2 cells, which drive sensitization, and IL-4 variants exaggerate the Th2 response.

When it fails

Salmonella targets and destroys M cells, and in typhoid, Peyer patch necrosis perforates the distal ileum, most often in the third week of fever. Shigella, Listeria, and Yersinia enter through M cells, Yersinia using them as its main route, HIV spreads through them to mucosal T cells, and prions are taken up by M cells and replicate in Peyer patches. Peyer patches enlarged by new infections in infancy can lead the bowel into intussusception.

Selective IgA deficiency, the most common primary immunodeficiency at about 1 in 700, is IgA below 7 mg/dL after age 4 with normal IgG and IgM; it is often silent but causes recurrent sinopulmonary infections and giardiasis, is linked most often with celiac disease and with autoimmune disease in 20–30%, and anti-IgA antibodies can cause anaphylaxis after blood products containing IgA.

Misdirected immunity underlies several conditions: in celiac disease, linked to HLA DQ2/8, gliadin activates T helper cells and B cells make tTG, DGP, and EMA antibodies; in IBD, Th1 predominates in Crohn disease, with NOD-2 mutations and defective Paneth cell defensins in ileal disease, and Th2 in ulcerative colitis; in autoimmune gastritis, autoantibodies against parietal cells reduce HCl and intrinsic factor; chronic H. pylori gastritis can cause gastric MALT lymphoma, treated by eradication; in eosinophilic esophagitis, Th2 cytokines such as IL-5 and IL-13 recruit 15 or more eosinophils per high-power field; and in Whipple disease, Tropheryma whipplei infects macrophages.

Pharmacology

5-ASA drugs, mesalazine and sulfasalazine, act topically on the colonic mucosa, inhibiting COX, lipoxygenase, and NF-κB. Glucocorticoids such as budesonide and prednisone act through the cytoplasmic glucocorticoid receptor to reduce inflammatory gene transcription, and budesonide's high first pass limits systemic effects. Azathioprine becomes thioguanine nucleotides that block purine synthesis, and methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, both reducing lymphocyte proliferation.

Anti-TNF antibodies, infliximab and adalimumab, bind TNF-α. Vedolizumab blocks the α4β7 integrin from binding MAdCAM-1, a gut-selective block on lymphocytes entering the mucosa. Ustekinumab binds the p40 subunit of IL-12 and IL-23, reducing Th1 and Th17 responses, and JAK inhibitors such as tofacitinib and upadacitinib block signaling by many cytokines.

The enteric nervous system: plexuses, pacemakers, and motility

The gut has its own nervous system. The submucosal plexus governs secretion and blood flow, and the myenteric plexus, running from the upper esophagus to the internal anal sphincter, governs motility, while ICC generate the slow waves on which contractions ride. Parasympathetic fibers in the vagus and pelvic nerves are mostly excitatory and sympathetic fibers from T8–L2 inhibitory. Here, we review the structure of the ENS, the peristaltic reflex and regional motility patterns, the disorders that follow missing or damaged neurons, and the drugs that speed or slow the gut.

Key points

  • The myenteric plexus controls motility and the submucosal plexus secretion and blood flow; ICC set the rhythm.
  • Distension makes the gut contract above, through ACh, and relax below, through NO and VIP.
  • The fasting MMC sweeps the small intestine every 90–120 minutes.
  • Hirschsprung disease is failed neural crest migration, with no ganglion cells in the rectosigmoid in about 80%.
  • Prucalopride and metoclopramide stimulate 5-HT4; loperamide acts on μ receptors to slow transit.

Structure

The submucosal (Meissner) plexus, present in the small and large intestine but not the stomach or esophagus, senses the mucosa and controls secretion and blood flow. The myenteric (Auerbach) plexus lies between the circular and longitudinal muscle as a continuous network from the upper esophagus to the internal anal sphincter, controls motility, and about 30% of its neurons are sensory. ICC, between nerve terminals and smooth muscle, generate slow waves, but contraction needs a spike on top of the slow wave. Parasympathetic fibers, through the vagus to the esophagus, stomach, pancreas, and upper colon and through pelvic nerves to the lower colon, rectum, and anus, are mostly excitatory, with ACh exciting and NO inhibiting, while sympathetic fibers from T8–L2 inhibit through prevertebral ganglia. ENS neurons come from neural crest cells.

Motility patterns

By the law of the intestine, distension is sensed by mechanoreceptors, and the gut contracts above and relaxes below. Mucosal 5-HT activates afferent nerves through 5-HT3 receptors and enhances ACh release through 5-HT4 receptors. In the esophagus, swallowing starts vagal inhibition followed by a contraction wave as the LES relaxes. The stomach relaxes to receive food through inhibitory vagal fibers, then mixes it under NANC, sympathetic, and vagal control, contracting 3–8 times a minute. The small intestine mixes its contents, and between meals the MMC, quiet for about 70 minutes, intermittent for 10–20, then propulsive for 5, recurs every 90–120 minutes; a meal reaches the colon in 90–120 minutes. The colon mixes, segments, and pushes stool by mass movements over 8–15 hours.

After a meal, gastric stretch increases colonic motility through the gastrocolic reflex, with possible mediators CCK, serotonin, neurotensin, and gastrin. Motilin, like gastrin, is released directly by neural stimulation. In defecation, a mass movement fills the rectum, the internal sphincter relaxes involuntarily, the external sphincter and puborectalis relax voluntarily through the pudendal nerves, and Valsalva helps expel stool.

When it fails

In Hirschsprung disease, neural crest cells fail to reach the distal colon, so both plexuses lack ganglion cells, in the rectosigmoid in about 80%; it affects 1 in 5000 births, 4 boys for each girl, is linked to RET mutations, delays meconium, and is diagnosed by rectal suction biopsy with calretinin staining. In achalasia, myenteric ganglion cells are lost, so the LES cannot relax. In chronic Chagas disease, about 30% develop heart disease or megaesophagus and megacolon. Gastroparesis delays gastric emptying without obstruction, causing nausea, vomiting, and early satiety; it is most often idiopathic, then diabetic, in 25% and worse in type 1, postsurgical, or postinfectious, and is confirmed by 4-hour scintigraphy.

In diffuse esophageal spasm, the esophagus contracts instead of relaxing when distended. In ileus, slow waves continue without spikes under continuous inhibitory input. Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction affects sick inpatients through autonomic dysregulation, and CIPO follows disorders of enteric nerves, smooth muscle, or ICC. In infectious diarrhea, rapid, powerful peristalsis leaves no time to absorb water.

Pharmacology

Metoclopramide blocks D2 receptors and stimulates 5-HT4, speeding gastric emptying, and with erythromycin, a motilin agonist, is first line in gastroparesis. Prucalopride, a selective 5-HT4 agonist, increases colonic peristalsis in laxative-refractory constipation, neostigmine blocks AChE and is the first-line drug for acute colonic pseudo-obstruction, and bisacodyl stimulates enteric neurons.

Loperamide acts on μ-opioid receptors on enteric neurons, reducing ACh release and slowing transit with little effect on the brain. Octreotide, through somatostatin receptors, inhibits secretion of hormones including VIP and serotonin, reducing secretion and motility, and hyoscine butylbromide blocks M3 receptors on smooth muscle as an antispasmodic.

Acid-suppressing and protective drugs: from neutralizing acid to blocking the proton pump

Drugs for acid-related disease either lower gastric acid or strengthen the mucosal barrier against it. Antacids neutralize acid already secreted, H2R antagonists block the histamine signal to the parietal cell, and PPIs block the final step of secretion, whereas misoprostol, sucralfate, and bismuth protect the mucosa. Here, we review how each class works, where it is used, and the properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions that follow from its mechanism.

Key points

  • PPIs are prodrugs activated by acid in parietal cell canaliculi that irreversibly bind active pumps, so they work best 30–60 minutes before the first meal.
  • H2R antagonists are reversible and weaker, with tolerance within weeks, and cimetidine inhibits several CYP enzymes and has antiandrogen effects.
  • Long-term PPI use is associated with hypomagnesemia, B12 and iron deficiency, C. difficile infection, pneumonia, and fractures.
  • Misoprostol prevents NSAID ulcers but is contraindicated in pregnancy, and sucralfate needs acid, so it works poorly with acid suppression.
  • Antacids, sucralfate, and bismuth bind other drugs in the gut, so doses are separated.

Suppressing acid

Antacids, among them calcium carbonate, sodium bicarbonate, magnesium salts, and aluminum hydroxide, neutralize HCl in the gastric lumen. They raise gastric pH within minutes, but the effect lasts only 1–2 hours, so their role is relief of mild, intermittent heartburn and acid symptoms in GERD. Most are not absorbed, apart from sodium bicarbonate and some of the calcium. Each salt carries its own hazards: magnesium causes diarrhea, aluminum causes constipation, hypophosphatemia, osteomalacia, and neurotoxicity, calcium causes constipation and hypercalcemia, and sodium bicarbonate causes bloating and metabolic alkalosis. Aluminum and magnesium accumulate in renal failure, where magnesium also causes hypermagnesemia, and sodium bicarbonate is unsuitable in heart failure, edema, and low-sodium diets. Antacids chelate tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, iron, and levothyroxine, so these are taken at a different time, and the higher gastric pH lowers absorption of drugs that need acid.

H2R antagonists, such as famotidine and cimetidine, reversibly block histamine at H2 receptors on parietal cells. Less cAMP is formed, H+/K+ ATPase activity falls, and acid secretion decreases, especially basal and nocturnal secretion. They are used for GERD, including a bedtime dose for nighttime symptoms, for PUD, and as second-line stress ulcer prophylaxis. Because the block is reversible, they are weaker and shorter acting than PPIs, and tolerance develops within weeks. They are renally cleared, so the dose is lowered in renal impairment. Apart from hypersensitivity they have no absolute contraindication, but older patients and those with renal or hepatic impairment are prone to confusion and delirium, and headache and GI upset are common. Cimetidine also causes gynecomastia, impotence, and galactorrhea, and it inhibits CYP1A2, CYP2C9, and CYP2D6, raising levels of drugs such as warfarin, phenytoin, and theophylline; famotidine has few interactions.

PPIs, such as omeprazole and lansoprazole, are the most potent acid suppressants. They are prodrugs, given in enteric-coated forms, that are activated by acid in the parietal cell canaliculi and then bind the H+/K+ ATPase irreversibly, profoundly reducing both basal and stimulated acid. Because they bind only active pumps, they work best 30–60 minutes before a meal, and their effect outlasts a short plasma half-life, since new pumps must be made. They are metabolized mainly by CYP2C19. Their uses are broad: esophagitis, GERD (a standard dose before the first meal for 8 weeks), PUD, including IV use for bleeding ulcers, prevention of NSAID ulcers, gastrinoma at high doses, H. pylori regimens, and stress ulcer prophylaxis. Hypersensitivity is the only contraindication, with caution in severe liver disease. Long-term use is associated with hypomagnesemia, B12 and iron deficiency, C. difficile infection, pneumonia, and fractures, and stopping can cause rebound acid hypersecretion. Omeprazole inhibits CYP2C19, which activates clopidogrel; less acid lowers absorption of iron and of some antifungals and antivirals; and sucralfate, which needs acid, works poorly alongside them.

Protecting the mucosa

Misoprostol is a PGE1 analog that acts on EP3 receptors on parietal cells, decreasing acid while increasing mucus and bicarbonate. Its use is gastroprotection in people who must continue NSAIDs. It is a prodrug de-esterified to active misoprostol acid, and its short action means several doses a day. It causes uterine contractions and abortion, so it is contraindicated in pregnancy, and its common adverse effects are diarrhea and abdominal cramps, which magnesium antacids worsen, along with uterine bleeding.

Sucralfate polymerizes at pH under 4 into a paste that binds proteins in the ulcer base, forming a barrier against acid, pepsin, and bile while local PG and bicarbonate rise. It is used for duodenal ulcers and mucosal protection. It is barely absorbed, and because it needs acid to activate, it works poorly with PPIs, H2R antagonists, and antacids. Aluminum accumulates in end-stage renal disease, which is a contraindication, as is impaired swallowing. Adverse effects are constipation, aluminum toxicity in renal failure, hypophosphatemia, and bezoars. It binds digoxin, levothyroxine, fluoroquinolones, furosemide, and warfarin, so these are given at least 2 hours apart.

Bismuth salts, bismuth subcitrate and subsalicylate, coat the ulcer base and kill H. pylori, while increasing PG and bicarbonate. Bismuth quadruple therapy for H. pylori uses them, and subsalicylate also treats traveler diarrhea. Bismuth itself is barely absorbed, but subsalicylate releases salicylate, which is, so subsalicylate is avoided in children and teenagers with viral illness because of Reye syndrome, in bleeding disorders, and with salicylate allergy; it also adds to anticoagulants and methotrexate. Bismuth turns the stools and tongue black, causes neurotoxicity with prolonged excess, and lowers tetracycline absorption.

PPIs block the final step of acid secretion, so they suppress acid more than H2R antagonists, which block only the histamine signal.

Relationships to other conditions

GERD and peptic ulcer disease. Both are treated by suppressing acid, usually with a PPI. GERD adds a bedtime H2R antagonist for nighttime symptoms, whereas PUD also needs its cause removed: H. pylori is eradicated, and NSAIDs are stopped or covered with misoprostol. A gastrinoma needs a high-dose PPI.

Antibacterials in digestive disease: H. pylori, abdominal sepsis, and C. difficile

Antibacterials appear across the GI pages, from H. pylori regimens to abdominal sepsis and the treatment and causation of C. difficile infection. Their usefulness depends on spectrum and where they act, and their hazards include CYP interactions, chelation, QT prolongation, and disruption of the colonic flora. Here, we review each antibacterial named on these pages by target, mechanism, use, key properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • H. pylori triple therapy combines a PPI, amoxicillin, and clarithromycin, with metronidazole in place of amoxicillin for penicillin allergy; clarithromycin resistance is the main reason it fails.
  • Clarithromycin strongly inhibits CYP3A4, and metronidazole causes a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol and raises the INR on warfarin.
  • Tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones are chelated by divalent cations, and both are avoided in pregnancy and children.
  • Oral vancomycin and fidaxomicin treat C. difficile because they act in the gut lumen, and rifaximin is barely absorbed.

H. pylori regimens

Amoxicillin binds penicillin-binding proteins, blocking peptidoglycan cross-linking and lysing the bacterial wall. It is part of H. pylori triple therapy and of the alternative regimens, and with clavulanate, which inhibits β-lactamase and widens its cover, it treats outpatient diverticulitis. It is renally cleared. In penicillin allergy, metronidazole replaces it in triple therapy. It causes diarrhea, rash, particularly with EBV infection, anaphylaxis, and C. difficile infection, and clavulanate adds cholestatic hepatitis. Rash is more likely with allopurinol, and probenecid raises its levels.

Clarithromycin binds the 23S rRNA of the 50S ribosomal subunit and blocks translocation, stopping protein synthesis. It is the macrolide of triple therapy, and resistance to it is the main reason triple therapy fails. It is a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor that raises levels of statins, causing myopathy, as well as calcineurin inhibitors, carbamazepine, and warfarin, so it is contraindicated with simvastatin, lovastatin, and colchicine and in QT prolongation. It causes GI upset, a metallic taste, QT prolongation, and cholestatic hepatitis.

Metronidazole is reduced inside anaerobes to free radicals that damage DNA, killing anaerobic bacteria and protozoa. It replaces amoxicillin in triple therapy for penicillin allergy and is part of bismuth quadruple therapy; it treats diverticulitis with a fluoroquinolone and amebic abscess before a luminal agent, and it is used for C. difficile only when fidaxomicin and oral vancomycin are unavailable. It is well absorbed by mouth, crosses the BBB, and is metabolized in the liver. It causes a metallic taste, nausea, peripheral neuropathy, and confusion. Alcohol is avoided during treatment and for 3 days after because of a disulfiram-like reaction, it is avoided in the first trimester, and it inhibits warfarin metabolism, raising the INR.

Fluoroquinolones such as levofloxacin inhibit DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, killing bacteria by blocking DNA replication. Levofloxacin-based regimens treat H. pylori, and fluoroquinolones treat diverticulitis with metronidazole and tropical sprue. They have excellent oral bioavailability and are renally cleared. Cartilage damage makes them unsuitable in pregnancy and children, and they are avoided in myasthenia gravis and QT prolongation. Their adverse effects include tendinitis and tendon rupture, worse with age and steroids, QT prolongation, dysglycemia, peripheral neuropathy, aortic aneurysm, and C. difficile infection. Antacids, iron, calcium, and sucralfate chelate them, and QT prolongation is additive with other drugs.

Tetracyclines, tetracycline and doxycycline, bind the 30S ribosomal subunit and block attachment of aminoacyl-tRNA, so they are bacteriostatic. Tetracycline is part of bismuth quadruple therapy, both treat tropical sprue, and doxycycline treats cholera. Divalent and trivalent cations chelate them, so antacids, dairy, iron, and bismuth are taken apart, and doxycycline is cleared mainly in feces, which makes it safe in renal failure. Because they discolor teeth and impair bone growth, they are avoided in pregnancy and in children under 8; they also cause photosensitivity, GI upset, and pill esophagitis.

Rifabutin inhibits bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase, blocking transcription, and is used in a rescue H. pylori regimen with a PPI and amoxicillin. It induces CYP3A4, though less than rifampin, lowering levels of substrates such as oral contraceptives. It is avoided with rifamycin hypersensitivity and causes orange body fluids, neutropenia, and uveitis.

Abdominal infections

Cephalosporins, such as ceftriaxone and other third-generation drugs, bind penicillin-binding proteins and are bactericidal. They treat SBP with albumin, cholangitis and cholecystitis, and, as ceftriaxone, the induction phase of Whipple disease. Ceftriaxone is cleared in bile as well as urine and causes biliary sludge; it is avoided in neonates with hyperbilirubinemia, and it precipitates with IV calcium in neonates. Severe β-lactam allergy is a contraindication, and diarrhea, C. difficile infection, and hypersensitivity are the main adverse effects.

Penicillin G acts in the same way and is the alternative to ceftriaxone for inducing remission in Whipple disease. It is given IV or IM, is renally cleared, and is destroyed by β-lactamase; probenecid slows its excretion. Penicillin allergy rules it out, and it can cause hypersensitivity, interstitial nephritis, and hemolytic anemia.

Broad-spectrum β-lactams, piperacillin-tazobactam and the carbapenems, also bind penicillin-binding proteins but kill gram-negative bacteria and anaerobes as well. Piperacillin-tazobactam treats cholangitis, and a carbapenem treats infected pancreatic necrosis. Tazobactam inhibits β-lactamase, and carbapenems resist most β-lactamases. Both are avoided in β-lactam allergy and can cause C. difficile infection and hypersensitivity; carbapenems also lower the seizure threshold and sharply lower valproate levels.

TMP-SMX blocks two steps of bacterial folate synthesis, dihydropteroate synthase and dihydrofolate reductase, and the combination is bactericidal. It maintains remission in Whipple disease for at least 12 months, helped by its entry across the BBB, and treats tropical sprue. It is renally cleared. Sulfa allergy, G6PD deficiency, late pregnancy, and infants under 2 months are contraindications, and it can cause rash including SJS, hyperkalemia, bone marrow suppression, hemolysis in G6PD deficiency, and kernicterus in neonates. It raises warfarin and phenytoin levels, causes hyperkalemia with ACE inhibitors and spironolactone, and adds to the marrow toxicity of methotrexate.

C. difficile and the gut lumen

Oral vancomycin binds D-Ala-D-Ala on cell wall precursors and kills gram-positive bacteria. Given by mouth for 10 days, it treats C. difficile infection, because it is not absorbed and acts only in the gut lumen, whereas IV vancomycin does not reach the colon. Its only contraindication is hypersensitivity, and by mouth it causes few adverse effects, whereas IV use causes nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, and the vancomycin flushing reaction, with additive nephrotoxicity alongside aminoglycosides.

Fidaxomicin inhibits the RNA polymerase of C. difficile with a narrow spectrum that spares the colonic flora. It is first line for C. difficile infection and for recurrence, it is barely absorbed, and its narrow spectrum lowers recurrence. Apart from hypersensitivity it has no contraindications, and it causes nausea and abdominal pain.

Rifaximin inhibits RNA polymerase of bacteria in the gut lumen, reducing ammonia- and gas-producing bacteria. It is added to lactulose for hepatic encephalopathy and treats SIBO for 2 weeks and IBS when other drugs fail. Under 1% is absorbed, so its effects stay in the gut, its adverse effects are similar to placebo, and it has few interactions. Rifamycin hypersensitivity is the contraindication.

Bezlotoxumab is a monoclonal antibody that binds and neutralizes C. difficile toxin B, reducing recurrence. It is given as a single IV dose alongside ABX for recurrent infection. It can cause nausea, fever, and worsening heart failure, so it is used with caution in heart failure.

Broad ABX, especially clindamycin, cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and penicillins, are the main cause of C. difficile infection.

Relationships to other conditions

Peptic ulcer disease. H. pylori regimens pair a PPI with two or three antibacterials. First-line triple therapy uses amoxicillin and clarithromycin, metronidazole replaces amoxicillin in penicillin allergy, and alternatives use levofloxacin, bismuth with metronidazole and tetracycline, or rifabutin; cure is confirmed by urea breath test 4–6 weeks later.

C. difficile infection and abdominal sepsis. The broad agents that treat abdominal sepsis, including cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and penicillins, are also the main causes of C. difficile infection, which is treated with drugs that stay in the gut lumen: fidaxomicin and oral vancomycin.

Antifungals, antivirals, and antiparasitics for esophageal and hepatic infection

Esophageal infections in immunocompromised patients and parasitic infections of the liver each have specific drug treatments. Fluconazole, acyclovir, and ganciclovir treat Candida, HSV, and CMV, whereas albendazole, praziquantel, and paromomycin treat hydatid cysts, schistosomiasis, and amebae in the gut lumen. Here, we review how each works, its uses, and its key properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Candida esophagitis is always treated with systemic fluconazole for 14–21 days, never topically.
  • Fluconazole inhibits CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4, raising warfarin, phenytoin, and statin levels.
  • Acyclovir causes crystal nephropathy, prevented by hydration, and ganciclovir causes bone marrow suppression.
  • Metronidazole kills amebae in tissue but not cysts in the lumen, so paromomycin follows it for amebic abscess.

Esophageal infections

Fluconazole inhibits fungal lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51), blocking ergosterol synthesis, and is fungistatic against Candida. Candida esophagitis is treated with oral fluconazole for 14–21 days, always systemically rather than topically. It is well absorbed, renally cleared, and penetrates tissue well. It causes GI upset, hepatotoxicity, and QT prolongation, so it is avoided in QT prolongation, and it inhibits CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4, raising warfarin, phenytoin, and statin levels.

Acyclovir is phosphorylated by viral thymidine kinase and then terminates viral DNA chains, selectively blocking HSV replication. It treats HSV esophagitis and is given IV as soon as HSV hepatitis is suspected. Oral bioavailability is low, and it is renally cleared, so the dose is lowered in renal impairment, though hypersensitivity is the only contraindication. It causes crystal nephropathy, prevented by hydration, and neurotoxicity in renal failure, and its nephrotoxicity adds to that of other kidney-toxic drugs.

Ganciclovir is phosphorylated by the CMV kinase UL97 and then inhibits viral DNA polymerase, suppressing CMV. It treats CMV esophagitis and hepatitis in immunocompromised patients. It is given IV and renally cleared. Bone marrow suppression, especially neutropenia, is its main adverse effect, so severe neutropenia or thrombocytopenia is a contraindication; it is also nephrotoxic and teratogenic, and its marrow suppression adds to that of zidovudine and mycophenolate.

Liver parasites

Albendazole binds parasite β-tubulin, blocking microtubules and glucose uptake, so the parasite dies and hydatid cysts shrink. It treats hydatid cyst, with PAIR or surgery for larger cysts. It is better absorbed with a fatty meal, and its active metabolite is albendazole sulfoxide, which dexamethasone and praziquantel raise. It is avoided in pregnancy and causes hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression, and alopecia.

Praziquantel increases calcium entry into the worm through TRPM channels, causing spastic paralysis and damage to the worm surface, and it treats schistosomiasis. It has high first-pass metabolism by CYP3A4, so rifampin, carbamazepine, and phenytoin lower its levels and cimetidine raises them; it is contraindicated with rifampin and in ocular cysticercosis. It causes abdominal pain, headache, and dizziness.

Paromomycin is an aminoglycoside that binds the 30S ribosomal subunit of amebae in the gut lumen, clearing intestinal cysts. It is the luminal agent given after metronidazole for amebic abscess. It is barely absorbed by mouth, so it acts in the lumen, but absorption through ulcerated bowel can cause nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity; it otherwise causes GI upset and is avoided in bowel obstruction.

Metronidazole kills amebae in tissue but not cysts in the lumen, so a luminal agent follows it.

Relationships to other conditions

Infectious esophagitis. Infection follows impaired cell-mediated immunity and is due to Candida in 88%, HSV in 10%, and CMV in 2%, so the three drugs map onto the three organisms: fluconazole, acyclovir, and ganciclovir.

Abscesses and parasites of the liver. Pyogenic abscess needs drainage and ABX, whereas amebic abscess is treated medically with metronidazole and then a luminal agent, with drainage only in select cases. Hydatid cyst needs albendazole with PAIR or surgery and care to avoid spillage, which can cause anaphylaxis.

Antivirals for viral hepatitis and drugs acting on the bile salt pool

Chronic hepatitis B is suppressed by nucleotide analogs or peginterferon-α, whereas hepatitis C is cured by combinations of direct-acting antivirals, and ribavirin treats chronic hepatitis E. The bile salt pool is the target of cholestyramine, ursodeoxycholic acid, and obeticholic acid in cholestatic disease. Here, we review the targets, mechanisms, uses, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions of these drugs.

Key points

  • HBV cccDNA persists, so nucleotide analogs suppress rather than cure HBV and are usually taken long term; stopping them can cause severe flares.
  • Sofosbuvir-based combinations cure over 95% of HCV infections, but sofosbuvir with amiodarone causes symptomatic bradycardia.
  • NS3/4A protease inhibitors are avoided in decompensated cirrhosis and strongly inhibit CYP3A4.
  • Ursodeoxycholic acid is first line for PBC, and obeticholic acid is added when it fails; cholestyramine binds other drugs, including ursodeoxycholic acid.
  • Clearing HCV can reactivate HBV, so HBV is screened for first.

Hepatitis B and D

Nucleotide analogs, tenofovir and entecavir, compete with natural nucleotides for HBV reverse transcriptase and block reverse transcription of pregenomic RNA, suppressing HBV DNA. Because cccDNA persists in the hepatocyte, treatment is usually long term. They treat chronic HBV and severe acute HBV, and HDV is treated by treating the HBV. They have a high genetic barrier to resistance and are renally cleared, so the dose follows kidney function. Tenofovir disoproxil can cause kidney injury, low phosphate, and bone loss, so creatinine and phosphate are monitored, and its nephrotoxicity adds to that of other drugs. Stopping either drug can cause a severe hepatitis flare.

Peginterferon-α activates JAK-STAT signaling through interferon receptors, inducing antiviral proteins and boosting the immune response over a finite course. It treats chronic HBV in selected patients and HDV. Pegylation slows its clearance, allowing weekly injections. It is contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis, autoimmune disease, uncontrolled depression or thyroid disease, and pregnancy, and it causes flu-like symptoms, fatigue, depression, cytopenias, thyroid disease, and autoimmune reactions, with additive marrow suppression alongside other myelotoxic drugs.

Hepatitis C and E

NS3/4A protease inhibitors, such as glecaprevir and grazoprevir, block cleavage of the HCV polyprotein and are used for chronic HCV in fixed combinations. They are metabolized by CYP3A4 and carried by P-gp. As strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, they raise calcineurin inhibitor levels, and inducers such as rifampin and carbamazepine lower their own levels. They are contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis (Child-Pugh B or C), and they cause headache and fatigue and can reactivate HBV in coinfection.

NS5A inhibitors, ledipasvir, velpatasvir, and pibrentasvir, bind the NS5A protein and impair RNA replication and virion assembly. They are combined with an NS5B or NS3/4A inhibitor for chronic HCV. Ledipasvir and velpatasvir need acid to dissolve, so acid suppression lowers their absorption. Their adverse effects are headache and fatigue, and they too can reactivate HBV.

Sofosbuvir, an NS5B inhibitor, is a nucleotide prodrug built into HCV RNA by the NS5B polymerase, where it ends the chain. With a partner drug it cures over 95% of infections, and it is the backbone of most regimens, given for 8–12 weeks. It works across genotypes with a high barrier to resistance. It causes fatigue and headache, but with amiodarone it causes symptomatic bradycardia, so the combination is contraindicated, and P-gp inducers such as rifampin and carbamazepine lower its levels.

Ribavirin is a guanosine analog that depletes GTP and causes mutations in viral RNA. It treats chronic HEV and is added to some HCV regimens. It is renally cleared and accumulates in red cells, causing hemolytic anemia, which zidovudine worsens; it is also teratogenic and causes cough. It is contraindicated in pregnancy in either partner, severe anemia, and hemoglobinopathies.

Bile salts

Cholestyramine is a bile acid sequestrant, a non-absorbed resin that binds bile salts in the intestine and blocks their reabsorption, so the liver turns more cholesterol into bile salts. It relieves pruritus in PBC and PSC and treats bile acid diarrhea, and it lowers LDL. It is contraindicated in complete biliary obstruction and severe hypertriglyceridemia, and it causes constipation, bloating, raised triglycerides, and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency. It binds other drugs, including ursodeoxycholic acid, warfarin, digoxin, and levothyroxine, which are given 1 hour before or 4–6 hours after it.

Ursodeoxycholic acid is a hydrophilic bile salt that joins the enterohepatic circulation and displaces toxic hydrophobic bile salts, increasing bile flow and lowering biliary cholesterol saturation. Bile salt toxicity falls, LFTs improve, PBC progresses more slowly, and small cholesterol stones dissolve. It is first line for PBC and dissolves small cholesterol gallstones in people unfit for surgery, though fewer than half dissolve. It is contraindicated in obstructive cholestasis, causes diarrhea, and is bound by cholestyramine and aluminum antacids.

Obeticholic acid is a bile salt analog that activates the farnesoid X receptor, suppressing CYP7A1 and so bile salt synthesis and toxicity. It is added to ursodeoxycholic acid in PBC that has not responded. It joins the enterohepatic circulation and is bound by bile acid sequestrants. It is contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis, cirrhosis with portal hypertension, and complete biliary obstruction, and it causes pruritus, fatigue, lower HDL, and hepatic decompensation in advanced cirrhosis.

HBV is screened for before HCV treatment, because clearing HCV can reactivate HBV.

Antiemetics: blocking the inputs to vomiting

Vomiting is coordinated in the brainstem from inputs that include the chemoreceptor trigger zone, vagal afferents from the gut, and the vestibular system, and each antiemetic blocks one or more of them. D2, 5-HT3, and NK1 antagonists act on the trigger zone and gut, whereas H1 antagonists and scopolamine act on vestibular input. Here, we review their targets, uses, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • 5-HT3 antagonists treat acute chemotherapy-induced, radiation-induced, and postoperative vomiting, and NK1 antagonists prevent delayed vomiting on days 2–5.
  • D2 antagonists cause EPS and tardive dyskinesia, so metoclopramide is limited to 12 weeks.
  • First-generation antihistamines and scopolamine prevent motion sickness, whereas second-generation antihistamines do not.
  • NK1 antagonists inhibit CYP3A4 and raise dexamethasone levels.

The CTZ and the gut

D2 antagonists, metoclopramide and prochlorperazine, block D2 receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone, and metoclopramide also blocks D2 and stimulates 5-HT4 receptors in the gut, releasing ACh and speeding gastric emptying. They treat nausea and vomiting, and metoclopramide treats gastroparesis. Metoclopramide crosses the BBB and is renally cleared, whereas prochlorperazine has high first-pass metabolism. They are contraindicated in Parkinson disease, seizures, pheochromocytoma, and dementia-related psychosis in older adults, and metoclopramide also in GI obstruction, perforation, or bleeding. Their adverse effects are acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia, which limits metoclopramide to 12 weeks, hyperprolactinemia, NMS, and sedation. EPS add up with other D2 blockers, sedation adds up with other CNS depressants, and both drugs oppose levodopa.

5-HT3 antagonists, ondansetron and granisetron, block serotonin at ligand-gated 5-HT3 channels on vagal afferents and in the trigger zone. They treat acute chemotherapy-induced, radiation-induced, and postoperative vomiting. They are metabolized in the liver by CYP1A2, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 and come in oral, IV, and dissolving forms. They cause headache, constipation, and QT prolongation, so they are avoided in congenital long QT syndrome, and with apomorphine they cause profound hypotension. Other serotonergic drugs can cause serotonin syndrome with them, and QT prolongation is additive.

NK1 antagonists, aprepitant and netupitant, block substance P at NK1 receptors in the area postrema and NTS, preventing delayed chemotherapy-induced vomiting on days 2–5. They are used for highly emetogenic chemotherapy with a 5-HT3 antagonist and dexamethasone. They are contraindicated in severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C) and cause fatigue, hiccups, constipation, and, rarely, SJS.

The vestibular system

H1 antagonists, promethazine, dimenhydrinate, and diphenhydramine, block histamine and ACh in the brain, reducing vestibular input to vomiting. They treat motion sickness, vertigo, and general nausea. They are first-generation drugs that cross the BBB; second-generation antihistamines do not and do not prevent motion sickness. Promethazine is contraindicated in children under 2 because of fatal respiratory depression, and all are avoided in angle-closure glaucoma and urinary retention. They cause sedation and anticholinergic effects such as dry mouth, urinary retention, and confusion in older adults, and promethazine causes EPS and tissue injury if extravasated. Sedation adds up with opioids, benzodiazepines, and alcohol, and anticholinergic effects add up with other drugs.

Antimuscarinics, represented by scopolamine, block ACh at muscarinic receptors, inhibiting input to the vestibular nuclei and preventing motion sickness. Scopolamine is given as a transdermal patch behind the ear before travel and lasts about 3 days. It is contraindicated in angle-closure glaucoma and belladonna alkaloid allergy. It causes dry mouth, blurred vision, confusion, urinary retention, and dilated pupils, often from touching the patch and then the eye, and its anticholinergic effects add to those of TCAs and H1 antagonists.

. Each class blocks one input: the trigger zone in the area postrema (D2, 5-HT3, and NK1 antagonists), vagal afferents from the gut (5-HT3 antagonists), and the vestibular system (H1 antagonists and scopolamine).

Motility drugs: speeding and slowing the gut through the enteric nervous system

Most drugs that change gut motility act on the enteric motor neuron or the smooth muscle it drives, either enhancing acetylcholine release to speed transit or reducing it to slow transit. Prokinetics, neostigmine, and stimulant laxatives speed the gut, whereas loperamide, somatostatin analogs, and antispasmodics slow it. Here, we review their targets, uses, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Metoclopramide and erythromycin are first-line drugs for gastroparesis; metoclopramide is limited to 12 weeks, and erythromycin loses effect within weeks.
  • Neostigmine is the first-line drug for acute colonic pseudo-obstruction and is given with cardiac monitoring and atropine at hand.
  • Loperamide is avoided with bloody diarrhea, fever, C. difficile, HUS, toxic megacolon, and acute ulcerative colitis.
  • Octreotide causes gallstones and dysglycemia, and antispasmodics are avoided in angle-closure glaucoma and urinary retention.

Speeding the gut

Metoclopramide blocks D2 receptors in the gut and brain while stimulating 5-HT4 receptors in the gut, enhancing ACh release and speeding gastric emptying. It is first line for gastroparesis with erythromycin, and it is also an antiemetic. It crosses the BBB and is renally cleared. It is contraindicated in mechanical obstruction, perforation, or bleeding, Parkinson disease, seizures, and pheochromocytoma. Like other D2 antagonists it causes EPS and tardive dyskinesia, so use is limited to 12 weeks, and hyperprolactinemia. It opposes levodopa and speeds absorption of other drugs.

Erythromycin, a macrolide antibiotic, is also a motilin agonist on GI smooth muscle that triggers strong gastric contractions, speeding emptying. It is first line for gastroparesis with metoclopramide. Tachyphylaxis develops within weeks. As a CYP3A4 substrate and inhibitor it raises statin, carbamazepine, and calcineurin inhibitor levels, so it is avoided with simvastatin or lovastatin and in QT prolongation. It causes abdominal cramps, nausea, QT prolongation, and pyloric stenosis in newborns.

Prucalopride selectively stimulates 5-HT4 receptors on enteric neurons, enhancing ACh release and colonic peristalsis. It treats chronic constipation not relieved by laxatives. Its selectivity spares the cardiac effects of older 5-HT4 agonists, and it is renally cleared. It is contraindicated in bowel obstruction or perforation, severe IBD, and toxic megacolon, and it causes headache, abdominal pain, nausea, and diarrhea.

Laxatives and antidiarrheals: drugs for constipation, IBS, and dehydration

Constipation is treated by drawing water into the bowel, adding bulk, or stimulating secretion, and IBS with diarrhea by drugs that slow transit and visceral pain, while oral rehydration restores volume lost to diarrhea. Each approach has characteristic hazards, from electrolyte loss to ischemic colitis and pancreatitis. Here, we review the drugs named on these pages by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • PEG is the first drug for constipation in IBS, and lactulose also treats hepatic encephalopathy by trapping ammonia as NH4+.
  • Lubiprostone and the GC-C agonists increase intestinal secretion and act in the lumen with little absorption.
  • Eluxadoline causes pancreatitis and is contraindicated after cholecystectomy, and alosetron is reserved for women with severe IBS-D because of ischemic colitis.
  • ORS works because SGLT1 still carries sodium and glucose in secretory diarrhea.

Constipation

Osmotic laxatives, PEG, lactulose, and magnesium citrate, retain water in the bowel lumen osmotically, softening stools and making them more frequent; colonic bacteria also ferment lactulose to acids that trap ammonia as NH4+, so less ammonia is absorbed. PEG is the first drug for constipation in IBS and is used for bowel preparation, and lactulose treats hepatic encephalopathy, taking 1–2 days to work and titrated to 2–3 soft stools a day. They are poorly absorbed. They are contraindicated in bowel obstruction or perforation, and lactulose in galactosemia and magnesium in renal failure, where it causes hypermagnesemia. They cause bloating, flatulence, cramps, chronic osmotic diarrhea, dehydration, and electrolyte loss, and magnesium chelates tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones.

Bulk-forming laxatives such as psyllium absorb water, adding bulk and softening stool. Soluble fiber of this kind treats the global symptoms of IBS, whereas insoluble fiber does not, and fiber helps in diverticular disease. They are not absorbed, work over days, and need fluid, since taken without water they can obstruct. They are contraindicated in bowel obstruction, dysphagia, and esophageal stricture, cause bloating and flatulence, and slow absorption of other drugs.

Lubiprostone activates ClC-2 chloride channels on enterocytes, increasing chloride and water secretion, softening stools, and speeding transit. It treats IBS with constipation after PEG and chronic constipation. It is barely absorbed and is taken with food to lessen nausea. It is contraindicated in mechanical obstruction and causes nausea, diarrhea, and dyspnea.

GC-C agonists, linaclotide and plecanitide, raise cGMP in enterocytes, opening CFTR to secrete chloride, bicarbonate, and water; fluid in the lumen increases, transit speeds, and visceral pain lessens. They treat IBS with constipation after PEG and chronic constipation. They act in the gut lumen with almost no absorption and no CYP metabolism, so no interactions are known. Diarrhea, sometimes with dehydration, is the main adverse effect, so they are contraindicated in children under 2, as well as in mechanical obstruction.

Diarrhea

Eluxadoline is an agonist at μ- and κ-opioid receptors and an antagonist at δ-opioid receptors in the gut; it slows motility and secretion, while δ antagonism limits constipation, so stools become firmer and less frequent. It treats IBS with diarrhea after loperamide. Absorption is low, and the liver takes it up through OATP1B1, so cyclosporine and other OATP1B1 inhibitors raise its levels. It causes pancreatitis and sphincter of Oddi spasm, as well as constipation and nausea, so it is contraindicated without a gallbladder, with sphincter of Oddi problems, past pancreatitis, alcohol misuse, and severe liver impairment.

Alosetron blocks 5-HT3 receptors in the gut, slowing transit and visceral pain, so stools firm and urgency lessens. It is reserved for women with severe IBS-D that other treatment has not helped, because it causes constipation and ischemic colitis, which can be serious. It is metabolized by CYP2C9, CYP3A4, and CYP1A2, and fluvoxamine, a strong CYP1A2 inhibitor, is contraindicated with it. It is also contraindicated in constipation, obstruction, ischemic colitis, IBD, diverticulitis, impaired intestinal circulation, and severe liver disease.

Oral rehydration solution carries sodium and glucose into enterocytes together through SGLT1, and water follows, so it rehydrates even while secretory diarrhea continues, because SGLT1 still functions. It treats hypovolemia from diarrhea, including cholera. Shock, ileus, or persistent vomiting need IV fluids instead, and solution mixed too strong causes hypernatremia.

Loperamide, octreotide, and the antispasmodics are described with the motility drugs.

Relationships to other conditions

Drugs for inflammatory bowel disease and immune disorders of the gut and liver

Drugs for IBD climb from broad suppression of mucosal inflammation to targeted blockade of cytokines and lymphocyte trafficking. 5-ASA, glucocorticoids, thiopurines, methotrexate, and cyclosporine suppress inflammation broadly, whereas anti-TNF drugs, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, and JAK inhibitors act on defined targets. Here, we review these drugs and dapsone by target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • 5-ASA induces and maintains remission in mild to moderate UC, and sulfasalazine adds the sulfapyridine effects of oligospermia, folate deficiency, and hemolysis.
  • Glucocorticoids induce remission but are not used for maintenance, and budesonide's high first-pass metabolism limits systemic effects.
  • TPMT is tested before thiopurines, and allopurinol or febuxostat sharply raises thiopurine levels.
  • TB and HBV are screened for before biologics and JAK inhibitors, and live vaccines are avoided during them.

Broad suppression

5-ASA, as mesalazine or sulfasalazine, acts topically on the colonic mucosa, inhibiting COX, lipoxygenase, and NF-κB, so less PG and leukotriene is made. Colonic bacteria split sulfasalazine into 5-ASA and sulfapyridine. It induces and maintains remission in mild to moderate UC, and formulations release it in the ileum or colon, by mouth, suppository, or enema. It is contraindicated in salicylate allergy and severe renal impairment, and sulfasalazine also in sulfa allergy and G6PD deficiency. 5-ASA can cause interstitial nephritis, so creatinine is monitored, and it adds to thiopurine marrow toxicity; sulfasalazine also causes reversible oligospermia, folate deficiency, rash, agranulocytosis, and hemolysis, and lowers folate absorption.

Glucocorticoids, including prednisone, prednisolone, budesonide, and fluticasone, bind the cytoplasmic glucocorticoid receptor, and the complex enters the nucleus, increasing anti-inflammatory and decreasing NF-κB gene transcription, so cytokine production and lymphocyte activity fall. They induce remission in IBD flares but are not used for maintenance; they are given IV for toxic megacolon, treat autoimmune hepatitis and IgG4-related disease, and, as prednisolone, treat severe alcoholic hepatitis with a Maddrey score of 32 or more. Budesonide treats microscopic colitis, and swallowed fluticasone or budesonide treats EoE. Budesonide has high first-pass metabolism by CYP3A4, limiting systemic effects, so CYP3A4 inhibitors raise its levels, and prednisone is a prodrug that the liver activates to prednisolone. Active untreated infection is a contraindication. Systemic use causes cushingoid features, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, infection, adrenal suppression, so doses are tapered, mood change, cataracts, and glaucoma; swallowed topical steroids cause oral candidiasis, and NSAIDs add to the ulcer risk.

Thiopurines and methotrexate block lymphocyte proliferation. Azathioprine becomes 6-MP and then thioguanine nucleotides that block purine synthesis, and methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, so fewer T and B cells proliferate and steroids can be spared. They maintain remission in Crohn disease, and azathioprine treats autoimmune hepatitis. TPMT activity is tested before thiopurines, since low activity causes severe marrow toxicity, and xanthine oxidase breaks down 6-MP, so allopurinol and febuxostat sharply raise thiopurine levels. Methotrexate is given weekly and renally cleared, and TMP-SMX and NSAIDs raise its toxicity; it is contraindicated in pregnancy, liver disease, and alcohol misuse. All cause marrow suppression, infection, lymphoma, and nonmelanoma skin cancer; thiopurines also cause pancreatitis and hepatotoxicity, and methotrexate causes mucositis, hepatic fibrosis, pneumonitis, and teratogenicity.

Cyclosporine binds cyclophilin, blocking calcineurin and IL-2 transcription, so T cells are less activated. It rescues severe UC and toxic megacolon after IV steroids. It is a CYP3A4 and P-gp substrate and inhibitor with a narrow therapeutic index, so levels are monitored, and clarithromycin, azoles, and grapefruit raise them while rifampin and carbamazepine lower them. It is contraindicated in uncontrolled hypertension and renal impairment, and it causes nephrotoxicity, hypertension, hyperkalemia, gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, tremor, and seizures.

Dapsone inhibits neutrophil myeloperoxidase and migration, as well as bacterial dihydropteroate synthase, and rapidly relieves the itch of dermatitis herpetiformis in celiac disease alongside a gluten-free diet. It undergoes hepatic acetylation and CYP metabolism, and trimethoprim raises its levels. It causes hemolysis, especially in G6PD deficiency, which is a contraindication, as well as methemoglobinemia and agranulocytosis, and sulfa allergy needs caution.

Targeted

Anti-TNF drugs, infliximab, adalimumab, and certolizumab, bind soluble and transmembrane TNF-α so that it cannot reach its receptors, reducing cytokine signaling and leukocyte recruitment. They treat moderate to severe IBD, including fistulizing Crohn disease, and infliximab treats toxic megacolon after IV steroids. Infliximab is given IV and the others SC; antidrug antibodies lower response, less so with an immunomodulator. They are contraindicated in active infection including TB, moderate to severe heart failure, and demyelinating disease. They reactivate latent TB, which is screened for first, and HBV, and they cause serious infections, infusion reactions, lymphoma, drug-induced lupus, and psoriasiform rash. Live vaccines are avoided, and added immunosuppressants raise infection risk.

Vedolizumab blocks α4β7 integrin on gut-homing lymphocytes from binding MAdCAM-1 on gut endothelium, so fewer lymphocytes enter the inflamed mucosa; its action is gut-selective, unlike natalizumab, which also blocks lymphocyte entry to the brain, and the two are not combined. It is given by IV infusion for moderate to severe UC and Crohn disease. It is avoided in active severe infection, and it causes nasopharyngitis, headache, and arthralgia, while serious and opportunistic infections are not more common.

Ustekinumab binds the p40 subunit shared by IL-12 and IL-23, preventing receptor binding and reducing Th1 and Th17 differentiation and cytokine production. It treats moderate to severe Crohn disease and UC, with IV induction and then SC doses every 8 weeks. It is contraindicated in active infection including TB, causes nasopharyngitis, infections, and injection site reactions, and is not combined with live vaccines.

JAK inhibitors, tofacitinib and upadacitinib, block Janus kinases and so STAT phosphorylation, reducing signaling by many cytokines. They treat moderate to severe UC, and upadacitinib also Crohn disease. They are oral small molecules metabolized by CYP3A4 with some renal clearance, so strong CYP3A4 inhibitors raise and inducers lower their levels. They are not combined with biologics or strong immunosuppressants or used in active serious infection, and they cause herpes zoster, serious infections, raised cholesterol, VTE, MACE, and malignancy.

TB and HBV are screened for before biologics and JAK inhibitors, and no live vaccines are given during them.

Relationships to other conditions

Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis. 5-ASA maintains remission in UC, whereas immunomodulators maintain it in Crohn disease, and both move up to biologics as severity increases. Toxic megacolon is treated with IV corticosteroids, then infliximab or cyclosporine, before subtotal colectomy.

Antidotes, chelators, and drugs for fatty liver and sinusoidal injury

Several liver diseases have specific drug treatments: N-acetylcysteine and activated charcoal for paracetamol overdose, copper and iron removal for Wilson disease and hemochromatosis, GLP-1 receptor agonists and resmetirom for steatohepatitis, and defibrotide for sinusoidal obstruction. Thiamine and alpha-1 antitrypsin augmentation treat complications beyond the liver. Here, we review each drug's target, mechanism, use, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • N-acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione to detoxify NAPQI and is nearly 100% effective within 8 hours; activated charcoal helps only within 1–2 hours.
  • Copper chelators start treatment of Wilson disease, and zinc, which traps copper in enterocytes, maintains it.
  • Deferoxamine predisposes to Yersinia infection and causes hearing loss and retinopathy.
  • Thiamine is given before glucose in malnourished patients, and alpha-1 antitrypsin augmentation helps the lungs but not the liver.

Paracetamol overdose

N-acetylcysteine replenishes hepatic glutathione, which detoxifies NAPQI, the toxic metabolite of paracetamol, and so prevents hepatocellular necrosis. It treats paracetamol overdose, best within 8 hours, when it is nearly 100% effective, and is also used in acute liver failure. It is given by mouth or IV. IV doses can cause anaphylactoid reactions, mostly related to the infusion rate, and the IV diluent can cause fluid overload in heart failure; by mouth it causes nausea and vomiting.

Activated charcoal adsorbs drug in the gut lumen onto a large surface, so less is absorbed. It is given within 1–2 hours of a paracetamol overdose. It is not absorbed but binds other oral drugs given at the same time. It is contraindicated with an unprotected airway or reduced consciousness, GI obstruction or perforation, and caustic ingestion, and it causes aspiration pneumonitis, vomiting, and constipation.

Metals

Copper chelators, D-penicillamine and trientine, chelate copper in blood and tissues for urinary excretion, making copper balance negative and slowing liver and brain injury. They start treatment of Wilson disease. They are taken on an empty stomach, penicillamine needs pyridoxine, and neurologic symptoms can worsen early; iron, zinc, and antacids lower their absorption. Penicillamine is contraindicated after aplastic anemia with it and in penicillin allergy, and it causes rash, proteinuria, marrow suppression, dysgeusia, drug-induced lupus, and myasthenia; trientine causes fewer adverse effects.

Zinc induces metallothionein in enterocytes, which traps dietary copper until the cells are shed, so less copper is absorbed. It maintains treatment of Wilson disease but acts slowly, so it is not used alone at first. It causes gastric irritation and, in excess, copper deficiency, and it lowers absorption of tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, and penicillamine.

Iron chelators, deferoxamine and deferasirox, bind free iron for excretion, deferoxamine in urine and deferasirox in feces, lowering body iron in hemochromatosis when phlebotomy is not possible. Deferoxamine is given IV or SC, because it is not absorbed by mouth, and is contraindicated in severe renal disease or anuria; it causes hearing loss, retinopathy, and Yersinia infection. Deferasirox is taken once daily by mouth, causes kidney and liver injury and GI bleeding, and is bound by aluminum antacids.

Fatty liver, alcohol, and the sinusoids

GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide and liraglutide, increase glucose-dependent insulin release while slowing gastric emptying and reducing appetite, giving weight loss, better glycemia, and less steatohepatitis. They treat MASH with fibrosis, obesity, and T2DM. They are given SC weekly, or daily for liraglutide, and semaglutide also comes as a tablet. They are contraindicated with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or MEN2 and in pregnancy, and they cause nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, pancreatitis, and gallstones. Slower gastric emptying delays absorption of oral drugs, and hypoglycemia occurs with insulin or sulfonylureas.

Resmetirom selectively activates thyroid hormone receptor β in the liver, increasing hepatic fat breakdown and reducing liver fat and fibrosis in MASH with fibrosis. It is taken once daily by mouth and metabolized by CYP2C8, so strong CYP2C8 inhibitors such as gemfibrozil raise its levels, and it raises some statin levels. It is contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis and causes diarrhea and nausea.

Thiamine restores the cofactor thiamine pyrophosphate for the enzymes of glucose metabolism, preventing Wernicke encephalopathy. It is given with nutrition in alcoholic liver disease and before glucose in malnourished patients, IV when Wernicke encephalopathy is possible, because absorption is poor with alcohol use. Its only notable adverse effect is rare anaphylaxis with IV doses.

Defibrotide protects sinusoidal endothelial cells and restores the balance between clotting and fibrinolysis, relieving sinusoidal obstruction. It is the only approved drug for sinusoidal obstruction syndrome and is started early, IV. It causes hemorrhage and hypotension, so active bleeding and use with anticoagulants or fibrinolytics are contraindications.

Alpha-1 antitrypsin augmentation replaces the missing protease inhibitor of neutrophil elastase in the lung, slowing emphysema in A1AT deficiency, though it does not help the liver. It is a weekly IV infusion from pooled plasma, causes infusion reactions, and is contraindicated in IgA deficiency with anti-IgA antibodies.

Liver transplant treats end-stage disease and cures the defect in Wilson disease and A1AT deficiency.

Paracetamol overdose and Wilson disease. Both have specific treatments that remove a toxin, but on different timescales: N-acetylcysteine works best within 8 hours, whereas copper chelation and zinc work over months.

Drugs for portal hypertension, ascites, and vascular disease of the gut and liver

Cirrhosis is complicated by portal hypertension, ascites, and hepatorenal syndrome, each treated with drugs that change blood flow or sodium balance, while vascular disease of the gut and liver is treated with anticoagulation or vasodilation. Non-selective beta-blockers, terlipressin, diuretics, and albumin act on the circulation and kidney, and heparin and papaverine treat clots and vasospasm. Here, we review their targets, uses, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • Non-selective beta-blockers lower portal pressure by reducing cardiac output and leaving splanchnic α vasoconstriction unopposed.
  • Terlipressin with albumin treats hepatorenal syndrome but causes ischemia and respiratory failure.
  • Spironolactone causes hyperkalemia and gynecomastia, and furosemide causes hypokalemia, hypovolemia, and ototoxicity.
  • Vasopressors and α-agonists cause mesenteric vasospasm, so they are avoided in intestinal ischemia.

Portal hypertension and ascites

Non-selective beta-blockers, propranolol and nadolol, block β1 receptors, lowering cardiac output, and β2 receptors, leaving splanchnic α vasoconstriction unopposed, so portal inflow and pressure fall. They prevent variceal bleeding as an alternative to band ligation. Propranolol is lipophilic with high first-pass metabolism, whereas nadolol is hydrophilic and renally cleared. They are contraindicated in asthma, severe bradycardia or heart block, and decompensated heart failure, and they cause bradycardia, hypotension, fatigue, bronchospasm, and masked hypoglycemia, with additive AV block alongside verapamil and diltiazem.

Terlipressin is a vasopressin analog that constricts splanchnic vessels through V1 receptors on their smooth muscle, redirecting blood to the kidneys and lowering portal pressure. It treats hepatorenal syndrome with albumin. It is a prodrug converted to lysine vasopressin, so it acts longer than vasopressin. It is contraindicated in hypoxia or worsening respiratory failure and volume overload, and it causes ischemia of the gut, fingers, or heart, respiratory failure, and hyponatremia, with additive bradycardia alongside beta-blockers.

Diuretics act at two sites: spironolactone blocks aldosterone receptors in the collecting duct, and furosemide blocks NKCC2 in the loop of Henle, so sodium is excreted and ascites shrinks. They treat ascites with sodium restriction, and they treat the heart failure behind congestive hepatopathy. Spironolactone acts over days, whereas furosemide is fast, protein bound, and secreted into the tubule. Spironolactone is contraindicated in hyperkalemia and severe renal impairment, and diuretics are stopped if sodium falls sharply, creatinine rises, or encephalopathy develops. Spironolactone causes hyperkalemia, gynecomastia, and lower libido, and with ACE inhibitors, TMP-SMX, or potassium the hyperkalemia worsens; furosemide causes hypokalemia, hypovolemia, hyponatremia, metabolic alkalosis, and ototoxicity, which aminoglycosides worsen. NSAIDs blunt both.

Albumin raises plasma oncotic pressure and expands plasma volume, holding fluid in vessels and protecting the kidneys and circulation. It accompanies large-volume paracentesis, ABX in SBP, and a vasoconstrictor in hepatorenal syndrome, and it treats Ménétrier disease. It stays in vessels longer than crystalloid. Heart failure and severe anemia are contraindications because it causes volume overload and pulmonary edema.

Clots and vasospasm

Heparin activates antithrombin, inhibiting thrombin and factor Xa, so clots stop propagating, and oral anticoagulants follow it. It treats acute mesenteric ischemia, mesenteric venous thrombosis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, and portal vein thrombosis. It is given IV with a short half-life, monitored by aPTT, and reversed by protamine. It is contraindicated in active bleeding, past HIT, and severe thrombocytopenia, and it causes bleeding, HIT, hyperkalemia, and osteoporosis with long use; antiplatelets, NSAIDs, and other anticoagulants add to the bleeding.

Papaverine inhibits phosphodiesterase in vascular smooth muscle, raising cAMP and relaxing vessels, which dilates the mesenteric circulation. It treats NOMI, infused by catheter into the SMA for a local effect. It is contraindicated in complete AV block and causes hypotension, which other vasodilators worsen, as well as arrhythmia and hepatotoxicity.

Vasopressors and α-agonists cause mesenteric vasospasm, so they are avoided in intestinal ischemia.

Relationships to other conditions

Cirrhosis. Each complication has its drugs: non-selective beta-blockers or banding for varices; octreotide, IV ABX, and banding for variceal bleeding; diuretics, sodium restriction, and albumin with paracentesis for ascites; a third-generation cephalosporin with albumin for SBP; and albumin with terlipressin for hepatorenal syndrome.

Intestinal ischemia and vascular liver disease. Thrombosis in the mesenteric or hepatic circulation is treated with anticoagulation, whereas NOMI is a low-flow state treated by restoring flow and with papaverine, and vasoconstrictors are avoided.

Replacement therapy in digestive disease: vitamins, minerals, enzymes, and insulin

Malabsorption, gastric atrophy, and pancreatic disease leave deficiencies that are corrected by replacing what is missing. Vitamin B12, iron, folic acid, and the fat-soluble vitamins replace nutrients, pancreatic enzymes and lactase replace digestive enzymes, and an insulin infusion clears triglycerides in hypertriglyceridemic pancreatitis. Here, we review how each is used and its properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions.

Key points

  • In autoimmune gastritis, B12 is given IM 1000 µg daily or on alternate days for 1–2 weeks, then IM or high-dose oral for life, because high oral doses are absorbed without IF.
  • Iron is absorbed as ferrous iron in the duodenum, helped by acid, so PPIs and antacids lower its absorption.
  • Folic acid is not given alone in untreated B12 deficiency, since it corrects the anemia while neuropathy progresses.
  • Pancreatic enzymes relieve steatorrhea but not pain.

Vitamins and minerals

Vitamin B12, as cyanocobalamin, restores the cofactor of methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase for DNA synthesis and myelin, so reticulocytes rise within days and nerves recover if treated early. In autoimmune gastritis it is given IM 1000 µg daily or on alternate days for 1–2 weeks, then IM or high-dose oral for life, since high oral doses work by passive absorption without IF; it is also given in SIBO and, parenterally, in tropical sprue. Cobalt hypersensitivity is a contraindication. Treating severe anemia can cause early hypokalemia, injections are painful, and PPIs, H2R antagonists, and metformin lower absorption of dietary B12.

Iron, as ferrous sulfate or IV iron, replaces iron through duodenal DMT1 or directly, raising reticulocytes and hemoglobin. It treats the iron deficiency of Plummer-Vinson syndrome, autoimmune gastritis, and tropical sprue. It is absorbed as ferrous iron in the duodenum, helped by acid and vitamin C, so PPIs, antacids, and calcium lower its absorption, and it chelates tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, and levothyroxine. It is contraindicated in hemochromatosis and other iron overload, and it causes constipation, dark stools, and nausea, with hypersensitivity to IV forms.

Folic acid replenishes folate for thymidine synthesis, correcting megaloblastic anemia, and is given in tropical sprue and to people taking methotrexate or sulfasalazine. It is absorbed in the jejunum and lowers phenytoin levels. Adverse effects are rare, but untreated B12 deficiency is a contraindication, since folic acid corrects the anemia while neuropathy progresses.

Fat-soluble vitamins, A, D, E, and K, replace those lost with fat malabsorption, with calcium for bone. They are given in PBC and PSC, in pancreatic insufficiency, and, as very large doses of vitamin E, in abetalipoproteinemia. They need bile salts and micelles to be absorbed, and cholestyramine lowers their absorption. Vitamin A causes hepatotoxicity and teratogenicity, vitamin D hypercalcemia, and vitamin E bleeding with warfarin, while vitamin K opposes warfarin.

Enzymes and hormones

Pancreatic enzymes, as pancrelipase, replace pancreatic lipase, protease, and amylase in the duodenum, reducing steatorrhea. They are taken with meals for pancreatic insufficiency and the steatorrhea of chronic pancreatitis, but they do not relieve pain. They are enteric coated against acid, and a PPI helps if they fail. Pork protein allergy is a contraindication, and they cause abdominal pain and, at very high doses, fibrosing colonopathy.

Lactase replaces brush border lactase, splitting lactose into glucose and galactose, so symptoms after dairy lessen. It is taken with dairy for lactose intolerance and has no notable contraindications, adverse effects, or interactions.

An insulin infusion activates lipoprotein lipase through insulin receptors, clearing triglycerides rapidly in pancreatitis caused by hypertriglyceridemia. It is given IV with dextrose to prevent hypoglycemia. Hypoglycemia and hypokalemia are both its contraindications and its adverse effects, and beta-blockers mask hypoglycemia.

Relationships to other conditions

Malabsorption. Each cause leaves its own deficiency: celiac disease and tropical sprue iron and folate, ileal disease and SIBO B12, cholestasis and pancreatic insufficiency the fat-soluble vitamins, and lactase deficiency lactose intolerance, so replacement follows the cause.

Autoimmune gastritis and pancreatitis. Autoimmune gastritis needs lifelong B12 and iron because parietal cell loss removes IF and acid, whereas chronic pancreatitis needs enzymes with meals for steatorrhea, and acute pancreatitis from hypertriglyceridemia needs an insulin infusion.

Analgesics and smooth muscle relaxants in digestive disease

Pain in digestive disease is treated with paracetamol, NSAIDs, pregabalin, opioids, and TCAs, chosen by the cause of pain and the risks to the gut, while esophageal spasm and achalasia are treated with smooth muscle relaxants and botulinum toxin when procedures are unsuitable. Here, we review the targets, uses, properties, contraindications, adverse effects, and interactions of these drugs.

Key points

  • Paracetamol replaces NSAIDs in diverticulitis, and its overdose causes hepatotoxicity through NAPQI.
  • NSAIDs cause gastritis, peptic ulcer, and bleeding, and aspirin is avoided in children because of Reye syndrome.
  • In chronic pancreatitis, paracetamol, NSAIDs, or pregabalin come first, and opioids only if these fail.
  • Low-dose TCAs treat IBS pain and dyspepsia, but they are lethal in overdose.

Pain

Paracetamol inhibits central COX with little effect in the periphery, giving analgesia and antipyresis with little anti-inflammatory effect. It treats uncomplicated diverticulitis, in place of NSAIDs, and chronic pancreatitis. It is glucuronidated and sulfated in the liver, and a small fraction becomes NAPQI through CYP2E1, which glutathione detoxifies. It is contraindicated in severe liver disease, and its overdose causes hepatotoxicity, worse with chronic alcohol use, which induces CYP2E1 and depletes glutathione, and with fasting. Regular use raises the INR on warfarin.

NSAIDs, such as ibuprofen and aspirin, inhibit COX and decrease PG synthesis, giving analgesia but also less gastric mucus and bicarbonate; aspirin binds irreversibly, so its effect on platelets lasts their lifespan. They treat biliary colic and chronic pancreatitis. They are metabolized in the liver. They are contraindicated with peptic ulcer or GI bleeding, renal impairment, late pregnancy, and diverticulitis, and aspirin is avoided in children because of Reye syndrome. They cause gastritis, peptic ulcer and bleeding, pill esophagitis, kidney injury, and fluid retention. Anticoagulants and SSRIs add to the bleeding, corticosteroids raise ulcer risk, and they raise methotrexate and lithium levels.

Opioids, such as morphine, activate μ-opioid receptors, opening potassium channels and closing calcium channels, giving analgesia and slowing gut transit. They treat chronic pancreatitis pain only if other drugs fail. They are metabolized in the liver, and morphine metabolites accumulate in renal failure. They are contraindicated in respiratory depression, paralytic ileus, and toxic megacolon, and they cause constipation, to which tolerance does not develop, nausea, sedation, respiratory depression, sphincter of Oddi spasm, and dependence, with additive respiratory depression alongside benzodiazepines and alcohol.

Pregabalin binds the α2δ-1 subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing calcium entry and excitatory transmitter release and so neuropathic pain. With paracetamol or NSAIDs, it is first line for chronic pancreatitis pain. It is renally cleared unchanged, without hepatic metabolism, so the dose is lowered in renal impairment. It causes dizziness, sedation, edema, weight gain, and misuse, and it adds to the CNS and respiratory depression of opioids.

TCAs, such as amitriptyline, block serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake while also blocking muscarinic, H1, and α1 receptors, reducing visceral pain and slowing transit. Low doses treat IBS pain after antispasmodics and peppermint oil and H. pylori-negative dyspepsia. They are metabolized by CYP2D6, so inhibitors such as fluoxetine raise their levels, and with MAOIs they cause serotonin syndrome. They are contraindicated after recent MI and in heart block, angle-closure glaucoma, urinary retention, and MAOI use, and they cause anticholinergic effects, sedation, orthostatic hypotension, and QRS and QT prolongation, and are lethal in overdose.

Esophageal muscle

Smooth muscle relaxants, CCBs and nitrates, relax esophageal smooth muscle and the LES, CCBs by blocking L-type calcium channels and nitrates through nitric oxide and cGMP, lowering LES pressure and weakening spasm. They treat achalasia in people unfit for dilation or myotomy and diffuse esophageal spasm. They are taken before meals, and the effect is short and wanes. They cause headache, flushing, hypotension, and edema, and they are contraindicated in hypotension, with nitrates and PDE-5 inhibitors together causing severe hypotension.

Botulinum toxin cleaves SNARE proteins in cholinergic nerve terminals, blocking ACh release and relaxing the LES for months. It treats achalasia in people unfit for dilation or myotomy and diffuse esophageal spasm. It is injected endoscopically into the LES and wears off over months as terminals regrow. It is avoided with infection at the injection site and causes chest pain and scarring that makes later myotomy harder; aminoglycosides strengthen the block.

Relationships to other conditions

Chronic pancreatitis and diverticulitis. Pain relief in chronic pancreatitis starts with paracetamol, NSAIDs, or pregabalin, with opioids only if these fail, whereas uncomplicated diverticulitis uses paracetamol rather than NSAIDs.

Achalasia and diffuse esophageal spasm. Pneumatic dilation or myotomy is first line for achalasia, so CCBs, nitrates, and botulinum toxin are kept for patients unfit for these, whereas in diffuse esophageal spasm CCBs or nitrates come first.

Drugs and the conditions they cause

In the esophagus, NSAIDs, KCl, doxycycline, and bisphosphonates cause pill esophagitis, lodging in the mid-esophagus, especially when taken lying down with little water, and corticosteroids and chemotherapy predispose to infectious esophagitis by impairing cell-mediated immunity. In the stomach, NSAIDs cause gastritis and peptic ulcers by decreasing PG.

In the bowel, clindamycin, cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and penicillins cause C. difficile infection. Senna and bisacodyl cause chronic secretory diarrhea, and lactulose, PEG, and magnesium citrate cause chronic osmotic diarrhea. Opioids, anticholinergics, and antimotility drugs can precipitate toxic megacolon in severe colitis, and vasopressors and α-agonists cause mesenteric vasospasm.

In the liver, paracetamol overdose causes zone 3 necrosis; isoniazid, amiodarone, methotrexate, valproate, amoxicillin-clavulanate, and statins cause drug-induced liver injury, which is cholestatic with amoxicillin-clavulanate and rare with statins; and aspirin given to children after influenza or varicella causes Reye syndrome. Busulfan, cyclophosphamide, and oxaliplatin cause sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, oral contraceptives cause Budd-Chiari syndrome, and oral contraceptives and anabolic steroids cause hepatic adenoma.

Relationships to other conditions

Drug-induced liver injury. The diagnosis rests on the timing of the drug, exclusion of other causes, and improvement after stopping it, and treatment is stopping the drug with supportive care; paracetamol overdose alone has a specific antidote, N-acetylcysteine.

Pill esophagitis and NSAID ulcers. NSAIDs injure both organs, but by different routes: in the esophagus by direct contact where the pill lodges, and in the stomach by decreasing PG, which misoprostol can replace in people who must continue them.
