Asthma

Pathophysiology

Asthma is chronic airway inflammation that makes the airway hyperresponsive, so triggers cause variable, reversible obstruction: inflammation, obstruction, and AHR are its triad.

  1. SensitizationEpithelial alarmins drive type 2 immunity and IgE
  2. TriggerAllergen, virus, exercise, or irritant
  3. Mast cells degranulateHistamine, PGD2, and leukotrienes
  4. BronchoconstrictionWithin minutes
  5. Eosinophils arriveEdema and mucus, hours later
  6. RemodelingRepeated injury thickens the wall, and some loss becomes fixed

The airway wall

  • ALumenNarrow and plugged by mucus, with Curschmann spirals (mucus casts) and Charcot-Leyden crystals (eosinophil protein) in severe attacks
  • BEpitheliumShed, exposing nerve endings; more goblet cells
  • CBasement membraneThickened by collagen
  • DInflammationEosinophils, mast cells, and Th2 cells; edema and new vessels
  • ESubmucosal glandsEnlarged, making more mucus
  • FSmooth muscleHyperplasia and hypertrophy; contracts too easily and too hard

Type 2 inflammation

  • 1IL-4 and IL-13 → IgEB cells switch to IgE, which arms mast cells through FcεRI; allergen cross-links it, and the cell releases its mediators
  • 2IL-5 → eosinophilsMatured, recruited, and kept alive; their major basic protein damages the epithelium, and TGF-β drives remodeling
  • 3IL-13 → airwayGoblet cell mucus, AHR, and nitric oxide, measured as FeNO

Mast cells

  • Activated byIgE and allergen at FcεRI; pathogen and damage signals (through NF-κB); and mediators at G protein-coupled receptors
  • Granules, at onceHistamine, serotonin, TNF-α, and growth factors
  • Lipids, in minutesPGD2 and leukotrienes (LTC4)
  • Genes, in hoursIL-4, IL-5, and IL-13

What tightens the muscle

  • HistamineH1 receptor
  • Cysteinyl leukotrienesCysLT1 receptor; blocked by montelukast
  • PGD2TP receptor on muscle; its DP receptors mainly relax vessels and recruit cells
  • ACh from the vagusM3-AChR; inhibitory M2-AChR on the nerve fail in inflammation, so more ACh is released

Early and late phases

  • AEarly phaseMinutes to 3 hours: mast cell mediators constrict the muscle; a β2 agonist reverses it
  • BLate phaseAbout 4–8 hours: eosinophils and T cells bring edema and mucus; corticosteroids prevent it

Airway hyperresponsiveness

  • CSensitivityA smaller dose causes the same fall: the curve shifts left
  • DReactivityEach further dose causes a bigger fall: the curve is steeper
  • EEfficacyThe maximal fall is greater, and may have no plateau

Etiology

  • Risk factors
  • 1Atopy and familyAtopy is the strongest risk; family history, above all the mother's; with eczema and hay fever
  • 2Before and around birthPreterm birth, low birth weight, C-section, neonatal jaundice, and maternal smoking
  • 3Childhood exposuresRSV hospitalization, ABX, PVC, damp, and mold
  • 4Obesity and smokingBoth raise the risk, and make asthma harder to control
  • Triggers and phenotypes
  • 5AllergensHouse dust mite, animals, cockroach, molds, and grass and tree pollen
  • 6Viral infectionThe most common trigger of an attack, above all rhinovirus
  • 7Exercise and cold airDrying and cooling of the airway release mediators; peaks within 15 minutes of stopping, and settles within about 1 hour
  • 8WorkSensitizers (flour, isocyanates, and animal proteins) after a latent period, or a heavy irritant exposure; better away from work
  • 9Drugsβ-blockers, including eye drops; aspirin and NSAIDs in AERD, where COX-1 block lowers PGE2 and releases leukotriene production
  • 10Irritants, weather, and emotionSmoke, fumes, perfume, and strong soaps; thunderstorms in pollen season; stress and laughter

Note: Asthma is more common in boys before 15 and in women after; maternal vitamins D and E and zinc, and a diet of fruit, vegetables, and fish, may protect.

Symptoms and complications

Four cardinal symptoms

  • Wheeze, on breathing out
  • Cough, worse at night
  • Shortness of breath
  • Chest tightness

The pattern

  • Over timeVaries: hours to days, then settles by itself or with treatment
  • Time of dayWorse at night and in the early morning
  • TriggersInfection, allergen, exercise, cold, or laughter
  • Between attacksOften completely normal
  • AtopyEczema, hay fever, and nasal polyps (think AERD)
  • ExaminationWidespread, high-pitched expiratory wheeze; often normal. Clubbing is not asthma

Gas exchange as an attack worsens

  1. Air trappingAirways close early; the chest hyperinflates
  2. V/Q mismatchPaO2 falls; hyperventilation gives a low PaCO2
  3. TiringPaCO2 returns to normal: a danger sign
  4. FailurePaCO2 rises, with respiratory acidosis

A silent chest or a normal PaCO2 in a breathless patient means exhaustion, not improvement.

Who may die

  • Past intubation or ICU admission
  • 2 or more admissions, or 3 or more ED visits, in a year
  • 3 or more SABA canisters a year
  • No ICS, or poor adherence
  • No written action plan
  • Delayed presentation
  • Social isolation or mental illness
  • Heart disease

Complications

  • Respiratory failureType 2 as the patient tires
  • Pneumothorax and pneumomediastinumFrom air trapping, or ventilation
  • Mucus plugging and collapseOf a lobe or segment
  • Fixed obstructionFrom remodeling; overlap with COPD
  • ABPAAspergillus allergy: worsening asthma, high IgE, and central bronchiectasis
  • Treatment effectsOral thrush and hoarse voice; with repeated oral courses, diabetes, osteoporosis, cataract, and adrenal suppression

Diagnosis

Asthma is diagnosed from variable symptoms with no better explanation, plus proof of variable airflow obstruction or an FeNO of 40 ppb or more, before starting a preventer if possible.

  1. HistoryVariable symptoms, triggers, atopy, and family
  2. ExaminationWheeze; nose, skin, and other causes
  3. SpirometryBefore and after a bronchodilator
  4. FeNOIn the same session if available
  5. If unprovenRepeat when symptomatic, PEF diary, or challenge test

Spirometry

  • AObstruction (solid red)Low FEV1 and FEV1/FVC, with a scooped expiratory curve
  • BReversibility (dashed)FEV1 rises by 12% or more and 200 mL or more, 10–15 minutes after salbutamol; above 400 mL strongly favors asthma

Other tests

  • Reading spirometryCompare FEV1 and FVC rather than the ratio after a bronchodilator; a normal or irreversible result does not exclude asthma
  • PEF diaryTwice daily for 2 weeks: average daily variation above 10% in adults
  • Trial of ICSFEV1 up by more than 12% and 200 mL after 4 weeks
  • Methacholine challengeFEV1 falls 20% at a PC20 of 8 mg/mL or less; sensitive, so a negative test makes active asthma unlikely
  • Methacholine doseThe 2017 ERS standard reports the delivered dose instead, so nebulizers compare: PD20 of 200 µg or less is positive, and above 400 µg negative
  • Indirect challengeMannitol or hypertonic saline: a fall of 15%; exercise: a fall of more than 10% and 200 mL
  • FeNO40 ppb or more supports asthma; a normal result does not exclude it
  • Eosinophils, IgE, and skin prick testsShow type 2 inflammation and atopy; guide treatment, not diagnosis
  • CXRNot routine: hyperinflation, or another cause

In an attack

  • At onceSpeech, effort, consciousness, and SpO2; treat while assessing
  • ABGSevere or life-threatening attack, or suspected hypercapnia
  • CXRLife-threatening attack, poor response, or suspected pneumothorax or pneumonia
  • FEV1 or PEFOnce able; repeat after treatment

Red flag: anaphylaxis

Wheeze with a second system involved (rash, swelling, vomiting, or collapse), or sudden severe wheeze after exposure, is anaphylaxis: give IM adrenaline at once, before salbutamol.

Treatment

Everyone with asthma needs an inhaled corticosteroid, ideally in the same inhaler as the reliever, because a SABA alone relieves symptoms while inflammation goes untreated and attacks and deaths rise.

  1. ConfirmDiagnosis, control, and risk
  2. StartBudesonide-formoterol as needed, for most
  3. TeachInhaler technique and a written action plan
  4. ReviewControl, technique, adherence, and triggers
  5. StepUp if poorly controlled, down once stable

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease

Pathophysiology

Inhaled particles inflame the airways and alveoli for years, so small airways narrow and alveolar walls are lost, causing airflow limitation that does not fully reverse, hyperinflation, and uneven ventilation.

  1. Smoke or particlesIn a susceptible lung
  2. InflammationMacrophages, neutrophils, and CD8+ T cells
  3. Proteases and oxidantsOutweigh A1AT and antioxidants
  4. Airways and alveoliSmall airways scar; walls dissolve
  5. Air trappingHyperinflation, worse on exercise
  6. Gas exchange failsHypoxemia, then hypercapnia

Emphysema

  • ACentriacinarSmoking: respiratory bronchioles, upper lobes, outer acinus spared; goes with chronic bronchitis
  • BPanacinarA1AT deficiency: the whole acinus, lower lobes
  • CParaseptalOuter acinus under the pleura, upper lobes: bullae, and spontaneous pneumothorax in tall young men

A fourth type, irregular emphysema, lies beside scars and is rarely significant.

Chronic bronchitis

  • DGoblet cells and mucusProductive cough for 3 months a year in 2 consecutive years or more
  • EGlandsReid index=gland layerepithelium to cartilage>0.4

Small airways disease

  • WhereBronchioles under 2 mm, the main site of obstruction in early COPD
  • WhatInflammation, fibrosis, mucus plugs, and loss of the airway itself
  • Why they closeLost alveolar walls no longer hold them open in expiration

Protease and antiprotease

  1. SmokeRecruits neutrophils, and oxidizes A1AT
  2. Neutrophil elastaseReleased in excess
  3. A1AT too littleInactivated, or deficient (SERPINA1)
  4. Elastin digestedAlveolar walls lost, recoil lost

Flow limitation

  • FMaximal expirationLow and scooped: less recoil and narrow airways
  • GAt restBreathing already uses all the expiratory flow there is
  • HOn exerciseNo time to empty, so end-expiratory volume climbs toward TLC (dynamic hyperinflation) and breathing becomes hard work

Lung volumes

  • IRV and FRCHigh: trapped air; TLC also rises
  • JInspiratory capacity and VCLow: little room left to breathe in

Etiology

  • What is inhaled
  • 1SmokingThe main cause in developed countries: cigarettes, cannabis, and secondhand smoke; vaping also injures the lung. Up to 25–45% have never smoked
  • 2Biomass smoke and air pollutionWood, crop, and dung fires indoors: the leading cause worldwide in poorer regions
  • 3WorkCoal, silica, cadmium, organic dusts, and fumes
  • What makes the lung susceptible
  • 4A1AT deficiencySERPINA1 Pi*ZZ: early, basal, panacinar emphysema, and liver disease from protein trapped in hepatocytes
  • 5Early lifePoor growth in utero, prematurity, and childhood infection lower the peak lung function
  • 6InfectionTB, HIV, and repeated infection
  • 7AsthmaChildhood or long-standing asthma, with airway remodeling

Note: Suspect A1AT deficiency (4) below 45 years, with little smoking, basal emphysema, a family history, or liver disease, but test everyone once.

Symptoms and complications

  1. SilentBlamed on age or unfitness
  2. Cough and sputumMornings, then daily
  3. Exertional dyspneaSlowly worse
  4. ExacerbationsEach speeds the decline
  5. FailureDyspnea at rest, hypoxemia, and cor pulmonale

The pattern

  • DyspneaPersistent, progressive, and worse on exercise
  • Cough and sputumMucoid; purulent in exacerbations
  • Wheeze and tightnessVaries; more in exacerbations
  • BodyWeight loss, muscle wasting, and fatigue in advanced disease
  • FamilyEmphysema or liver disease: A1AT deficiency (4)

Examination

  • LookPursed lips, tripod posture, accessory muscles, barrel chest, and cyanosis
  • PercussHyperresonant, with low liver and lost cardiac dullness
  • ListenQuiet breath sounds, long expiration, and wheeze
  • Lower ribsDrawn in on inspiration (Hoover sign)
  • HeartHigh JVP, edema, loud P2: cor pulmonale
  • ClubbingNot COPD: look for lung cancer, bronchiectasis, or ILD

  3. GradeFEV1 of predicted: GOLD 1–4

Treatment

Stopping smoking and rehabilitation come first; long-acting bronchodilators relieve symptoms and prevent exacerbations, and an ICS is added only for exacerbations with high eosinophils, or asthma.

  1. Remove the causeStop smoking
  2. ProtectVaccines and exercise
  3. BronchodilateLAMA and LABA
  4. Prevent exacerbationsICS if eosinophils are high
  5. AdvancedOxygen, NIV, surgery, and palliative care

Bronchiectasis

Pathophysiology

Bronchi are permanently widened and cannot clear mucus, so bacteria persist and the inflammation they provoke destroys more of the wall.

  1. First injuryInfection, immune defect, or blocked airway
  2. Mucus staysCilia and cough fail
  3. Bacteria persistOften Haemophilus, then Pseudomonas
  4. NeutrophilsElastase and other proteases
  5. Wall destroyedElastin, muscle, and cartilage lost
  6. Bronchi dilateMore mucus stays

The vicious vortex

  • AInfectionChronic, often as biofilm: Haemophilus influenzae, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus aureus, and NTM
  • BInflammationNeutrophils called by IL-8 and leukotriene B4; T cells; some patients are eosinophilic
  • CAirway damageDilation, with loss of elastin, smooth muscle, and cartilage
  • DMucus stasisToo much, too thick, and poorly beaten by cilia

What the airway becomes

  • ECylindricalParallel tram-track walls; the mildest
  • FVaricoseBeaded
  • GCysticGrape-like clusters, often with fluid levels; the worst
  • HSignet ringBronchus wider than its artery

  • The most common

  • 2After infectionAbout 30%: childhood pneumonia, measles, pertussis, RSV, TB, and NTM
  • 3ImmunodeficiencyAbout 5%: low immunoglobulins, HIV, or drugs

  • 5ABPAAbout 3%: Aspergillus allergy in asthma or CF
  • The rest

  • 7PCDImmotile cilia: neonatal distress, sinusitis, otitis, infertility; with situs inversus, Kartagener syndrome
  • 8AutoimmuneRA, Sjögren syndrome, and IBD
  • 9Obstruction and aspirationForeign body, tumor, hilar nodes (middle lobe syndrome), and reflux
  • 10Structural and otherMounier-Kuhn and Williams-Campbell syndromes, yellow nail, and Young syndrome

Symptoms and complications

  1. CoughDaily, for years
  2. SputumThick, large, purulent
  3. ExacerbationsSeveral a year
  4. DeclineDyspnea, weight loss, and respiratory failure

Cystic Fibrosis

Pathophysiology

Two faulty CFTR genes leave epithelia unable to move chloride and bicarbonate, so secretions are dry and thick and block the lungs, pancreas, bowel, bile ducts, and vas deferens, while sweat is salty.

  1. CFTR variantsAutosomal recessive, chromosome 7
  2. No Cl− or HCO3− secretioncAMP-gated channel fails
  3. Dehydrated secretionsThick, acid mucus
  4. Ducts and airways plugEvery exocrine organ
  5. Infection and scarBronchiectasis; pancreatic fibrosis
  6. Respiratory failureMain cause of death

The airway surface

  • AChlorideCannot leave, so water does not follow
  • BSodium and waterOver-absorbed through ENaC
  • CMucusThick and stuck: cilia flatten, and Staphylococcus aureus, then Pseudomonas, persist

Why the sweat is salty

  • DSweat ductHere CFTR normally reabsorbs chloride, so the defect is reversed: NaCl is lost on the skin, with salt depletion in heat

What each organ does

  • ESinusesPansinusitis and nasal polyps
  • FLungsNormal at birth; plugs, infection, IL-8 and neutrophils, then bronchiectasis
  • GLiver and bilePlugged ductules: focal biliary cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and gallstones
  • HPancreasBlocked ducts: enzyme insufficiency, pancreatitis, then loss of islets (CFRD)
  • IBowelMeconium ileus; later DIOS and intussusception
  • JVas deferensAbsent on both sides: azoospermia
  • KSweat glandsSalt loss: hyponatremia and metabolic alkalosis

Bacteria over a lifetime

  1. InfancyStaphylococcus aureus and Haemophilus influenzae
  2. Childhood to adultPseudomonas, then mucoid biofilm
  3. LateBurkholderia cepacia, Stenotrophomonas, and NTM

Note: In CF the lung is damaged by infection and inflammation after birth, while the pancreas is damaged before it.

Etiology

  • Little or no function: severe, pancreas insufficient
  • 1Class I: no proteinNonsense or frameshift, such as G542X
  • 2Class II: misfoldedΔF508, the most common: retained and destroyed at the ER
  • 3Class III: gate closedG551D: at the surface, but will not open
  • Some function: milder, pancreas often sufficient
  • 4Class IV: poor conductionR117H: opens, with little flow
  • 5Class V: too littleSplicing variants: less normal protein
  • 6Class VI: unstableRemoved from the surface too soon

Who and how

  • InheritanceBoth parents carriers: 1 in 4
  • AncestryAbout 1 in 3500 of northern European descent; occurs in all
  • One copyCarriers are well, with a higher risk of pancreatitis and sinusitis
  • One mild variantCFTR-related disorder: absent vas deferens, pancreatitis, or bronchiectasis alone

Note: ΔF508 behaves as more than one class: it misfolds, and the little that reaches the surface opens poorly and is unstable, so it needs correctors and a potentiator.

Symptoms and complications

  1. NewbornScreening, meconium ileus, or jaundice
  2. InfantPoor growth, greasy stool, and cough
  3. ChildInfections, polyps, and DIOS
  4. AdultBronchiectasis, diabetes, and infertility

Asthma with COPD

  • SuspectSmoker with asthma before 40, atopy, or a large response
  • MarkersEosinophils 300 cells/µL or more, or high FeNO
  • TreatAs asthma first: always an ICS, never a LABA alone

Note: Wheeze says airways are narrow, not why, and one-sided or inspiratory noise is not asthma.

Diagnosis

Prove obstruction by spirometry, ask whether it varies, then use sputum, age, and imaging to name the disease and look for its treatable traits.

  1. HistoryAge, pattern, smoke, sputum, atopy
  2. SpirometryBefore and after a bronchodilator
  3. Variable?FeNO, diary, or challenge
  4. Fixed?Smoke: COPD. Sputum: CT
  5. Young or odd?Sweat test, A1AT, cilia, immunoglobulins
  6. TraitsEosinophils, infection, and comorbidities

Red flags

  • Silent chest
  • Drowsiness
  • Stridor
  • Massive hemoptysis

  • Failure to thrive

Treatment

All are treated by removing the cause, inhaled bronchodilators, airway clearance, vaccines, and exercise, but corticosteroids are central in asthma, selective in COPD, and not used for bronchiectasis or CF themselves.

  1. RemoveSmoke, allergen, and exposure
  2. InhaleRight drug, right device
  3. ClearIf there is sputum
  4. TrainPulmonary rehabilitation
  5. ProtectVaccines and action plan
  6. RescuePlan for exacerbations
