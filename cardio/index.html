Aortic Stenosis

Pathophysiology

A valve stiffened by fibrosis and calcium obstructs LV outflow, so the LV generates more pressure and thickens to keep wall stress normal, until a stiff, ischemic ventricle can no longer raise its output.

  1. Valve narrowsFibrosis, then calcium
  2. LV pressure risesA gradient forms between LV and aorta
  3. Concentric LVHThicker walls restore wall stress (Laplace)
  4. Stiff LVHigh filling pressure: dyspnea
  5. Supply falls short of demandAngina and subendocardial fibrosis
  6. LV failsLVEF and output fall late

What it does to the heart

  • AValveSmaller AVA, faster jet, and larger gradient; flow can be kept up only by more pressure and a longer LVET
  • BLVConcentric hypertrophy: sarcomeres added in parallel. LVEF stays normal until late
  • CLeft atriumA stiff LV depends on atrial contraction for filling, so A-fib can cause sudden HF
  • DLungsHigh LV EDP passes back: pulmonary congestion, then pulmonary hypertension
  • ECoronary supplyDemand rises with pressure, mass, and LVET; supply falls with short diastole, high EDP, and few vessels per muscle, so angina occurs even with normal coronaries
  • FAortaSlow, late, small pulse; output cannot rise when exercise dilates vessels, so BP falls

How the valve calcifies

  1. Endothelial injuryMechanical stress, on the aortic side
  2. Lipid entersLDL and Lp(a), oxidized
  3. InflammationMacrophages, cytokines, and angiotensin II
  4. FibrosisInterstitial cells lay down collagen
  5. CalcificationThey turn osteoblast-like, as NOTCH1 loses its brake on RUNX2

The gradient and the murmur

  • ALV pressureFar above aortic pressure all through ejection
  • BAortic pressureRises slowly, peaks late, and has a narrow pulse pressure (pulsus parvus et tardus)
  • CMurmurCrescendo-decrescendo, shaped by the gradient; the later the peak, the more severe

The pressure-volume loop

  • DTallerMore pressure is needed to eject
  • EHigher EDPA stiff LV fills at high pressure; EDV changes little
  • FNarrowerHigher afterload leaves a larger ESV and smaller stroke volume

From valve to symptom

SymptomWhy
DyspneaHigh LV filling pressure → pulmonary congestion
AnginaOxygen demand exceeds supply
Exertional syncopeExercise vasodilates while output is fixed, and LV baroreceptors misfire → BP falls

Note: Severe AS is load-dependent in both directions: too little preload empties a stiff LV, and too much floods the lungs.

Etiology

  • 1Calcific (degenerative)The most common cause in developed countries, usually after 70; risks are age, male sex, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, high LDL and Lp(a)
  • 2Bicuspid or other congenital valveThe main cause under 70: turbulent flow calcifies it 1–2 decades early; linked to NOTCH1 variants, aortic dilation, and coarctation. Critical congenital AS presents in infancy
  • 3RheumaticThe main cause in developing countries: commissures fuse, usually with mitral disease
  • 4Metabolic and mineralCKD (high phosphate and parathyroid hormone), Paget disease of bone, alkaptonuria, Fabry disease, and familial hypercholesterolemia
  • 5Autoimmune and radiationSLE, RA, and chest radiation; infective endocarditis can obstruct with vegetations
  • 6Above or below the valveSupravalvular (Williams syndrome); subvalvular, as a fixed membrane or the dynamic obstruction of HCM

Note: Aortic sclerosis is the same process without obstruction (jet below 2 m/s); about 2% a year progress to AS.

Symptoms and complications

  1. Latent phase10–20 years without symptoms
  2. Effort intoleranceDyspnea and fatigue on exertion, often blamed on age
  3. Classic triadDyspnea, angina, and exertional syncope
  4. FailureHF and death, on average within 1–3 years of symptoms

Survival without a new valve: classic teaching and trial

  • AnginaAbout 5 years
  • SyncopeAbout 3 years
  • HFAbout 2 years
  • Inoperable, without TAVIAbout 94% died within 5 years in the PARTNER trial, against 72% with TAVI

Examination

  • PulseSlow-rising, small, and delayed (parvus et tardus), with a narrow pulse pressure; may be lost in stiff arteries
  • ApexHeaving and sustained, not displaced; displaced only once the LV dilates and fails
  • MurmurHarsh ejection systolic, right second intercostal space to the carotids; louder in expiration and leaning forward
  • GallavardinHigh-pitched part heard at the apex, mimicking mitral regurgitation
  • S2A2 soft or absent as the valve stops moving; single, or reversed splitting because A2 comes late
  • Extra soundsS4 from the stiff LV; an ejection click in a mobile bicuspid valve, lost once it calcifies

Signs of severe AS

  • Slow-rising pulse
  • Soft or absent A2
  • Late-peaking murmur
  • Reversed S2 splitting
  • Thrill
  • S4
  • Signs of HF

Complications

  • HF and pulmonary hypertensionDiastolic first, systolic late
  • Sudden deathMostly once symptoms appear
  • A-fibLoss of the atrial kick can precipitate pulmonary edema
  • Heart blockCalcium extends into the conduction system
  • Infective endocarditisAbove all on bicuspid valves
  • Heyde syndromeShear unfolds large VWF multimers, which are cleaved: GI bleeding from angiodysplasia, better after AVR
  • Calcific emboliStroke or retinal artery occlusion
  • Aortic aneurysm or dissectionWith a bicuspid valve

Diagnosis

Transthoracic echocardiography diagnoses AS, grades it, and shows the cause and the LV; the symptoms, not the numbers alone, decide the timing of treatment.

  1. SuspectMurmur, or dyspnea, angina, or syncope
  2. EchocardiogramValve, velocity, gradient, AVA, and LVEF
  3. Do they agree?If not, look for low flow
  4. Symptoms?If none, consider an exercise test
  5. PlanCT and coronary imaging before AVR

Bedside value

  • Delayed carotid upstrokeLikelihood ratio about 9

Treatment

No drug slows AS; valve replacement is the only effective treatment, and symptoms with severe AS are the main reason to do it, because survival falls sharply once they begin.

  1. Mild to moderateTreat risk factors and watch by echo
  2. Severe, no symptomsLook for a trigger below, or discuss early AVR
  3. Severe with symptomsReplace the valve
  4. Too frail for benefitSymptom care

Which prosthesis?

  • MechanicalLasts; needs lifelong warfarin (never a DOAC), so suits younger patients
  • BioprostheticNo long-term warfarin, but wears out; suits older patients, and those planning pregnancy
  • Ross procedureThe patient's own pulmonary valve moved to the aortic position, for selected young patients

Until the valve is replaced

  • CongestionDiuretics in small steps: the stiff LV needs its preload
  • HypertensionTreat, starting low: an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker is well tolerated
  • A-fibRate or rhythm control promptly; cardiovert if unstable
  • LipidsStatin for vascular risk; it does not slow AS
  • Balloon valvuloplastyShort-lived: a bridge, or palliation; useful in children
  • TeethGood dental care; prophylaxis for prosthetic valves or past endocarditis, not native AS

Take care in severe AS

  • Nitrates and other vasodilators
  • Overdiuresis
  • Tachycardia
  • Spinal anesthesia
  • Strenuous exercise
  • Pregnancy before treatment

Aortic Regurgitation

Pathophysiology

Blood leaks back from the aorta in diastole, so the LV carries a volume load and ejects a large stroke into a low-resistance aorta; a slow leak is tolerated for years by dilation, but a sudden one causes pulmonary edema and shock.

  1. Diastolic leakFrom cusp damage or a wide root
  2. LV volume overloadEDV rises
  3. Eccentric hypertrophyDilated chamber holds stroke volume up
  4. Wide pulse pressureHigh systolic, low diastolic
  5. DecompensationWall stress rises, LVEF falls, EDP climbs

What it does to the heart

  • AValveLeak depends on orifice, pressure difference, and length of diastole: worse with bradycardia
  • BLVSarcomeres in series: the largest hearts of any valve disease, with both preload and afterload high
  • CMitral leafletThe jet holds the anterior leaflet partly shut: Austin Flint murmur
  • DAorta and coronariesLow diastolic pressure lowers coronary flow while LV mass raises demand: angina, above all at night when the heart slows
  • ELeft atrium and lungsHigh LV EDP passes back, late in chronic disease and at once in acute

Pressure and murmur

  • FSystolicHigh: a large stroke volume
  • GDiastolicLow and falling fast: run-off into the LV, with peripheral vasodilation (about 160/60 mmHg)
  • HSoundsEarly decrescendo murmur, longer as it worsens; a mid-diastolic rumble at the apex

The pressure-volume loop

  • IWideLarge EDV and stroke volume; forward output is less, because part leaks back
  • JRoundedNo isovolumetric relaxation or contraction while the valve leaks

Etiology

  • The valve itself (primary)
  • 1Bicuspid and degenerative cuspsBicuspid valve, calcific disease (often with AS), prolapse, and ventricular septal defect
  • 2RheumaticRetracted cusps, nearly always with mitral disease; the main cause in developing countries
  • 3Endocarditis or traumaPerforated or torn cusps; acute
  • The aortic root (secondary)
  • 4Heritable and degenerative aortaMarfan and Ehlers-Danlos syndromes, bicuspid aortopathy, familial thoracic aneurysm, and age with hypertension
  • 5AortitisSyphilis, giant cell arteritis, Takayasu arteritis, axial spondyloarthritis, and Behçet disease
  • 6DissectionType A: a flap or dilated root; acute

Note: Acute AR is endocarditis (3), dissection (6), or trauma (3) until shown otherwise.

Symptoms and complications

  1. CompensatedYears without symptoms
  2. SymptomsExertional dyspnea and palpitation
  3. Failing LVOrthopnea, nocturnal dyspnea, and angina
  4. LateHF, arrhythmia, and death

Symptoms

  • DyspneaOn exertion, then lying flat and at night
  • PalpitationForceful beats and head pounding, worse on the left side
  • Nocturnal anginaA slow rate and low diastolic pressure
  • AcuteSudden breathlessness, shock, and pain (tearing in dissection, fever in endocarditis)

Examination

  • PulseCollapsing (water hammer), sometimes double (bisferiens); wide pulse pressure
  • ApexDisplaced down and out, hyperdynamic
  • MurmurHigh-pitched, early diastolic, left sternal edge (right if the root is dilated); best leaning forward in full expiration
  • AlsoEjection systolic flow murmur, soft A2, and S3
  • Austin FlintApical rumble, without an opening snap or loud S1

Mitral Stenosis

Pathophysiology

A narrow mitral valve keeps left atrial pressure high to fill the LV, so pressure backs up into the lungs and right heart while the LV itself is spared and underfilled.

  1. Valve narrowsBelow about 2 cm² (normal 4–6)
  2. Left atrial pressure risesA gradient through diastole
  3. Atrium dilatesA-fib and clot
  4. Lungs congestDyspnea and hemoptysis
  5. Pulmonary hypertensionRV fails: edema and ascites

What it does to the heart

  • AValveFlow needs time and pressure: a fast heart shortens diastole and raises the gradient
  • BLeft atriumLarge and fibrotic; loss of the atrial kick in A-fib drops output suddenly; stasis forms clot
  • CLungsPulmonary venous congestion, then edema; bronchial veins can burst
  • DLVLow EDV and low output; LVEF usually normal
  • EPulmonary arteriesPassive pressure, then vasoconstriction and remodeling; the RV and tricuspid valve fail

Pressure and murmur

  • FGradientLeft atrial above LV pressure all through diastole
  • GOpening snapThe earlier after A2, the higher the atrial pressure and the worse the stenosis
  • HRumbleLow-pitched and mid-diastolic, swelling before S1 as the atrium contracts; that swell is lost in A-fib

How rheumatic disease scars the valve

  1. GAS pharyngitisAnd acute rheumatic fever
  2. Molecular mimicryAntibodies and T cells attack the valve
  3. Repeated carditisEach attack adds scar
  4. FusionCommissures, thick leaflets, short chordae
  5. Fish-mouth valve10–20 years or more later

Why it decompensates

TriggerWhy
A-fibNo atrial kick, and a fast rate
Exercise, fever, anemia, or thyrotoxicosisMore flow in less time

Note: Left-sided congestion here is not LV failure: the failing ventricle in MS is the right one.

Etiology

  • 1RheumaticThe main cause worldwide; about 80% of patients are women, with symptoms in the third to fourth decades
  • 2Mitral annular calcificationThe main cause in older people in developed countries: age, CKD, hypertension, and radiation; commissures stay open
  • 3CongenitalParachute valve, or cor triatriatum; with an atrial septal defect, Lutembacher syndrome
  • 4Autoimmune, infiltrative, and drugSLE, RA, Fabry disease, mucopolysaccharidosis, ergot drugs, and carcinoid (left-sided only with lung metastasis or a shunt)
  • 5Obstruction that mimics itLeft atrial myxoma, large vegetation, or a ball-valve clot

Note: Up to 40–50% of patients with rheumatic MS recall no rheumatic fever.

Symptoms and complications

  1. Latent10–20 years after rheumatic fever
  2. Exertional dyspneaAnd fatigue
  3. A-fibPalpitation, sudden worsening, or stroke
  4. Pulmonary hypertensionBreathless at rest, with edema

Symptoms

  • DyspneaOn exertion, then orthopnea and nocturnal
  • PalpitationOften the onset of A-fib
  • HemoptysisBurst bronchial vein, or pink frothy edema
  • EmbolismStroke may be the first sign
  • Hoarseness or dysphagiaLarge atrium pressing on the recurrent laryngeal nerve (Ortner syndrome) or esophagus
  • Swelling and ascitesRV failure

Examination

  • FaceMalar flush, from low output and vasoconstriction
  • PulseSmall; irregular in A-fib
  • ApexTapping (a palpable S1), not displaced; left parasternal heave of the RV
  • SoundsLoud S1, opening snap, and loud P2
  • MurmurLow rumble at the apex, with the bell, on the left side in expiration, after exercise

Signs of severe MS

  • Snap close to A2
  • Long rumble
  • Soft S1 (rigid valve)
  • Loud P2 and heave
  • Signs of RV failure

Complications

  • A-fibIn about 40–50% of those with symptoms
  • Stroke and systemic embolismHigher risk than in any other valve disease
  • Pulmonary edemaWith a fast rate, or in pregnancy and labor
  • Pulmonary hypertension and RV failureWith tricuspid regurgitation
  • Infective endocarditisLess often than in regurgitation
  • Maternal deathSevere MS in pregnancy

Diagnosis

Echocardiography confirms MS, measures valve area and gradient, and shows whether the valve suits balloon treatment.

  1. SuspectDyspnea, A-fib, stroke, or pregnancy
  2. EchocardiogramArea, gradient, and pulmonary pressure
  3. CauseFused commissures, or calcified ring?

  5. TOEExclude atrial clot before a balloon

Mitral Regurgitation

Pathophysiology

The mitral valve leaks in systole, so part of each stroke goes back to the left atrium and returns in diastole as extra volume; chronic leaks dilate the atrium and LV, while a sudden one floods a small atrium and the lungs.

  1. Systolic leakInto a low-pressure atrium
  2. Volume overloadMore EDV, more total stroke
  3. DilationAtrium, LV, and ring
  4. MR begets MRA wider ring leaks more
  5. LV failsLVEF falls late, from a high baseline

What it does to the heart

  • AValveAfterload drives the leak: high BP worsens it, vasodilation eases it
  • BLeft atriumDilates and spares the lungs for years; then A-fib and clot
  • CLVEccentric hypertrophy. Ejecting into the atrium unloads it, so LVEF looks high: 60% is already abnormal
  • DAortaForward output is total stroke less the leak
  • ELungsEdema and pulmonary hypertension, late or acute

Pressure and murmur

  • Fv waveTall, above all when acute and the atrium is stiff
  • GMurmurPansystolic, flat, and through S2; short in acute severe MR as pressures equalize
  • HS3Rapid filling by the returning volume

The pressure-volume loop

  • IWideLarge EDV; low ESV while compensated
  • JSlopingNo true isovolumetric contraction or relaxation: blood leaves for the atrium in both

Carpentier: how the leaflets move

  • KType I, normal motionRing dilation (atrial MR, A-fib), or a perforation: central jet
  • LType II, too much motionProlapse, or ruptured chordae or papillary muscle: jet away from the bad leaflet
  • MType IIIa, stiff throughoutRheumatic, radiation, or drugs
  • NType IIIb, held down in systoleIschemic or dilated LV: jet toward the bad leaflet

  • Primary: the valve itself

  • 2RheumaticThe most common cause in developing countries, often with MS

  • 6Ventricular secondaryIschemic or dilated LV pulls the leaflets down

Tricuspid and Pulmonary Valve Disease

Pathophysiology

Right-sided valves work at low pressure, so a leak or narrowing is tolerated for years until the right atrium and RV enlarge, the veins congest, and forward output falls.

  1. Leak or narrowingTR is by far the most common
  2. Right heart loadsVolume from a leak, pressure from stenosis
  3. Right atrium enlargesA-fib and high venous pressure
  4. Veins congestLiver, gut, kidneys, and legs
  5. Output fallsLess blood reaches the LV: fatigue

What it does to the heart

  • ATricuspid valveIts ring has little fibrous support and stretches easily, so a leak widens the ring and the leak grows
  • BRight atriumCompliant, so it hides a leak at first; later large, with A-fib
  • CVeins and organsCongestive hepatopathy, ascites, gut edema, and poor kidney perfusion
  • DRVDilates in TR and pulmonary regurgitation; thickens in pulmonary stenosis; stays small in tricuspid stenosis
  • ESeptumA volume-loaded RV flattens it in diastole, so the LV fills less
  • FPulmonary valve and arteryThe artery dilates beyond a stenosis; high pulmonary pressure stretches the ring and makes it leak

The jugular venous pulse

  • GGiant v (cv) waveTR fills the atrium in systole, so the x descent is lost; the liver pulses with it
  • HGiant a waveThe atrium contracts against a narrow tricuspid valve, or a stiff RV; absent in A-fib

  2. Venous return risesMore flow through the right heart
  3. Right-sided murmur louderCarvallo sign in TR

  • 2A-fibThe atrium and ring dilate: atrial TR, typical of older women
  • Acquired valve disease
  • 3Infective endocarditisIV drug use, central lines, and devices; mostly Staphylococcus aureus: TR
  • 4Leads, biopsy, and traumaPacemaker or defibrillator lead, its extraction, biopsy, or blunt chest trauma: TR

  • 6CarcinoidSerotonin from liver metastases thickens right-sided valves, usually TR with pulmonary stenosis; the lung spares the left
  • 7Myxomatous, autoimmune, and drugProlapse, Marfan syndrome, SLE, radiation, ergot drugs, and fenfluramine
  • 8Mass at the valveMyxoma, thrombus, large vegetation, or renal cell carcinoma growing up the vena cava: tricuspid stenosis
  • Congenital and its repair
  • 9Ebstein and other tricuspid anomaliesEbstein anomaly (linked to lithium in pregnancy), with atrial septal defect and accessory pathways; tricuspid atresia
  • 10Congenital pulmonary stenosisNoonan (about 70% have it), Williams, and Alagille syndromes, rubella, and tetralogy of Fallot
  • 11After surgery or balloonRepaired tetralogy, pulmonary valvotomy, or Ross procedure: pulmonary regurgitation

  • JSubvalvularTetralogy, double-chambered RV, or muscle thickened by valvular stenosis

Diagnosis

Echocardiography answers five questions in order: which valve, what mechanism, how severe, what it has done to the heart, and whether other valves are involved.

  1. MurmurTiming, site, and maneuver
  2. Echo it?See below
  3. MechanismLeaflets or chambers; primary or secondary
  4. SeveritySeveral measures that agree
  5. EffectSymptoms, LVEF, size, rhythm, and pulmonary pressure
  6. CauseThen plan

Treatment

Drugs treat congestion, rhythm, and clotting, but only repairing or replacing the valve changes the course, and the time to do it is at the first symptom or the first sign that the ventricle is failing.

  1. MildEcho every 3–5 years
  2. ModerateEcho every 1–2 years
  3. Severe, no triggerEvery 6–12 months
  4. TriggerHeart team: repair, replace, or catheter
  5. AfterwardAnticoagulate, prevent endocarditis, and follow
