Fluids

Body fluid compartments

Water is about 60% of body weight in men and 50% in women. Two thirds of it is inside cells (ICF) and one third outside (ECF), and the ECF is split again into the interstitium (three quarters) and plasma (one quarter).

  • AIsotonic lossBleeding, vomiting, and diarrhea remove fluid with about as much Na+ as plasma, so only the ECF shrinks and plasma Na+ is unchanged
  • BWater drawn from cellsSweat and osmotic diuresis lose more water than Na+; the ECF becomes concentrated and draws water out of cells, so both compartments shrink and plasma Na+ rises
  • CThird spacingFluid leaves plasma for the interstitium or a body cavity; total body water is unchanged, but the circulating volume falls
  • DSalt and water gainedRetained or infused isotonic fluid spreads through the ECF, about three parts to the interstitium for each part kept in plasma, so most of it becomes edema

What each compartment holds

The Na+/K+-ATPase pumps 3 Na+ out of cells for every 2 K+ it brings in, so the ECF is a Na+ and Cl− space and the ICF a K+, phosphate, and protein space; this is why Na+ sets the size of the ECF.

  • LExtracellular: Na+, Cl−, and HCO3−They fill the ECF and are kept out of cells by the Na+/K+-ATPase; in plasma, Na+ is 135–145 mmol/L and HCO3− 22–26 mmol/L; Ca2+ is mostly extracellular too
  • MIntracellular: K+, Mg2+, phosphate, and proteinMost of the body's K+ is inside cells, so plasma K+ (3.5–5 mmol/L) is a small window onto a large store; free Ca2+ inside cells is very low
  • NPlasma versus interstitium: proteinThe two ECF compartments share their ions but not albumin, which stays in the vessels and holds water there, the basis of the Starling forces

What changes total body water

  • AgeAbout 75% of body weight in infants, 50–60% in adults, and less in older adults, who lose ECF faster for the same fluid loss
  • Sex and body fatFat holds little water, so women and people with obesity have a lower proportion of body water

Osmosis and tonicity

Water crosses cell membranes freely and moves toward the side with more dissolved particles; the particles that cannot cross the membrane decide where it goes.

  • OsmolalityAll dissolved particles per kilogram of water; normal 275–295 mOsm/kg. Serum osmolality = 2 × Na+ + glucose + urea
  • TonicityOnly the particles that cannot cross cell membranes, so only these move water. Tonicity = 2 × Na+ + glucoseUrea and ethanol add to osmolality but not tonicity; glucose moves water when insulin is lacking
  • EHypotonic fluidFewer particles outside than inside, so water enters cells and they swell, as in hyponatremia
  • FIsotonic fluidEqual particles on both sides, so there is no net movement and cell volume is unchanged
  • GHypertonic fluidMore particles outside, so water leaves cells and they shrink, as in hypernatremia and hyperglycemia

Water balance

  • IntakeDrinking and food, driven by thirst, which begins at an osmolality of about 295 mOsm/kg; adults need about 1.6 L a day at minimum
  • OutputUrine, stool, sweat, and insensible loss from the skin and lungs, which can reach 1 L a day
  • Urine concentrationThe kidney can make urine from about 50 to 1200 mOsm/kg, and normal kidneys can excrete up to about 20 L of water a day
  • Solute sets water excretionA normal diet gives 600–900 mOsm of solute a day, and urine volume follows the solute excreted, so a very low solute intake limits how much water can be excreted

Starling forces

Fluid crosses the capillary wall according to the balance of pressures across it; anything that tips the balance outward faster than the lymphatics can return the fluid causes edema or third spacing.

Net filtration = Kf × [(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)]
  • HCapillary hydrostatic pressurePushes fluid out; raised when the ECF is expanded or venous outflow is blocked, as in HF, CKD, and portal hypertension
  • IPlasma oncotic pressureAlbumin holds fluid in; falls when albumin is lost (nephrotic syndrome), not made (cirrhosis), or not eaten (malnutrition)
  • JCapillary permeabilityCytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) and burns open the wall, letting fluid and protein leak, which also narrows the oncotic gradient
  • KLymphatic drainageReturns filtered fluid and protein to the blood; edema forms when filtration outruns it or the lymphatics are blocked

The revised Starling model and the glycocalyx

  • Classic modelFiltration at the arteriolar end, where hydrostatic pressure is high, and reabsorption at the venular end, where oncotic pressure wins
  • Revised modelNet filtration continues along most capillaries, with little reabsorption; the lymphatics return the filtered fluid
  • GlycocalyxA layer of glycosaminoglycans lining the endothelium acts as the filtration barrier; albumin is largely kept out of the space beneath it, so the oncotic gradient that matters is across the glycocalyx, not between plasma and the whole interstitium
  • Albumin outside the vesselsAlbumin still leaks slowly: about half the body's albumin is extravascular, and interstitial oncotic pressure is 30–60% of plasma's
  • Glycocalyx damageDisruption of the glycocalyx, as in inflammation, increases filtration and causes edema

Why edema does not form at once

  • Negative interstitial pressureNormally −2 to −4 mmHg, held by the collagen and gel matrix; while it stays negative, the interstitium is stiff, so a little extra fluid raises its pressure and opposes further filtration
  • Compliance rises sharplyOnce interstitial pressure reaches zero, the interstitium becomes far more compliant and fluid accumulates freely as edema
  • Lymph flow increasesThe lymphatics carry 8–12 L of lymph a day and speed up as filtration rises, returning the extra fluid
  • Weight before pittingBody weight can rise by nearly 10% before pitting edema appears

Body fluids: compartments, tonicity, the regulation of volume and concentration, and the forces across the capillary wall

Body water is divided between the cells and the extracellular fluid, which is divided again between the interstitium and plasma, and the Na+/K+-ATPase keeps the ECF a Na+ space and the ICF a K+ space. Water follows effective osmoles across cell membranes, so the tonicity of a gain or loss decides whether it stays in the ECF or also changes cell volume, while separate systems regulate ECF volume, through Na+, and its concentration, through water. Across the capillary wall, hydrostatic and oncotic pressures, refined by the glycocalyx, decide how fluid divides between plasma and the tissues, and the interstitium and lymphatics resist edema until they are overwhelmed. Here, we review the size and composition of the compartments, osmosis and tonicity, the effects of gains and losses, water balance, the Starling forces and their revised form, and the classification of edema and effusions.

Key points

  • Water is about 60% of body weight in men and 50% in women, two thirds in the ICF and one third in the ECF, which is three quarters interstitium and one quarter plasma; infants have more water and older adults, women, and people with obesity less.
  • The Na+/K+-ATPase pumps 3 Na+ out for every 2 K+ in, so Na+ and Cl− dominate the ECF and K+, phosphate, and protein the ICF; plasma differs from the interstitium mainly in albumin.
  • Tonicity, 2 × Na+ + glucose, counts only the particles that cannot cross cell membranes; isotonic gains and losses stay in the ECF, whereas changes in tonicity move water into or out of cells.
  • Volume is set by Na+ and corrected by RAAS, the sympathetic system, and natriuretic peptides; concentration is set by water and corrected by ADH and thirst.
  • In the revised Starling model, net filtration continues along most capillaries, the oncotic gradient across the glycocalyx is what holds fluid in, and the lymphatics return the filtrate.
  • Light criteria classify a pleural effusion as an exudate if the protein ratio is above 0.5, the LDH ratio above 0.6, or the pleural LDH above two thirds of the upper limit of normal; a SAAG of 11 g/L or more indicates portal hypertension.

Body fluid compartments

Water makes up about 60% of body weight in men and 50% in women. Two thirds of it lies inside cells and one third outside, and the ECF is divided again into the interstitium, three quarters, and plasma, one quarter. The Na+/K+-ATPase pumps 3 Na+ out of cells for every 2 K+ it brings in, so Na+ (135–145 mmol/L in plasma), Cl−, and HCO3− (22–26 mmol/L) dominate the ECF, and K+ (3.5–5 mmol/L in plasma but high inside cells), Mg2+, phosphate, and protein dominate the ICF; Ca2+ is mostly extracellular, with very little free inside cells. Because the ECF is a Na+ space, Na+ sets its size. Plasma differs from the interstitium mainly in protein, because albumin stays in the vessels and holds water there. Body water is about 75% of body weight in infants and less in older adults, who lose ECF faster for the same loss, and fat holds little water, so women and people with obesity have a lower proportion.

Osmosis and tonicity

Water crosses cell membranes freely and moves toward the side with more dissolved particles. Osmolality counts all dissolved particles per kilogram of water, calculated as 2 × Na+ + glucose + urea and normally 275–295 mOsm/kg, whereas tonicity, 2 × Na+ + glucose, counts only the particles that cannot cross cell membranes and so move water; urea and ethanol add to osmolality but not tonicity, and glucose moves water when insulin is lacking. In hypotonic fluid, water enters cells and they swell, as in hyponatremia; in isotonic fluid, there is no net movement; and in hypertonic fluid, water leaves cells and they shrink, as in hypernatremia and hyperglycemia. Isotonic NS and LR, the latter slightly hypotonic at 273 mOsm/L, spread through the ECF, leaving about 250 mL of each liter in plasma; half-normal saline acts half as saline and half as free water; D5W is near-isotonic in the bag but hypotonic once its dextrose is metabolized, spreading through all body water with only about 1/12 remaining in plasma; 3% saline stays in the ECF and draws water out of cells; and albumin stays in plasma and draws interstitial fluid in.

Gains and losses

Isotonic losses, such as diarrhea, vomiting, and bleeding, shrink only the ECF and leave plasma Na+ unchanged, and isotonic gains, such as NS or the retention of HF and cirrhosis, expand only the ECF. Losing more water than salt, through sweat, osmotic diuresis, or diabetes insipidus, shrinks both compartments and raises Na+, whereas losing more salt than water, through diuretics, adrenal insufficiency, or losses replaced with water alone, shrinks the ECF while water enters cells and Na+ falls. Gaining water, in SIADH or primary polydipsia, expands both compartments and lowers Na+, and a hypertonic gain, of 3% saline, glucose, or mannitol, expands the ECF at the expense of the cells. In third spacing, fluid moves from plasma into the interstitium without changing total body water.

Volume, concentration, and water balance

Volume and concentration are regulated separately. Volume is set by total body Na+, sensed by baroreceptors and the afferent arteriole, and corrected by RAAS, the sympathetic system, and natriuretic peptides; its failures are hypovolemia and hypervolemia. Concentration is set by water, sensed by hypothalamic osmoreceptors, and corrected by ADH and thirst; its failures are hyponatremia and hypernatremia. A large fall in effective volume also releases ADH, so volume can override concentration, which is how hypovolemia and HF cause hyponatremia.

Thirst begins at an osmolality of about 295 mOsm/kg, and adults need about 1.6 L of water a day at minimum. Water leaves in urine, stool, sweat, and insensible losses from the skin and lungs, which can reach 1 L a day. The kidney can make urine from about 50 to 1200 mOsm/kg and can excrete up to about 20 L of water a day, but because urine volume follows the solute excreted, of which a normal diet provides 600–900 mOsm a day, a very low solute intake limits how much water can be excreted.

Starling forces

Fluid movement across the capillary wall follows the balance of capillary and interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic pressures, net filtration = Kf × [(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)]. Capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out and rises when the ECF is expanded or venous outflow is blocked; plasma oncotic pressure, generated by albumin, holds fluid in and falls when albumin is lost, not made, or not eaten; cytokines and burns increase permeability; and lymphatics return filtered fluid and protein.

In the classic model, fluid is filtered at the arteriolar end of the capillary and reabsorbed at the venular end, but net filtration in fact continues along most capillaries, with the lymphatics returning the filtrate. The endothelium is lined by a glycocalyx of glycosaminoglycans that acts as the filtration barrier; because albumin is largely kept out of the space beneath it, the oncotic gradient that holds fluid in is the one across the glycocalyx rather than between plasma and the whole interstitium. Albumin still leaks slowly, so about half the body's albumin is extravascular and interstitial oncotic pressure is 30–60% of plasma's, and disruption of the glycocalyx, as in inflammation, increases filtration and causes edema.

Edema does not form at once. Interstitial pressure is normally −2 to −4 mmHg, held by the collagen and gel matrix, and while it stays negative the interstitium is stiff, so a little extra fluid raises its pressure and opposes further filtration; once it reaches zero, compliance rises sharply and fluid accumulates freely. The lymphatics, which carry 8–12 L of lymph a day, speed up as filtration rises and return the extra fluid, and body weight can rise by nearly 10% before pitting edema appears.

Edema and effusions

Pitting edema is free, low-protein fluid that pressure pushes aside, from high capillary pressure or low albumin, as in HF, CKD, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, IV fluid, and drugs, and in early lymphedema; it collects in dependent areas. Non-pitting edema is fluid held by protein, glycosaminoglycans, fat, or fibrosis, as in late lymphedema, which involves the feet, myxedema of hypothyroidism, with thickened yellow-orange skin and periorbital swelling, and lipedema, which spares the feet.

A transudate forms when the Starling forces change across an intact capillary wall and is low in protein and LDH, as in pleural effusions from left HF, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, low albumin, and peritoneal dialysis. An exudate forms when inflammation, infection, or malignancy makes the wall leak protein and cells, as in pneumonia, TB, malignancy, pancreatitis, SLE, and rheumatoid arthritis. By Light criteria, a pleural effusion is an exudate if the pleural fluid to serum protein ratio is above 0.5, the pleural fluid to serum LDH ratio is above 0.6, or the pleural fluid LDH is above two thirds of the upper limit of normal serum LDH, and a transudate if none is met. Protein alone classifies ascites poorly, so the SAAG, serum albumin minus ascitic fluid albumin, is used instead: 11 g/L or more indicates portal hypertension, as in cirrhosis, alcoholic hepatitis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, portal vein thrombosis, and HF, and below 11 g/L indicates other causes, such as peritoneal carcinomatosis, pancreatitis, TB peritonitis, and nephrotic syndrome.

Review article

Acid-base balance: buffers, compensation, and a stepwise reading of the blood gas

Plasma pH depends on the ratio of HCO3−, regulated by the kidney, to CO2, regulated by the lungs. A primary change in HCO3− is a metabolic disorder and a primary change in pCO2 a respiratory one, and the other organ compensates: the lungs within hours and the kidney over days. Here, we review the bicarbonate buffer, the four primary disorders, the expected compensation for metabolic disorders, and a stepwise approach to the blood gas that leads to the anion gap in acidosis and the urine Cl− in alkalosis.

Key points

  • Normal values are pH 7.35–7.45, pCO2 35–45 mmHg, and HCO3− 22–26 mmol/L.
  • HCO3− moving the same way as pH means a metabolic disorder; pCO2 moving the opposite way means a respiratory one.
  • Metabolic disorders are compensated by the lungs within hours, and respiratory disorders by the kidney over days.
  • Expected pCO2 is 1.5 × HCO3− + 8 (± 2) in metabolic acidosis and 0.7 × HCO3− + 20 (± 5) in metabolic alkalosis; a pCO2 outside the range means a second, respiratory disorder.

Buffers and primary disorders

Plasma pH is set by the balance H+ + HCO3− ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ CO2 + H2O, with the kidney regulating HCO3− and the lungs regulating CO2; normal values are pH 7.35–7.45, pCO2 35–45 mmHg, and HCO3− 22–26 mmol/L. Metabolic acidosis is a low pH with a low HCO3−, and metabolic alkalosis a high pH with a high HCO3−; in both, pCO2 compensates within hours, falling in acidosis through hyperventilation and rising in alkalosis through hypoventilation. Respiratory acidosis is a low pH with a high pCO2, and respiratory alkalosis a high pH with a low pCO2; in both, HCO3− compensates over days, rising in respiratory acidosis as the kidney retains it and falling in respiratory alkalosis as the kidney excretes it.

Expected compensation

The third step is whether compensation is as expected. In metabolic acidosis, the Winter formula predicts pCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3− + 8 (± 2); other rules are that pCO2 is about the last two digits of the pH, or falls about 1.2 mmHg for each 1 mmol/L fall in HCO3−. In metabolic alkalosis, expected pCO2 = 0.7 × HCO3− + 20 (± 5), or rises 0.6–0.7 mmHg for each 1 mmol/L rise in HCO3− above 24; it rarely passes 55 mmHg, because hypoxia drives breathing. A pCO2 higher than expected means a respiratory acidosis as well, and one lower than expected a respiratory alkalosis as well, as in aspirin poisoning.

Reading a blood gas

On an ABG or VBG, the first step is whether the pH is low (acidemia, below 7.35) or high (alkalemia, above 7.45). The second is which value explains it: HCO3− moving the same way as pH means a metabolic disorder, and pCO2 moving the opposite way means a respiratory one. The third is whether compensation is as expected; if it is not, a second disorder is present. A metabolic acidosis is then classified by its anion gap, and a metabolic alkalosis by its urine Cl−.
