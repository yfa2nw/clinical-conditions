Nephron Structure

Overview

Each kidney holds about 1 million nephrons, each a filter, the glomerulus, followed by a tubule that changes what is filtered: the glomeruli and convoluted tubules lie in the cortex, and the loops of Henle and collecting ducts run down into the medulla, ending at the papillae.

  • 1GlomerulusA tuft of fenestrated capillaries between the afferent and efferent arterioles, supported by mesangial cells
  • 2Bowman capsulePodocytes form its inner, visceral layer on the capillaries; its outer, parietal layer is simple squamous; filtrate collects in the space between
  • 3Proximal convoluted tubuleIn the cortex; the main site of reabsorption
  • 4Thin descending limbRuns down into the medulla; passes water
  • 5Thin ascending limbTurns back up; passes no water
  • 6Thick ascending limbReturns to the cortex beside its own glomerulus, where its macula densa touches the afferent arteriole
  • 7Distal convoluted tubuleFrom the macula densa to the connecting tubule; fine-tunes the urine
  • 8Connecting tubule and collecting ductCollect from many nephrons and run through the medulla to the papillary ducts and minor calyces

Note: Collecting ducts come from the ureteric bud, like the calyces, pelvis, and ureter, whereas the nephron itself comes from the metanephric mesenchyme.

The structure of the nephron: segments, epithelia, blood supply, and the juxtaglomerular apparatus

Each kidney contains about 1 million nephrons, each a glomerulus that filters plasma followed by a tubule whose segments are built for different tasks. Glomeruli and convoluted tubules lie in the cortex, and loops of Henle and collecting ducts run into the medulla, with juxtamedullary nephrons reaching deepest. The epithelium of each segment reflects its work, from the brush border of the proximal tubule to the thin, inactive limbs and the mitochondria-packed distal tubule. Blood passes through two capillary beds in series, and the juxtaglomerular apparatus links tubule, arteriole, and renin. Here, we review the segments, their histology, the two kinds of nephron, the blood supply, and the juxtaglomerular apparatus.

Key points

  • Each kidney has about 1 million nephrons; glomeruli lie only in the cortex, and collecting ducts, from the ureteric bud, drain to the papillae.
  • The filtration barrier is fenestrated endothelium, basement membrane, and podocytes; mesangial cells support the capillaries.
  • The proximal tubule has a brush border and basal mitochondria; the thin limbs have small cells with few mitochondria; the thick ascending limb and distal tubule have many mitochondria, the distal tubule the most.
  • Juxtamedullary nephrons have long loops that build the medullary gradient; superficial nephrons have short loops.
  • Blood passes from the afferent arteriole through the glomerulus and efferent arteriole into peritubular capillaries and vasa recta.
  • The juxtaglomerular apparatus joins the macula densa, the renin-releasing granular cells, and mesangial cells.

Overview

Each kidney holds about 1 million nephrons. The glomerulus, a tuft of fenestrated capillaries between afferent and efferent arterioles, sits in the Bowman capsule, whose visceral layer of podocytes covers the capillaries and whose parietal layer is simple squamous, with filtrate collecting between them. The proximal convoluted tubule, in the cortex, leads into the thin descending limb, which runs into the medulla, and the thin ascending limb, which turns back; the thick ascending limb returns to the cortex beside its own glomerulus, where the macula densa touches the afferent arteriole, and the distal convoluted tubule runs from there to the connecting tubule and collecting duct, which drain many nephrons through the medulla to the papillae and minor calyces. Collecting ducts come from the ureteric bud, like the calyces, pelvis, and ureter.

Histology

Mesangial cells support the glomerular capillaries, which have no interstitium. The proximal tubule is simple cuboidal with a brush border of microvilli, long basal mitochondria, and many vesicles, built for bulk transport. The thin limbs are simple squamous with few mitochondria and short microvilli. The thick ascending limb is cuboidal with many mitochondria for active Na+ transport, ending in the tall, dense cells of the macula densa, and the distal tubule has tall cells with the most mitochondria in the nephron, in a palisade along its infolded base, and the highest Na+/K+-ATPase activity. The collecting duct holds principal cells and type A and B intercalated cells. Superficial nephrons have glomeruli near the surface and short loops; juxtamedullary nephrons have glomeruli near the corticomedullary junction and long loops that reach the inner medulla and build the gradient that concentrates urine.

Blood supply

The kidneys receive 20–25% of cardiac output, about 1.2 L a minute. The renal artery divides into segmental, interlobar, arcuate, and interlobular arteries, which give off afferent arterioles; blood passes through the glomerulus and leaves by the efferent arteriole, which sets glomerular pressure, into a second capillary bed, the peritubular capillaries and, beside the long loops, the vasa recta.

The juxtaglomerular apparatus

At the vascular pole, the macula densa senses NaCl delivery through NKCC2, granular cells in the afferent arteriole release renin, and mesangial cells connect them, mediating tubuloglomerular feedback, described in , and renin release, described in . Podocyte injury causes nephrotic syndrome and glomerular inflammation nephritic syndrome, as in the review of nephrotic and nephritic syndromes, and the energy-hungry proximal tubule and thick ascending limb suffer first in acute tubular necrosis.

Glomerular Filtration

The filtration barrier

Plasma is filtered through three layers that hold back cells and proteins by size and charge, so the filtrate is plasma without its large proteins.

  • ACapillary bloodCells, albumin, and immunoglobulins stay behind
  • BFenestrated endotheliumPores of about 60–100 nm keep blood cells out; a negatively charged glycocalyx covers it
  • CGlomerular basement membraneNegatively charged, so it repels proteins and lets cations through more easily
  • DPodocyte foot processesSlit diaphragms of about 14 nm add a final size barrier
  • EBowman spaceWater, ions, glucose, creatinine, and small proteins pass; filtration falls with size until none above about 90 kDa

The forces of filtration

Filtration is driven by glomerular capillary pressure, about 55 mmHg, and opposed by the pressure in Bowman space and the oncotic pressure of plasma proteins; the oncotic pressure in Bowman space is normally zero, because protein is not filtered.

Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure (PGC)Rises filtration55 mmHg
Bowman space hydrostatic pressure (PBS)Falls filtration15 mmHg
Glomerular capillary oncotic pressure (πGC)Falls filtration30 mmHg, rising along the capillary as fluid leaves

Net filtration pressure = (PGC − PBS) − (πGC − πBS), about 10 mmHg GFR = Kf × net filtration pressure, where Kf reflects the surface area and permeability of the capillary wall

Note: Because oncotic pressure rises as fluid is filtered, a higher plasma protein lowers GFR (myeloma) and a lower one raises it (nephrotic syndrome).

The kidneys filter about 120 mL a minute, roughly 180 L a day, from a renal plasma flow of about 600 mL a minute, and excrete only 1.5–2 L of urine.

Clearance and its formulas

Clearance is the volume of plasma cleared of a substance each minute; a substance that is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted has a clearance equal to GFR.

  • ClearanceC = (U × V) ÷ PU, urine concentration; V, urine flow; P, plasma concentration
  • GFR from inulinInulin is filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted, so its clearance equals GFR; accurate but impractical
  • GFR from creatinineCreatinine is made at a steady rate by muscle and filtered, but also slightly secreted, so creatinine clearance overestimates GFR by about 10–20%; eGFR (CKD-EPI) and the Cockcroft-Gault estimate of creatinine clearance avoid a urine collection
  • RPF from PAHRPF = (UPAH × V) ÷ PPAHPAH is filtered and secreted so completely that one pass clears it
  • RBFRBF = RPF ÷ (1 − hematocrit)
  • Filtration fractionFF = GFR ÷ RPFAbout 20%
  • Filtered load, excretion, and reabsorptionFiltered load = GFR × P; excretion = U × V; reabsorbed = filtered load − excretionA clearance below the inulin clearance means net reabsorption, above it net secretion
  • FENaFENa = (UNa × PCr) ÷ (PNa × UCr) × 100The share of filtered Na+ excreted; below 1% means the tubules are holding Na+, as in prerenal AKI

Estimating GFR: eGFR

Measuring GFR directly is impractical, so it is estimated from serum creatinine, which muscle makes at a steady rate and the kidney filters; the estimate is only as good as its assumptions about how much creatinine the body makes.

How it is calculated

  • CKD-EPI (2021)Uses serum creatinine, age, and sex, without race; a version adds cystatin C, and using both gives the most accurate estimate. Reported in mL/min/1.73 m², indexed to an average body surface area
  • Cockcroft-GaultCrCl = (140 − age) × weight in kg × 1.23 ÷ creatinine in µmol/L, × 0.85 for womenEstimates creatinine clearance in mL/min, not indexed to body size; still used for drug dosing
  • Cystatin CMade by all nucleated cells at a steady rate and not tied to muscle mass, so it helps when creatinine misleads

What it assumes

  • Steady stateCreatinine must be stable; in AKI it lags behind the true GFR
  • Average muscle mass and dietFor the person's age and sex; creatinine comes from muscle and from meat
  • Unchanged secretionA little creatinine is secreted; drugs that block this raise creatinine without changing GFR
  • Average body sizeIndexed to 1.73 m², so very large or small people may need an unindexed estimate for drug dosing

Autoregulation

GFR and renal blood flow stay nearly constant across a wide range of blood pressure, protecting the glomerulus from high pressure and keeping filtration going when pressure falls.

  • Myogenic responseA rise in pressure stretches the afferent arteriole, which depolarizes and constricts; a local reflex, independent of nerves
  • Tubuloglomerular feedbackMore filtration delivers more NaCl to the macula densa, which senses it through NKCC2 and releases ATP, adenosine, and thromboxane to constrict the afferent arteriole; loop diuretics blunt it
  • Angiotensin IIConstricts the efferent arteriole, holding GFR up when renal perfusion falls
  • Prostaglandins (PGE2 and PGI2)Dilate the afferent arteriole, keeping blood flowing into the glomerulus
  • Sympathetic nervesConstrict the renal vessels when volume falls sharply, lowering RBF and GFR

Note: NSAIDs remove the afferent dilation and ACEi or ARBs remove the efferent constriction, so together they can stop filtration in a patient who depends on both, such as one who is volume depleted.

When it fails

  • Prerenal AKILow perfusion lowers GFR, made worse by NSAIDs, ACEi, and ARBs; the tubules hold Na+, so FENa is below 1%
  • Nephrotic vs Nephritic SyndromeLoss of the charge and size barrier lets protein through, or inflammation lowers Kf
  • Postrenal AKIObstruction raises the pressure in Bowman space and lowers GFR
  • Chronic Kidney DiseaseDefined and staged by eGFR

Glomerular filtration: the barrier, the Starling forces, clearance, and autoregulation

The glomerulus filters about 120 mL of plasma a minute through a barrier of fenestrated endothelium, a negatively charged basement membrane, and podocyte slit diaphragms, which hold back cells and proteins by size and charge. Filtration is driven by glomerular capillary pressure and opposed by Bowman space pressure and plasma oncotic pressure, and the arterioles on either side set the balance. Clearance measures GFR with inulin or creatinine and renal plasma flow with PAH, and autoregulation keeps GFR stable across a wide range of blood pressure. Here, we review the barrier, the forces of filtration, GFR and renal blood flow, the clearance formulas, and autoregulation.

Key points

  • The barrier holds back molecules by size, with no filtration above about 90 kDa, and by charge, favoring cations; slit diaphragms are about 14 nm.
  • Net filtration pressure = (PGC − PBS) − (πGC − πBS), about 10 mmHg, and GFR = Kf × net filtration pressure.
  • RBF is about 1.2 L/min, RPF about 600 mL/min, GFR about 120 mL/min, and FF about 20%.
  • Afferent constriction lowers GFR and RPF without changing FF; efferent constriction raises GFR and FF and lowers RPF.
  • Clearance = UV ÷ P: inulin gives GFR, creatinine overestimates it by 10–20%, and PAH gives RPF; FENa below 1% means the tubules are holding Na+.
  • The myogenic response and tubuloglomerular feedback autoregulate GFR; angiotensin II constricts the efferent and prostaglandins dilate the afferent arteriole.

The filtration barrier

Plasma passes through fenestrated endothelium, with pores of about 60–100 nm under a negatively charged glycocalyx, a negatively charged basement membrane that repels proteins and favors cations, and podocyte foot processes with slit diaphragms of about 14 nm. Water, ions, glucose, creatinine, and small proteins pass, filtration falls with size, and nothing above about 90 kDa is filtered, so albumin and immunoglobulins stay in the blood.

The forces of filtration

Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure, about 55 mmHg, drives filtration; Bowman space hydrostatic pressure, about 15 mmHg, and glomerular capillary oncotic pressure, about 30 mmHg and rising along the capillary, oppose it, and Bowman space oncotic pressure is normally zero. Net filtration pressure, (PGC − PBS) − (πGC − πBS), is about 10 mmHg, and GFR is Kf, reflecting surface area and permeability, times net filtration pressure. A high plasma protein, as in myeloma, therefore lowers GFR, and a low one, as in nephrotic syndrome, raises it.

GFR and renal blood flow

Renal blood flow is about 1.2 L a minute, 20–25% of cardiac output, renal plasma flow about 600 mL a minute, and GFR about 120 mL a minute, roughly 180 L a day, of which only 1.5–2 L becomes urine; the filtration fraction is about 20%. Afferent constriction, as with NSAIDs, lowers GFR and RPF with no change in FF; efferent constriction, as with angiotensin II, raises GFR and FF and lowers RPF; ureteric obstruction lowers GFR and FF; and volume depletion lowers RPF more than GFR, raising FF.

Clearance and its formulas

Clearance is (U × V) ÷ P. Inulin, filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted, gives GFR exactly but impractically; creatinine, made steadily by muscle, is also slightly secreted, so creatinine clearance overestimates GFR by about 10–20%, and eGFR and the Cockcroft-Gault estimate avoid a urine collection. PAH, cleared in one pass, gives RPF, and RBF is RPF ÷ (1 − hematocrit). The filtered load is GFR × P and excretion U × V, so a clearance below that of inulin means net reabsorption and above it net secretion. FENa, (UNa × PCr) ÷ (PNa × UCr) × 100, below 1% means the tubules are holding Na+, as in prerenal AKI.

Estimating GFR

The eGFR is calculated with the CKD-EPI equation from serum creatinine, age, and sex, without race, optionally with cystatin C, and reported per 1.73 m²; the Cockcroft-Gault formula, (140 − age) × weight × 1.23 ÷ creatinine in µmol/L, × 0.85 for women, estimates creatinine clearance for drug dosing. It assumes a steady creatinine, average muscle mass and diet, unchanged secretion, and average body size, so a muscular weightlifter taking creatine has a falsely low eGFR, a frail older person, an amputee, or a vegetarian a falsely high one, AKI a falsely high one early, and trimethoprim, which blocks secretion, raises creatinine by 15–22% without changing GFR; cystatin C, independent of muscle mass, gives a truer estimate in these cases.

Autoregulation

The myogenic response constricts the afferent arteriole when pressure stretches it, and in tubuloglomerular feedback the macula densa senses increased NaCl delivery through NKCC2 and releases ATP, adenosine, and thromboxane to constrict the afferent arteriole, an effect loop diuretics blunt. Angiotensin II constricts the efferent arteriole and prostaglandins dilate the afferent arteriole, preserving GFR when perfusion falls, and sympathetic nerves constrict the renal vessels in severe volume loss. NSAIDs and ACEi or ARBs remove these protections, which is why they lower GFR in prerenal AKI.

Overview

Tubular transport: the transporters and permeability of each segment

Of 160–180 L filtered each day, all but 1.5–2 L is reabsorbed, and each segment of the tubule carries its own transporters, powered by the basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase. The proximal tubule reabsorbs about 65% of Na+ and water isosmotically, with nearly all glucose and amino acids; the loop of Henle separates salt from water; the distal tubule fine-tunes Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+; and the collecting duct adjusts Na+, K+, acid, and water under hormonal control. Here, we review each segment's transporters and its permeability to water, Na+, and urea.

Key points

  • The proximal tubule reabsorbs about 65% of Na+ and water isosmotically through SGLT2, NHE3, Na+-phosphate cotransport, and aquaporin-1, and secretes organic acids, bases, and drugs.
  • Glucose carriers saturate, so glucose spills into urine above a plasma threshold; SGLT2 inhibitors reduce reabsorption by 30–60%.
  • The thin descending limb passes water only; the ascending limbs pass no water; the thick ascending limb reabsorbs about 25% of Na+ through NKCC2 and dilutes the fluid to about 100 mOsm/kg.
  • The distal tubule reabsorbs 5–10% of Na+ through NCC, Ca2+ under PTH, and Mg2+ through TRPM6, and is impermeable to water.
  • Principal cells reabsorb Na+ through ENaC and secrete K+ through ROMK under aldosterone and pass water through aquaporin-2 under ADH; type A intercalated cells secrete acid and type B secrete HCO3−.

Proximal tubule

The Na+/K+-ATPase on the blood side of every tubular cell keeps cell Na+ low and powers most other transport. The proximal tubule reabsorbs about 65% of Na+ and water, nearly all glucose and amino acids, and most HCO3−, phosphate, and K+, isosmotically, so its fluid leaves at about 300 mOsm/kg: SGLT2 takes up Na+ with glucose, NHE3 exchanges Na+ for H+, Na+ also enters with phosphate and amino acids, glucose leaves through a basolateral transporter, and water follows through aquaporin-1 and between the cells. Glucose carriers saturate, so glucose spills into urine above a plasma threshold, as in uncontrolled DM, and SGLT2 inhibitors reduce reabsorption by 30–60%. About half of urea is reabsorbed passively, PTH and FGF23 reduce phosphate reabsorption, and organic acids and bases, including PAH, creatinine, and protein-bound drugs, are secreted.

Loop of Henle and distal tubule

The loop reabsorbs 30–40% of Na+. The thin descending limb passes water through aquaporin-1 but little solute, so fluid concentrates; the thin ascending limb passes no water and loses NaCl passively; and the thick ascending limb takes up Na+, K+, and 2 Cl− through NKCC2, returns K+ through ROMK, releases Cl− through ClC-Kb, and passes Ca2+ and Mg2+ between its cells, reabsorbing about 25% of Na+ without water and diluting the fluid to about 100 mOsm/kg. The distal tubule, with the most Na+/K+-ATPase activity, reabsorbs 5–10% of Na+ through NCC, Ca2+ under PTH and calcitriol, and Mg2+ through TRPM6, and stays impermeable to water, diluting the fluid further.

Collecting duct

Principal cells reabsorb Na+ through ENaC and secrete K+ through ROMK under aldosterone, and pass water through apical aquaporin-2 under ADH and basolateral aquaporin-3 and -4; type A intercalated cells secrete acid through H+-ATPase and H+/K+-ATPase, and type B secrete HCO3− through pendrin. The inner medullary collecting duct passes urea through UT-A1 under ADH, which helps build the medullary gradient described in . The collecting duct reabsorbs the last 2–3% of Na+, and final urine osmolality, between 50 and 1200 mOsm/kg, is set here.

When it fails

Fanconi syndrome loses glucose, amino acids, phosphate, and HCO3− from a failing proximal tubule, and RTA type 1 reflects failed acid secretion, as in the review of metabolic acidosis; Bartter and Gitelman syndromes mimic loop and thiazide diuretics and Liddle syndrome keeps ENaC open, as in the review of metabolic alkalosis; and in diabetes insipidus the collecting duct stays impermeable to water.

Body fluid compartments

The Na+/K+-ATPase pumps 3 Na+ out of cells for every 2 K+ it brings in, so Na+ and Cl− dominate the ECF and K+, phosphate, and protein the ICF, as reviewed with . Because the ECF is a Na+ space, Na+ sets its size. Plasma differs from the interstitium mainly in protein, because albumin stays in the vessels and holds water there. Body water is about 75% of body weight in infants and less in older adults, who lose ECF faster for the same loss, and fat holds little water, so women and people with obesity have a lower proportion.

Water crosses cell membranes freely and moves toward the side with more dissolved particles. Gains and losses

Isotonic losses, such as diarrhea, vomiting, and bleeding, shrink only the ECF and leave plasma Na+ unchanged, and isotonic gains, such as NS or the retention of HF and cirrhosis, expand only the ECF. Losing more water than salt, through sweat, osmotic diuresis, or diabetes insipidus, shrinks both compartments and raises Na+, whereas losing more salt than water, through diuretics, adrenal insufficiency, or losses replaced with water alone, shrinks the ECF while water enters cells and Na+ falls. Gaining water, in SIADH or primary polydipsia, expands both compartments and lowers Na+, and a hypertonic gain, of 3% saline, glucose, or mannitol, expands the ECF at the expense of the cells. In third spacing, fluid moves from plasma into the interstitium without changing total body water.

Starling forces

Fluid movement across the capillary wall follows the balance of capillary and interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic pressures, net filtration = Kf × [(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)]. Capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out and rises when the ECF is expanded or venous outflow is blocked; plasma oncotic pressure, generated by albumin, holds fluid in and falls when albumin is lost, not made, or not eaten; cytokines and burns increase permeability; and lymphatics return filtered fluid and protein.

In the classic model, fluid is filtered at the arteriolar end of the capillary and reabsorbed at the venular end, but net filtration in fact continues along most capillaries, with the lymphatics returning the filtrate. The endothelium is lined by a glycocalyx of glycosaminoglycans that acts as the filtration barrier; because albumin is largely kept out of the space beneath it, the oncotic gradient that holds fluid in is the one across the glycocalyx rather than between plasma and the whole interstitium. Albumin still leaks slowly, so about half the body's albumin is extravascular and interstitial oncotic pressure is 30–60% of plasma's, and disruption of the glycocalyx, as in inflammation, increases filtration and causes edema.

Edema does not form at once. Interstitial pressure is normally −2 to −4 mmHg, held by the collagen and gel matrix, and while it stays negative the interstitium is stiff, so a little extra fluid raises its pressure and opposes further filtration; once it reaches zero, compliance rises sharply and fluid accumulates freely. The lymphatics, which carry 8–12 L of lymph a day, speed up as filtration rises and return the extra fluid, and body weight can rise by nearly 10% before pitting edema appears.

Edema and effusions

Pitting edema is free, low-protein fluid that pressure pushes aside, from high capillary pressure or low albumin, as in HF, CKD, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, IV fluid, and drugs, and in early lymphedema; it collects in dependent areas. Non-pitting edema is fluid held by protein, glycosaminoglycans, fat, or fibrosis, as in late lymphedema, which involves the feet, myxedema of hypothyroidism, with thickened yellow-orange skin and periorbital swelling, and lipedema, which spares the feet.

A transudate forms when the Starling forces change across an intact capillary wall and is low in protein and LDH, as in pleural effusions from left HF, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, low albumin, and peritoneal dialysis. An exudate forms when inflammation, infection, or malignancy makes the wall leak protein and cells, as in pneumonia, TB, malignancy, pancreatitis, SLE, and rheumatoid arthritis. By Light criteria, a pleural effusion is an exudate if the pleural fluid to serum protein ratio is above 0.5, the pleural fluid to serum LDH ratio is above 0.6, or the pleural fluid LDH is above two thirds of the upper limit of normal serum LDH, and a transudate if none is met. Regulating volume and osmolality: separate sensors, shared effectors

The body regulates the volume of the extracellular fluid through Na+ and its osmolality through water, with separate sensors and hormones: baroreceptors and the afferent arteriole drive RAAS, the sympathetic system, and natriuretic peptides, while hypothalamic osmoreceptors drive ADH and thirst. A large fall in volume also releases ADH and overrides osmolality. The systems act in sequence after hemorrhage, a water load, a salt load, or standing, and pressure natriuresis links blood pressure to Na+ excretion over the long term. Here, we review the two controls, water balance, the systems involved, their integrated responses, and pressure natriuresis.

Key points

  • Volume is set by total body Na+ and corrected by RAAS, the SNS, and natriuretic peptides; osmolality is set by water and corrected by ADH and thirst.
  • A large fall in effective volume releases ADH even when osmolality is low, which is how hypovolemia and HF cause hyponatremia.
  • Thirst begins at about 295 mOsm/kg; urine ranges from about 50 to 1200 mOsm/kg, and urine volume follows the solute excreted.
  • After hemorrhage, the SNS acts within seconds, RAAS over minutes to hours, and ADH and thirst with larger falls.
  • Pressure natriuresis raises Na+ excretion as perfusion pressure rises, mainly in the proximal tubule.

Two separate controls

Volume is set by total body Na+, sensed by baroreceptors and the afferent arteriole, and corrected by RAAS, the sympathetic system, and natriuretic peptides, with hypovolemia and hypervolemia as its failures. Osmolality is set by water, sensed by hypothalamic osmoreceptors in the subfornical organ and OVLT, and corrected by ADH and thirst, with hyponatremia and hypernatremia as its failures. A large fall in effective volume also releases ADH, even when osmolality is low, so volume overrides concentration.

Water balance

Thirst begins at about 295 mOsm/kg, and adults need about 1.6 L of water a day at minimum. Water leaves in urine, stool, sweat, and insensible losses, which can reach 1 L a day. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system: release, cascade, receptors, and feedback

Renin, released by the juxtaglomerular cells when renal perfusion or distal NaCl delivery falls or sympathetic β1 activity rises, cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, which ACE converts to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II acts on AT1 receptors to constrict arterioles, release aldosterone, increase proximal Na+ reabsorption, and drive thirst, ADH, and sympathetic outflow, while AT2 receptors oppose these effects. Aldosterone acts through the mineralocorticoid receptor on the distal nephron to retain Na+ and secrete K+ and acid. Here, we review the cascade, the control of renin, receptor signaling, aldosterone, and the feedback loops.

Key points

  • Renin, the rate-limiting enzyme, is released by low afferent stretch, low NaCl at the macula densa, β1 input, and hypokalemia, and suppressed by angiotensin II, hyperkalemia, and ANP.
  • ACE in the pulmonary endothelium converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II and breaks down bradykinin.
  • Through AT1, angiotensin II constricts arterioles (efferent more than afferent), releases aldosterone, increases proximal Na+ reabsorption through NHE3, and drives thirst, ADH, and sympathetic outflow; AT2 opposes it.
  • Aldosterone is driven by angiotensin II, high K+, and, less, ACTH, and acts on principal cells (ENaC, Na+/K+-ATPase) and intercalated cells (acid and HCO3− secretion).
  • 11β-HSD2 keeps cortisol off the mineralocorticoid receptor.

The cascade

Renin is stored in the juxtaglomerular cells of the afferent arteriole, made from prorenin, and has a half-life of 10–15 minutes; it cleaves angiotensinogen, made steadily by the liver, to angiotensin I, which has no known action. ACE on vascular endothelium, mostly in the lung, removes 2 amino acids to make angiotensin II and also breaks down bradykinin. Angiotensin II has a half-life under 60 seconds and is broken down to angiotensin III, with full aldosterone-releasing activity and about 40% of the pressor effect, and angiotensin IV. It constricts arterioles, more at the efferent arteriole, releases aldosterone from the zona glomerulosa through CYP11B2, increases proximal Na+ reabsorption through NHE3, and drives thirst, ADH release, and sympathetic outflow.

Control and signaling

Renin rises with low renal perfusion, sensed by stretch receptors in the afferent arteriole, low NaCl at the macula densa, sympathetic β1 input, especially when upright, and hypokalemia, and falls with angiotensin II, hyperkalemia, and ANP. AT1 receptors, G protein-coupled and raising Ca2+, mediate vasoconstriction, Na+ and water retention, and aldosterone release and, when overactive, inflammation, fibrosis, and remodeling; AT2 receptors, mostly fetal, oppose them. The mineralocorticoid receptor is a cytoplasmic receptor that moves to the nucleus and changes transcription.

Aldosterone and feedback

Aldosterone, made from cholesterol in the zona glomerulosa with a half-life under 20 minutes, is driven by angiotensin II, high K+, and, less, ACTH. Na+ retention restores perfusion and lowers renin, angiotensin II suppresses renin directly, and K+ both raises aldosterone and lowers renin. Primary hyperaldosteronism has a high aldosterone-to-renin ratio and renovascular hypertension raises both, as in the review of hypoaldosteronism and hyperaldosteronism.

High-pressure baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch provide rapid control, and low-pressure receptors in the atria, ventricles, and pulmonary vessels sense volume. Acid-base balance: buffers, the chemoreceptor reflex, renal acid excretion, and the effects of pH

Plasma pH is set by the ratio of HCO3− to CO2, buffered first by HCO3−, hemoglobin, phosphate, proteins, and bone. The lungs correct a change in pH within hours through central and peripheral chemoreceptors that adjust ventilation, while the kidney reclaims filtered HCO3− and makes new HCO3− to replace what fixed acids consume, chiefly through ammoniagenesis, over days. Acidemia and alkalemia act on the heart, the vessels, O2 delivery, and the distribution of K+ and Ca2+. Here, we review acid production and buffering, the respiratory and renal responses, the effects of pH, the primary disorders and their expected compensation, and a stepwise reading of the blood gas.

Key points

  • pH = 6.1 + log (HCO3− ÷ (0.03 × pCO2)): pH depends on the ratio, so compensation moves the other value in the same direction.
  • Metabolism makes large amounts of CO2, which the lungs remove, and about 1 mmol/kg a day of fixed acid, which only the kidney can excrete.
  • Central chemoreceptors sense CSF H+, which follows pCO2, and peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid bodies sense blood H+, pCO2, and low pO2.
  • The proximal tubule reclaims 70–80% of filtered HCO3− through NHE3, carbonic anhydrase, and NBCe1; new HCO3− is made as titratable acid and, mainly, as NH4+.
  • Ammoniagenesis turns glutamine into 2 NH4+ and 2 new HCO3−; NH4+ is concentrated in the medulla by the thick ascending limb and trapped in the collecting duct; acidosis and hypokalemia increase it and hyperkalemia reduces it.
  • Acidemia weakens the heart, blunts catecholamines, and shifts the O2 curve right; alkalemia reduces cerebral blood flow, shifts the curve left, and lowers ionized Ca2+.

Acids and buffers

Plasma pH depends on the ratio of HCO3−, regulated by the kidney, to CO2, regulated by the lungs, as expressed by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, pH = 6.1 + log (HCO3− ÷ (0.03 × pCO2)), with normal values of pH 7.35–7.45, pCO2 35–45 mmHg, and HCO3− 22–26 mmol/L. Metabolism produces large amounts of CO2, which the lungs remove within minutes, and about 1 mmol/kg a day of fixed acid, mostly sulfuric and phosphoric acid from dietary protein, which only the kidney can excrete. HCO3− and CO2 form the main extracellular buffer and work despite a pKa of 6.1 because the CO2 made by buffering is breathed off. Hemoglobin buffers H+ in red cells, deoxygenated hemoglobin binding more H+ and CO2; phosphate buffers mainly in urine as titratable acid; proteins buffer inside cells and in plasma; and bone buffers chronic acidosis at the cost of its mineral.

The respiratory response

Respiratory centers in the pons and medulla adjust ventilation to chemoreceptor input. Central chemoreceptors in the medulla sense H+ in the CSF, which follows pCO2 because CO2 crosses the blood-brain barrier while H+ and HCO3− do not; peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid bodies sense blood H+, pCO2, and low pO2 and lead the response to a metabolic acidosis, producing deep, rapid Kussmaul breathing that lowers pCO2 within hours. In metabolic alkalosis, hypoventilation raises pCO2, but rarely beyond 55 mmHg, because the falling pO2 drives breathing. In chronic CO2 retention, the kidney raises HCO3− and the central response adapts, so ventilation leans on the drive from low pO2 and excessive O2 can worsen hypercapnia; an SpO2 of about 92% is a reasonable target. When lung or chest wall disease prevents ventilation from rising, the high pCO2 itself becomes a respiratory acidosis.

The renal response

The kidney reclaims nearly all filtered HCO3− and makes new HCO3− by secreting H+. In the proximal tubule, NHE3 secretes H+ in exchange for Na+, carbonic anhydrase on the brush border turns filtered HCO3− and H+ into CO2 and water, which enter the cell, carbonic anhydrase in the cell reforms H+ and HCO3−, and NBCe1 carries HCO3− with Na+ into the blood. About 70–80% of filtered HCO3− is reclaimed here, 10–15% in the thick ascending limb, and the rest distally; acetazolamide blocks carbonic anhydrase, and proximal failure is RTA type 2. Each H+ excreted on a urine buffer adds a new HCO3−: titratable acid on phosphate accounts for one third to one half of acid excretion normally, and NH4+ for the rest, with the greater capacity to rise in acidosis.

In ammoniagenesis, glutaminase in the proximal tubule breaks down glutamine into 2 NH4+ and α-ketoglutarate, which is metabolized to 2 new HCO3−. The thick ascending limb reabsorbs NH4+ and concentrates it in the medullary interstitium by countercurrent multiplication, NH3 diffuses into the collecting duct through RhCG, and H+ secreted by α-intercalated cells traps it as NH4+, which leaves in the urine. NH4+ returned to the blood is made into urea by the liver, consuming HCO3−, so new HCO3− is gained only when NH4+ is excreted. Acidosis increases ammoniagenesis, supported by angiotensin II, aldosterone, and endothelin-1; hypokalemia increases NH4+ uptake into collecting duct cells in place of K+, sustaining an alkalosis; and hyperkalemia reduces ammoniagenesis and NH3 transport, causing the acidosis of RTA type 4. The urine anion gap estimates urine NH4+.

In the collecting duct, α-intercalated cells secrete H+ through an H+-ATPase and an H+/K+-ATPase and fail in RTA type 1, while β-intercalated cells secrete HCO3− through pendrin in exchange for Cl−, a defense against alkalosis that needs Cl−. Acid excretion therefore rises with a high pCO2, the renal compensation for respiratory acidosis, with a low K+, with aldosterone, and with volume and Cl− depletion, which keep a chloride-responsive alkalosis going until saline is given, and falls with a high K+ and with acetazolamide.

The effects of pH

Acidemia weakens cardiac contraction, blunts the response to catecholamines, and causes arrhythmias and hypotension; a high pCO2 dilates cerebral vessels and can raise ICP, and causes confusion, myoclonus, and seizures. The O2 dissociation curve shifts right, the Bohr effect, so tissues receive O2 more easily, K+ moves out of cells, and ionized Ca2+ rises slightly. Alkalemia weakens contraction and causes arrhythmias, worse with a low K+, reduces cerebral blood flow, shifts the curve left so hemoglobin holds O2 more tightly, moves K+ into cells, lowers ionized Ca2+, and increases neuromuscular excitability, with tingling, spasm, and tetany. Many effects of a metabolic alkalosis come from the hypovolemia and K+ and Cl− depletion that accompany it.

Primary disorders and expected compensation

Metabolic acidosis lowers HCO3− and is compensated within hours by hyperventilation, with an expected pCO2 of 1.5 × HCO3− + 8 (± 2), the Winter formula; metabolic alkalosis raises HCO3− and is compensated by hypoventilation, with an expected pCO2 of 0.7 × HCO3− + 20 (± 5). Respiratory acidosis raises pCO2, and HCO3− rises about 1 mmol/L for each 10 mmHg when acute and about 3.5 when chronic; respiratory alkalosis lowers pCO2, and HCO3− falls about 2 mmol/L for each 10 mmHg when acute and about 4–5 when chronic. A pCO2 above the expected value adds a respiratory acidosis, one below it a respiratory alkalosis, as in aspirin poisoning, and an HCO3− outside the expected range in a respiratory disorder adds a metabolic disorder.

Reading a blood gas

The pH defines acidemia, below 7.35, or alkalemia, above 7.45; the value that explains it identifies the primary disorder, metabolic when HCO3− moves with the pH and respiratory when pCO2 moves against it; the compensation is compared with the expected value to detect a second disorder; and a metabolic acidosis is then characterized by its anion gap and a metabolic alkalosis by its urine Cl−, as set out in the reviews of metabolic acidosis, metabolic alkalosis, and the acid-base workup.
