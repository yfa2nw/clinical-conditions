Prerenal AKI

Pathophysiology

Too little blood reaches the glomeruli at too low a pressure, so GFR falls although the kidney itself is healthy; the tubules still work and hold on to Na+, water, and urea, so the urine is concentrated and low in Na+, and the injury reverses once perfusion returns.

  1. Perfusion fallsBaroreceptors raise sympathetic tone; the afferent arteriole releases renin; ADH rises
  2. Autoregulation holds GFRAngiotensin II constricts the efferent arteriole and prostaglandins dilate the afferent, until systolic pressure falls below about 80 mmHg
  3. GFR fallsFurther angiotensin II and sympathetic drive constrict the afferent arteriole too
  4. The tubules conserveNa+ and water are reabsorbed avidly, and urea follows passively in the proximal tubule, raising the urea-to-creatinine ratio
  5. Prolonged hypoperfusionTubular cells are injured: prerenal AKI becomes ATN, which no longer responds to fluid

Note: Patients with CKD have weaker autoregulation, so a smaller fall in perfusion lowers their GFR.

  • The heart and vessels
  • 2Low cardiac outputCardiogenic shock, massive PE, and acute coronary syndrome
  • 3VasodilationSeptic shock, anaphylaxis, and anesthesia
  • 4HF (cardiorenal syndrome)Low output and high venous pressure both lower renal perfusion; diuresis is used cautiously
  • 5Cirrhosis (hepatorenal syndrome)Splanchnic vasodilation underfills the arteries and the kidneys constrict their vessels intensely; triggered by bleeding, overdiuresis, large-volume paracentesis without albumin, infection, and NSAIDs (see Acute liver failure and cirrhosis)
  • The kidney's own vessels
  • 6Afferent constrictionNSAIDs remove the prostaglandins that dilate the afferent arteriole; iodinated contrast, amphotericin B, and CNIs constrict it
  • 7Efferent dilationACEi and ARBs remove the angiotensin II that holds glomerular pressure up
  • 8Renal artery stenosis and dissectionBilateral stenosis, or stenosis of a single working kidney, especially once ACEi or ARBs start; aortic dissection
  • 9Abdominal compartment syndromeRaised intra-abdominal pressure compresses the renal veins and kidneys and lowers renal perfusion

Note: Prerenal AKI and ATN together cause about three quarters of hospital AKI; prerenal AKI alone about 21%.

Symptoms and complications

Features come from the cause, such as thirst, postural dizziness, and low output, or edema in HF and cirrhosis, and from the AKI itself, as on .

  • HypovolemiaThirst, dry mucosa, low skin turgor, tachycardia, and a postural fall in blood pressure
  • Low urine outputConcentrated urine in small volumes
  • Progression to ATNIf perfusion is not restored, tubular injury follows and recovery takes weeks

Prerenal AKI: a healthy kidney without enough perfusion

Prerenal AKI follows reduced perfusion of otherwise healthy kidneys, from fluid loss, low cardiac output, vasodilation, low effective volume in HF and cirrhosis, or drugs and lesions that act on the renal vessels. Autoregulation holds GFR until perfusion falls further, and the intact tubules conserve Na+, water, and urea, producing bland, concentrated, Na+-poor urine. Restoring perfusion reverses it, but prolonged hypoperfusion causes ATN. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes, presentation, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider its relationships to ATN, hypovolemia, and cirrhosis.

Key points

  • Autoregulation, through efferent constriction by angiotensin II and afferent dilation by prostaglandins, holds GFR until systolic pressure falls below about 80 mmHg.
  • Causes are fluid loss; low cardiac output and vasodilation; HF and cirrhosis (cardiorenal and hepatorenal syndromes); NSAIDs, contrast, and CNIs (afferent constriction); ACEi and ARBs (efferent dilation); renal artery stenosis; and abdominal compartment syndrome.
  • The urine is bland, with urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L, FENa below 1% (FEUrea below 35% with diuretics), osmolality above 500 mOsm/kg, and a urea-to-creatinine ratio above 20 in US units.
  • Creatinine returning toward baseline within 24–72 hours of restoring volume is the best confirmation.
  • Treatment restores perfusion and stops NSAIDs, ACEi, ARBs, and diuretics; low-dose dopamine does not help.

Pathophysiology

When perfusion falls, baroreceptors raise sympathetic tone and the afferent arteriole releases renin, and ADH rises. Angiotensin II constricts the efferent arteriole and prostaglandins dilate the afferent, holding GFR until systolic pressure falls below about 80 mmHg; further angiotensin II and sympathetic drive then constrict the afferent arteriole too and GFR falls. The tubules still work, reabsorbing Na+ and water avidly, with urea following passively in the proximal tubule, so the urea-to-creatinine ratio rises. Patients with CKD autoregulate less well, and prolonged hypoperfusion injures the tubules, turning prerenal AKI into ATN.

Etiology

Fluid loss from bleeding, burns, vomiting, diarrhea, high ostomy output, or overdiuresis lowers volume; cardiogenic shock, massive PE, and acute coronary syndrome lower output; and septic shock, anaphylaxis, and anesthesia dilate vessels. In HF, low output and high venous pressure lower renal perfusion (cardiorenal syndrome), and in cirrhosis, splanchnic vasodilation underfills the arteries and the kidneys constrict their vessels (hepatorenal syndrome), often triggered by bleeding, overdiuresis, paracentesis without albumin, infection, or NSAIDs. NSAIDs remove afferent prostaglandins and contrast, amphotericin B, and CNIs constrict the afferent arteriole, whereas ACEi and ARBs remove efferent angiotensin II; bilateral renal artery stenosis, or stenosis of a single working kidney, aortic dissection, and abdominal compartment syndrome also lower renal perfusion. Prerenal AKI causes about 21% of hospital AKI.

Presentation and diagnosis

Features come from the cause and from the AKI itself, as in the review of : thirst, dry mucosa, tachycardia, and a postural fall in pressure in hypovolemia, and edema in HF and cirrhosis, with small volumes of concentrated urine. The urinalysis is normal or shows hyaline casts, urine Na+ is below 20 mmol/L, FENa below 1%, or FEUrea below 35% in patients taking diuretics, urine osmolality above 500 mOsm/kg, and the urea-to-creatinine ratio above 20 in US units; creatinine returning toward baseline within 24–72 hours of restoring volume is the best test, except in hepatorenal and cardiorenal syndromes. Cause-specific tests include orthostatic vital signs, cardiac tests, cultures and lactate, the drug list, renal artery imaging, and bladder pressure.

Management

Isotonic saline or 4% albumin, similar in outcome, restores volume, followed by maintenance fluid with care to avoid overload; shock and low output are treated by their cause with vasopressors and inotropes, and HF with cautious diuresis. Hepatorenal syndrome is treated by stopping diuretics and nephrotoxins and giving albumin and vasoconstrictors such as terlipressin, with transplant referral. NSAIDs are stopped and ACEi and ARBs held until recovery, renal artery stenosis may need revascularization, and abdominal compartment syndrome needs decompression when severe. Low-dose dopamine does not protect the kidney.

Relationships to other conditions

. Prolonged prerenal AKI becomes ATN; in prerenal AKI the tubules conserve Na+ and concentrate urine, whereas in ATN they cannot, and fluid no longer helps.

. True volume loss is the commonest prerenal cause and is treated with isotonic fluid, whereas the low effective volume of HF and cirrhosis is not corrected by giving fluid.

Intrarenal AKI

Pathophysiology

The injury lies in the kidney itself, in its vessels, glomeruli, tubules, or interstitium, so the tubules cannot conserve Na+ or concentrate urine, and restoring perfusion does not reverse it; injured tubules release vasoconstrictors that lower GFR further.

  • 1Small vesselsEndothelial injury and clots in thrombotic microangiopathy; cholesterol emboli; malignant hypertension
  • 2GlomerulusImmune injury and inflammation lower filtration and let blood and protein through
  • 3TubulesIschemia or toxins kill tubular cells, effacing the brush border; casts, crystals, pigments, or light chains block the lumen
  • 4InterstitiumAn immune reaction, usually to a drug, infiltrates the interstitium with white cells

Note: ATN causes about 45% of hospital AKI, glomerulonephritis and vasculitis about 4%, AIN about 2%, and atheroemboli about 1%.

Intrarenal AKI: injury to the vessels, glomeruli, tubules, or interstitium

Intrarenal AKI arises from injury within the kidney: to the small vessels, the glomeruli, the tubules, or the interstitium. ATN, from ischemia or toxins, is the commonest cause of hospital AKI, and acute interstitial nephritis, glomerulonephritis, and thrombotic microangiopathy are the others. The injured tubules cannot conserve Na+ or concentrate urine, so FENa and urine osmolality separate it from prerenal AKI, and the urine sediment points to the compartment. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes by compartment, clues, diagnosis, and treatment.

Key points

  • Intrarenal AKI injures the vessels, glomeruli, tubules, or interstitium; ATN causes about 45% of hospital AKI.
  • ATN follows prolonged ischemia, sepsis, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, amphotericin B, contrast, myoglobin, or hemoglobin; light chains, urate, and ethylene glycol obstruct the tubules.
  • AIN follows β-lactams, NSAIDs, PPIs, 5-ASA, infection, or autoimmune disease.
  • Muddy brown casts suggest ATN, white cell casts and sterile pyuria AIN, and red cell casts glomerulonephritis.
  • FENa above 2% and osmolality below 450 mOsm/kg separate ATN from prerenal AKI; biopsy is reserved for unexplained or rapidly progressive AKI.

Pathophysiology and etiology

Injury within the kidney prevents the tubules from conserving Na+ or concentrating urine, and injured tubules release vasoconstrictors, so restoring perfusion does not reverse it. In the tubules, ischemia or toxins kill cells and efface the brush border, as in ATN from prolonged prerenal AKI, sepsis, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, amphotericin B, pentamidine, contrast, rhabdomyolysis, and hemolysis, and light chains, urate, and ethylene glycol obstruct the lumen. In the interstitium, AIN follows β-lactams, NSAIDs, PPIs, 5-ASA, infection, SLE, or IgG4-related disease. Glomerular causes include anti-GBM disease, ANCA vasculitis, lupus nephritis, post-infectious and IgA disease, and cryoglobulinemia, and vascular causes thrombotic microangiopathy, atheroemboli, and malignant hypertension. Contrast-associated AKI is most likely in CKD, DM, HF, and volume depletion, and rhabdomyolysis gives a very high CK and a dipstick positive for blood with few red cells. ATN causes about 45% of hospital AKI, glomerulonephritis and vasculitis about 4%, AIN about 2%, and atheroemboli about 1%.

Clues and diagnosis

ATN follows a clear insult with a steadily rising creatinine; AIN follows a new drug by 1–2 weeks, with rash, fever, eosinophilia, and sometimes polyuria; glomerular disease brings hematuria, protein, edema, hypertension, and systemic signs; and vascular disease brings livedo and digital ischemia after a procedure, or anemia and low platelets. FENa above 2% and urine osmolality below 450 mOsm/kg separate ATN from prerenal AKI, as in the review of . The sediment shows muddy brown casts and tubular cells in ATN, though it may be normal early; white cell casts, sterile pyuria, and eosinophiluria in AIN; and red cell casts and dysmorphic red cells in glomerulonephritis. CK, urate, SPEP and UPEP, serology, complement, a blood film, and biopsy complete the workup.

Management

The cause is treated and the kidney supported while it recovers, which in ATN takes weeks to months: the insult is removed and further nephrotoxins avoided, rhabdomyolysis is treated with large volumes of IV fluid, and contrast-associated AKI is prevented by hydration and the lowest dose. AIN is treated by stopping the drug and with steroids, glomerulonephritis and vasculitis with immunosuppression, and thrombotic microangiopathy with plasma exchange or complement blockade, as described in , , and .

Postrenal AKI

Pathophysiology

Obstruction to urine flow raises the pressure in the tubules and Bowman space, so GFR falls; it causes AKI only when both kidneys, or a single working kidney, are blocked, and it reverses if relieved early but scars the kidney if it lasts.

  1. Urine cannot drainPressure rises up the urinary tract
  2. Bowman space pressure risesWithin hours, opposing filtration, so GFR falls
  3. The kidney is injuredRenal vasoconstriction, inflammation, tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis
  4. Weeks to monthsBladder atony, chronic interstitial nephritis, secondary FSGS, and ESRD
  5. After reliefPost-obstructive diuresis in up to 50%

Note: A gradual blockage of one ureter may cause no AKI, because the other kidney compensates.

Etiology

  • Below the bladder
  • 1Urethra and prostateBPH, the commonest cause in older men; urethral stricture, phimosis, and posterior urethral valves; prostate cancer
  • The bladder
  • 2Bladder outlet and functionNeurogenic bladder, clots from bladder or urinary tract cancer, bladder tumors, fecal impaction, and pelvic organ prolapse
  • Both ureters, or a single kidney
  • 3StonesThe commonest cause in younger patients; struvite and cystine stones grow fast
  • 4Masses and fibrosisPelvic masses and cancers, the commonest cause in older women; retroperitoneal fibrosis or lymph nodes
  • 5Pregnancy and pelvic diseaseThe gravid uterus and endometriosis compress the ureters

Symptoms and complications

  • Lower tractDifficulty starting to void, a weak stream, urinary retention, and a distended, tender bladder
  • Urine outputSudden anuria suggests complete obstruction; alternating polyuria and low output suggest a partial one
  • Upper tractFlank pain with stones; often painless when slow, as with fibrosis or tumor
  • InfectionAn infected, obstructed kidney needs urgent drainage; antibiotics alone fail
  • Post-obstructive diuresisUrine output of 200 mL/h or more for 2 hours, or over 3 L in 24 hours, after relief; it can cause hypovolemia and losses of Na+, K+, phosphate, and Mg2+

Postrenal AKI: obstruction to urine flow

Postrenal AKI follows obstruction to urine flow at the urethra, bladder, or both ureters, or the ureter of a single working kidney. Rising pressure in Bowman space lowers GFR within hours, and lasting obstruction causes vasoconstriction, tubular atrophy, and fibrosis. It is found with a bladder scan, a catheter, and ultrasound for hydronephrosis, and relieved by catheter, stent, or nephrostomy, after which post-obstructive diuresis may follow. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes by level, presentation, diagnosis, and treatment.

Key points

  • Obstruction raises Bowman space pressure and lowers GFR within hours; AKI needs both kidneys, or a single working kidney, to be blocked.
  • BPH is the commonest cause in older men, pelvic masses in older women, and stones in younger patients; retroperitoneal fibrosis, clots, and neurogenic bladder also obstruct.
  • A bladder scan and catheter find bladder outlet obstruction; ultrasound shows hydronephrosis.
  • An infected, obstructed kidney needs urgent stent or nephrostomy.
  • Post-obstructive diuresis, 200 mL/h or more for 2 hours or over 3 L in 24 hours, follows relief in up to 50% and needs electrolyte monitoring every 12–24 hours.

Pathophysiology and etiology

Obstruction raises pressure up the urinary tract and in Bowman space within hours, opposing filtration, and lasting obstruction causes renal vasoconstriction, inflammation, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and eventually bladder atony, chronic interstitial nephritis, secondary FSGS, and ESRD. A gradual blockage of one ureter may cause no AKI. BPH is the commonest cause in older men, with stricture, phimosis, valves, and prostate cancer; at the bladder, neurogenic bladder, clots, tumors, fecal impaction, and prolapse; and at both ureters, stones, the commonest cause in younger patients, pelvic masses, the commonest in older women, retroperitoneal fibrosis, pregnancy, and endometriosis.

Presentation and diagnosis

Lower tract obstruction causes difficulty voiding, retention, and a distended bladder; sudden anuria suggests complete obstruction; stones cause flank pain, whereas slow obstruction is often painless. A bladder scan and a catheter that drains a large volume find bladder outlet obstruction, with digital rectal examination and PSA when cancer is suspected, and ultrasound shows hydronephrosis, with CT for stones and masses, as in the review of .

Management

A urethral catheter, with a Coudé tip for a large prostate or a suprapubic catheter if it cannot pass, relieves outlet obstruction, followed by treatment of BPH; neurogenic bladder may need intermittent self-catheterization; and ureteral obstruction needs a stent or percutaneous nephrostomy, urgently if infected. Post-obstructive diuresis, 200 mL/h or more for 2 hours or over 3 L in 24 hours, follows relief in up to 50%, most likely when 1500 mL or more drains; Na+, K+, phosphate, Mg2+, urea, and creatinine are checked every 12–24 hours and losses replaced with care.

AKI: Comparison and Workup

Definition and staging

AKI is a sudden fall in kidney function, defined by KDIGO as any of: a rise in creatinine of 26.5 µmol/L or more within 48 hours, a rise to 1.5 times baseline or more within 7 days, or urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours.

Phases

  1. InitiationThe insult; GFR begins to fall, and creatinine may still be normal
  2. Oliguric (maintenance) phaseLow urine output, with overload, hyperkalemia, and acidosis most likely; dialysis is most often needed here
  3. Diuretic (polyuric) phaseGFR returns before the tubules can concentrate urine, so large volumes and Na+, K+, and water are lost
  4. RecoveryOver weeks to months, often to a new, higher baseline creatinine

  • OliguriaFavors AKI over CKD
  • Sudden anuriaComplete obstruction, severe glomerulonephritis, or a blocked renal artery
  • PolyuriaA concentrating defect, as in AIN, or post-obstructive diuresis

Acute kidney injury: staging, phases, the urine indices, and shared management

Acute kidney injury is a sudden fall in kidney function, defined and staged by KDIGO from creatinine and urine output. It is often silent but causes fluid overload, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, uremia, and drug toxicity, and carries a high mortality. The urine indices and sediment separate prerenal AKI, in which the tubules conserve Na+ and water, from ATN, in which they cannot, and ultrasound finds obstruction. Management treats the cause, protects the kidney, controls K+, fluid, and acid, and uses dialysis by the AEIOU indications. Here, we review the definition, staging, phases, complications, comparison, workup, and shared treatment of AKI.

Key points

  • KDIGO defines AKI as a creatinine rise of 26.5 µmol/L or more within 48 hours, a rise to 1.5 times baseline within 7 days, or urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours, staged 1–3.
  • Phases run from initiation through an oliguric phase, with most complications, and a diuretic phase to recovery over weeks to months.
  • Prerenal AKI has urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L, FENa below 1% (FEUrea below 35% with diuretics), osmolality above 500 mOsm/kg, and a urea-to-creatinine ratio above 20 in US units; ATN has FENa above 2%, osmolality below 450, and muddy brown casts.
  • The workup checks for immediate danger, volume, drugs, obstruction, the urine, and the blood, with biopsy for unexplained AKI.
  • Treatment restores perfusion, stops nephrotoxins, adjusts doses, manages K+, fluid, and acid, and dialyzes for acidosis, electrolytes, intoxication, overload, or uremia.

Definition, staging, and phases

KDIGO defines AKI as a creatinine rise of 26.5 µmol/L or more within 48 hours, a rise to 1.5 times baseline or more within 7 days, or urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours. Stage 1 is 1.5–1.9 times baseline or a rise of 26.5 µmol/L, stage 2 is 2.0–2.9 times, and stage 3 is 3 times, 354 µmol/L or more, or dialysis, with urine output thresholds of 6–12 hours, 12 hours or more, and 24 hours below 0.3 mL/kg/h or 12 hours of anuria. Creatinine lags behind injury, and cystatin C rises sooner. AKI passes through initiation, an oliguric phase in which overload, hyperkalemia, and acidosis are most likely, a diuretic phase in which recovering filtration outpaces tubular concentration, and recovery over weeks to months. Oliguria favors AKI, sudden anuria suggests complete obstruction, severe glomerulonephritis, or arterial occlusion, and polyuria a concentrating defect or post-obstructive diuresis.

Complications

AKI is often silent but causes fluid overload with edema, pulmonary edema, and HF; hyperkalemia with arrhythmias; metabolic acidosis; hyperphosphatemia, hyponatremia, and hypocalcemia; uremia, with nausea, vomiting, anorexia, pruritus, lethargy, confusion, asterixis, seizures, pericarditis, and platelet dysfunction; drug toxicity; and infection. In-hospital mortality is 40–50%, and over 50% in ICU, and 12–15% of patients later need long-term dialysis.

Comparison and diagnosis

Prerenal AKI has a urea-to-creatinine ratio above 20 in US units, urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L, FENa below 1%, FEUrea below 35%, osmolality above 500 mOsm/kg, bland sediment, and a response to fluid within 24–72 hours; ATN has a ratio near 15, urine Na+ above 40, FENa above 2%, FEUrea above 50%, osmolality below 450, muddy brown casts, and a creatinine rise of about 26.5–44 µmol/L a day; and postrenal AKI shows hydronephrosis. FENa is falsely high with diuretics and low despite intrinsic disease in HF, cirrhosis, contrast-associated AKI, glomerulonephritis, and vasculitis, whereas FEUrea, handled proximally, stays useful with diuretics, and CKD weakens all the indices. The workup stages the AKI, checks K+, the ECG, the blood gas, and volume, reviews drugs and recent contrast, looks for obstruction with a bladder scan and ultrasound, examines the urine and blood, and reserves biopsy for unexplained or rapidly progressive AKI; a prior normal creatinine and normal-sized kidneys favor AKI, whereas small kidneys, anemia, and high phosphate and PTH favor CKD.

Management

A monitored fluid challenge treats likely hypovolemia, and NSAIDs, nephrotoxins, ACEi, ARBs, and contrast are avoided, with drug doses adjusted. Hyperkalemia is treated as in the review of , fluid overload with high-dose IV loop diuretics, which do not change the course, and severe acidosis with HCO3−, and protein of 1.2–1.5 g/kg a day supports the catabolic state. Dialysis, through a temporary catheter and reassessed daily, is indicated for refractory acidosis, refractory hyperkalemia, dialyzable intoxications, refractory overload, and uremia with encephalopathy, pericarditis, neuropathy, seizures, or bleeding, with CRRT for unstable patients.

Metabolic acidosis: the anion gap, the urine, and the acid behind it

Metabolic acidosis is a fall in plasma HCO3− from a gain of acid or a loss of HCO3−, to which the lungs respond by hyperventilating to lower pCO2. The anion gap divides it into a normal gap form (NAGMA), in which HCO3− lost from the kidney or gut is replaced by Cl−, and a high gap form (AGMA), in which an added acid leaves its anion in the blood. The urine anion gap, plasma K+, and urine pH separate the renal causes of NAGMA, and the delta-delta ratio and osmolal gap refine AGMA. Here, we review the mechanisms, causes, consequences, diagnosis, and management of metabolic acidosis, and consider its relationship to disorders of potassium, aldosterone, kidney function, and metabolic alkalosis.

Key points

  • The anion gap separates NAGMA, in which HCO3− is lost and replaced by Cl−, from AGMA, in which an acid adds an unmeasured anion; the gap, compensation, and delta-delta calculations are on the comparison and workup page.
  • A negative urine anion gap points to gut losses, and a positive one to RTA types 1 and 4 or CKD; K+ is low in RTA types 1 and 2 and high in type 4, and urine pH stays above 5.5 only in type 1.
  • The causes of AGMA follow GOLDMARK; an osmolal gap above 15 with an AGMA suggests methanol or ethylene glycol.
  • HCO3− is replaced in NAGMA but is not routine in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis, whose anions are metabolized back into HCO3− once the cause is treated.

Pathophysiology

Plasma pH is set by the balance H+ + HCO3− ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ CO2 + H2O, with the kidney regulating HCO3− and the lungs regulating CO2. In NAGMA, HCO3− is lost from the kidney or gut, or the kidney fails to make new HCO3−, and Cl− takes its place, giving a hyperchloremic acidosis with a normal anion gap. In AGMA, an added acid uses up HCO3−, and its anion (lactate, ketoanions, formate, glycolate, sulfate, or phosphate) stays in the blood and widens the gap. In both, the lungs hyperventilate to lower pCO2.

In acidosis, cells take up H+, which softens the fall in pH, and K+ leaves them to keep the charge balanced, causing hyperkalemia that is most marked with mineral acids such as HCl and NH4Cl. Organic acids such as lactate and ketoacids enter cells together with their H+, so they shift less K+. There are exceptions to the usual shift: in DKA, K+ is high mainly from insulin deficiency and high osmolality although total body K+ is low, and insulin moves K+ back into cells; in RTA types 1 and 2 and in diarrhea, K+ is low despite acidosis because it is lost in urine or stool faster than it shifts out of cells; and correcting any acidosis moves K+ back into cells and can unmask a low total body K+.

Etiology and mechanisms

NAGMA. In CKD, fewer nephrons make less NH4+, so less acid is excreted and less new HCO3− is made; the NAGMA of earlier CKD becomes an AGMA as GFR falls further. With a normal GFR, the renal causes are the RTAs. In RTA type 1 (distal), α-intercalated cells cannot secrete H+, so urine cannot be acidified; causes include autoimmune disease (Sjögren syndrome and SLE), lithium, amphotericin B, and toluene. In RTA type 2 (proximal), the proximal tubule, which normally reclaims 85–90% of filtered HCO3−, reclaims less, so HCO3− spills into urine until plasma HCO3− falls below the lowered threshold; causes include multiple myeloma, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide and topiramate), tenofovir, and Fanconi syndrome. RTA type 4, the most common RTA, results from too little aldosterone or resistance to it, which cuts H+ and K+ secretion, while the resulting high K+ suppresses NH4+ production; causes include diabetic nephropathy, ACEi, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, heparin, trimethoprim, and adrenal insufficiency. Among gut causes, intestinal fluid beyond the stomach is rich in HCO3−, so diarrhea lowers HCO3−, the kidney keeps Cl− in its place, and K+ is lost too; secretory diarrhea loses more HCO3− than inflammatory diarrhea. Pancreatic fistulas and other bicarbonate-rich losses act in the same way, as pancreatic, biliary, or small bowel fluid drains outside the body, and a ureterosigmoidostomy lets the colon swap urinary Cl− for HCO3−. A Cl− load causes NAGMA when large volumes of 0.9% saline, with 154 mmol/L Cl− and no HCO3−, are given, and TPN without acetate and cholestyramine can do the same.

AGMA. The causes follow GOLDMARK. Ethylene glycol in antifreeze is made into glycolic acid, which causes the acidosis, and oxalic acid, which forms calcium oxalate crystals in the tubules, causing AKI and hypocalcemia; propylene glycol is made into lactate. 5-oxoproline (pyroglutamic acid) builds up with chronic paracetamol use, especially with malnutrition or chronic illness. L-lactic acidosis is type A when too little oxygen is delivered, as in shock, sepsis, and ischemia, driving anaerobic glycolysis, and type B when there is no hypoxia but mitochondria cannot use pyruvate, as with metformin, liver failure, and thiamine deficiency. D-lactate forms in short bowel syndrome, when colonic bacteria ferment unabsorbed carbohydrate into an isomer that humans metabolize poorly. Methanol, found in windscreen washer fluid, solvents, and contaminated spirits, is made into formic acid, which damages the retina and basal ganglia. Salicylates first stimulate the respiratory center, causing a respiratory alkalosis, then uncouple oxidative phosphorylation, producing lactate and ketoacids. Renal failure, in advanced CKD or AKI, retains sulfate, phosphate, urate, and hippurate, and rhabdomyolysis releases acids and phosphate. In ketoacidosis, low insulin and high glucagon drive lipolysis and the production of β-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate, as in DKA, AKA after a binge followed by vomiting and poor intake, and starvation.

Clinical presentation and complications

Metabolic acidosis causes Kussmaul breathing, deep and rapid breaths that blow off CO2 and can tire the respiratory muscles. K+ is often high, but not always: organic acidoses shift less K+, and RTA types 1 and 2 and diarrhea cause hypokalemia. Ionized Ca2+ rises, and chronic acidosis draws on bone to buffer acid, causing osteomalacia, rickets, and stones. The heart contracts more weakly, vessels dilate, and the response to catecholamines is poor, causing hypotension, with arrhythmias. The oxygen dissociation curve shifts right, releasing more O2 to tissues. Patients have headache, lethargy, confusion, and coma, with nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and fatigue; acidosis causes insulin resistance, and in the long term, muscle wasting and faster CKD progression.

Some findings point to the cause. Hypotension, tachycardia, and dry mucous membranes suggest volume loss from diarrhea or adrenal insufficiency. Fruity breath, polyuria, and polydipsia suggest DKA; blurred vision after drinking, methanol; inebriation, flank pain, hematuria, and tetany, ethylene glycol; and tinnitus with fast breathing, aspirin. Calcium phosphate stones and nephrocalcinosis suggest RTA type 1, with alkaline urine, high urine Ca2+, and low urine citrate, and bone pain and osteomalacia with glycosuria suggest RTA type 2 with Fanconi syndrome. Pruritus, nocturia, oliguria, and asterixis suggest renal failure.

Diagnosis

On an ABG or VBG, metabolic acidosis is a pH below 7.35 with a low HCO3−, which moves the same way as the pH. The expected compensation, the anion gap and its correction for low albumin, the delta-delta ratio, and the osmolal gap are calculated as set out in the review of ; a high osmolal gap with an AGMA suggests methanol or ethylene glycol, and the anion gap sorts the causes below.

In NAGMA, the urine anion gap (urine Na+ + urine K+ − urine Cl−) separates kidney from gut. A negative gap means the kidney is excreting plenty of NH4+, which leaves with Cl−, a normal renal response to HCO3− lost elsewhere, as in diarrhea, fistulas, or saline. A positive gap means the kidney cannot excrete NH4+, as in RTA types 1 and 4 or CKD. RTA type 1 has a normal GFR, low K+, a urine pH above 5.5 even in severe acidosis, plasma HCO3− that can fall below 10 mmol/L, and a positive urine anion gap, with high urine Ca2+, low urine citrate, calcium phosphate stones, and nephrocalcinosis. RTA type 2 has a normal GFR, low K+, a urine pH below 5.5 once HCO3− is below the threshold but above 5.5 when HCO3− is given, plasma HCO3− usually 12–18 mmol/L, and a variable urine anion gap, with Fanconi syndrome (glycosuria with normal blood glucose, phosphaturia, aminoaciduria, and low urate) and bone disease. RTA type 4 has a normal or mildly low GFR, high K+, a urine pH below 5.5, plasma HCO3− usually above 17 mmol/L, and a positive urine anion gap, with diabetes or culprit drugs and high urine Na+ despite a low-salt diet. CKD has a low GFR, normal or high K+, a urine pH usually below 5.5, HCO3− that falls as GFR falls, a positive urine anion gap, and high urea and creatinine. Before diagnosing an RTA, a UTI with a urease-producing organism, which raises urine pH, and low volume, which limits distal H+ secretion, are excluded; urine Na+ should be above 25 mmol/L.

Each AGMA cause has its own findings. Glycols give a high osmolal gap early, low Ca2+, rising creatinine, calcium oxalate crystals, and hematuria, after inebriation followed by flank pain and oliguria. Oxoproline gives normal lactate, no ketones, and 5-oxoproline in urine, with chronic paracetamol use and malnutrition. L-lactate is above 4 mmol/L in shock, sepsis, ischemia, metformin use, and liver failure, and D-lactate is high in short bowel syndrome. Methanol gives a high osmolal gap early, with blurred vision, dilated pupils, and papilledema. Aspirin gives a respiratory alkalosis with an AGMA and a measurable salicylate level, with tinnitus, nausea, and fast breathing. Renal failure gives high urea, creatinine, and phosphate, with reduced urine output. DKA gives high glucose and β-hydroxybutyrate, with K+ normal or high, and ketones and glucose in urine, after missed insulin, infection, or new diabetes. AKA and starvation give low or normal glucose with high β-hydroxybutyrate, and the urine dipstick, which detects acetoacetate, can show few ketones.

Management

Treatment is aimed at the cause, with volume and K+ replaced as needed. HCO3− is given when it has truly been lost, as oral alkali in RTA and CKD, but not routinely in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis, for the reasons set out in the review of .

In CKD, oral NaHCO3 protects bone and muscle and slows progression, and dialysis is needed when the disease is advanced. RTA type 1 responds to oral alkali in modest doses, with potassium citrate also replacing K+ and preventing stones, alongside treatment of the cause. RTA type 2 needs large doses of alkali with K+, because HCO3− given above the threshold is lost in urine and carries K+ with it; a thiazide can help by shrinking volume, and phosphate and vitamin D treat Fanconi bone disease. RTA type 4 is managed by stopping culprit drugs, a low-potassium diet, and a loop or thiazide diuretic, with fludrocortisone for aldosterone deficiency without hypertension or edema. Diarrhea and fistulas are managed by treating the cause, replacing fluid, K+, and HCO3−, and controlling the fistula, and saline-induced acidosis by switching to a balanced crystalloid such as Hartmann solution or Plasma-Lyte. Toxic alcohols are treated with fomepizole, or ethanol if it is unavailable, to block alcohol dehydrogenase, with hemodialysis for severe acidosis, visual change, AKI, or instability, and thiamine and pyridoxine for ethylene glycol. Oxoproline acidosis is treated by stopping paracetamol. Type A lactic acidosis is treated by restoring perfusion with fluids, vasopressors, ABX, and source control, and type B with thiamine and by stopping metformin, with hemodialysis if severe. D-lactic acidosis is treated by addressing the short bowel cause and limiting carbohydrate reaching the colon. Aspirin poisoning is treated with activated charcoal, IV NaHCO3 to alkalinize the urine and trap salicylate there, and hemodialysis if severe. Renal failure is treated with dialysis. DKA is treated with IV fluids, then insulin once K+ is at least 3.5 mmol/L, with K+ replacement, continuing insulin until the gap closes, and AKA and starvation with IV fluids with dextrose and thiamine and gradual feeding, watching for refeeding syndrome.

Metabolic alkalosis: generation, maintenance, and the urine chloride

Metabolic alkalosis is a rise in plasma HCO3− from a loss of H+ or a gain of HCO3−, to which the lungs respond by hypoventilating to raise pCO2. Because healthy kidneys excrete extra HCO3− quickly, an alkalosis persists only while low volume, Cl−, or K+, or a mineralocorticoid effect, keeps the kidney reabsorbing it. Urine Cl− divides alkalosis into chloride-responsive forms, which correct with saline, and chloride-resistant forms, which BP, renin, and aldosterone then separate. Here, we review the mechanisms, causes, consequences, diagnosis, and management of metabolic alkalosis, and consider its relationship to disorders of potassium, aldosterone, volume, and metabolic acidosis.

Key points

  • Vomiting and NG tube suction remove HCl and generate an alkalosis; low volume, Cl−, and K+ maintain it.
  • Urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive alkalosis that corrects with saline and KCl; above 20, BP, renin, and aldosterone separate the causes.
  • Alkalosis moves K+ into cells and makes albumin bind more Ca2+, so hypokalemia and tetany with a normal total calcium are typical.
  • Acetazolamide treats an alkalosis when saline cannot be given, as in heart failure.

Pathophysiology

Plasma pH is set by the balance H+ + HCO3− ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ CO2 + H2O, with the kidney regulating HCO3− and the lungs regulating CO2. In metabolic alkalosis, H+ is lost from the stomach or kidney, or HCO3− is gained, and Cl− often falls too; the lungs hypoventilate to raise pCO2. In a chloride-responsive alkalosis, H+ and Cl− are lost from the stomach or by a diuretic that has worn off, and low volume, Cl−, and K+ keep it going; in a chloride-resistant alkalosis, the kidney loses H+ and K+ because aldosterone, or something acting like it, is high, or a diuretic is still acting.

Alkalosis moves H+ out of cells and K+ into them, causing hypokalemia that urinary K+ loss makes worse, and makes albumin give up H+ and bind more Ca2+, so ionized Ca2+ falls while total Ca2+ is unchanged.

Healthy kidneys excrete extra HCO3− quickly, so a metabolic alkalosis lasts only while something keeps the kidney reabsorbing it. After the alkalosis is generated, low volume raises angiotensin II, which increases proximal HCO3− reabsorption, and aldosterone, which increases distal H+ and K+ secretion. Low Cl− leaves too little Cl− in the collecting duct to exchange for HCO3−, so HCO3− cannot be secreted, and low K+ increases H+ secretion and NH4+ production, which makes new HCO3−. The alkalosis lasts until volume, Cl−, and K+ are replaced.

Etiology and mechanisms

Vomiting and NG tube suction remove HCl, leaving the HCO3− made by parietal cells in the blood, and the volume and Cl− loss then keeps the kidney reabsorbing HCO3−; chloride-losing diarrhea (villous adenoma and congenital chloridorrhea) and the Cl− lost in the sweat of cystic fibrosis act similarly. Loop and thiazide diuretics deliver more Na+ to the collecting duct, where aldosterone, raised by the volume loss, exchanges it for H+ and K+, and the extracellular fluid shrinks around the same amount of HCO3−, a contraction alkalosis. In primary hyperaldosteronism, an adrenal adenoma (Conn syndrome) or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia makes aldosterone regardless of renin, causing Na+ retention, hypertension, and H+ and K+ loss. In secondary hyperaldosteronism, high renin drives aldosterone, as in renal artery stenosis and renin-secreting tumors, and low effective volume in heart failure and cirrhosis does the same and keeps other alkaloses going. In apparent mineralocorticoid excess, the collecting duct behaves as if aldosterone were high: in Cushing syndrome, cortisol acts on the mineralocorticoid receptor; licorice blocks 11β-HSD2, which normally inactivates cortisol; and in Liddle syndrome, ENaC is overactive. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes are inherited losses of NKCC2 in the loop, like a lifelong loop diuretic, and of NCC in the distal tubule, like a lifelong thiazide. An alkali load, from calcium carbonate in milk-alkali syndrome, HCO3− tablets or infusions, citrate from massive transfusion, or acetate in TPN, lasts mainly when the kidney cannot excrete HCO3−, as in CKD or low volume. Finally, in chronic respiratory acidosis from COPD, the kidney retains HCO3−, and when ventilation lowers pCO2 quickly, the extra HCO3− is left behind.

Clinical presentation and complications

Metabolic alkalosis causes shallow, slow breathing that is usually mild but can worsen hypercapnia and hypoxemia in COPD or obesity hypoventilation. Hypokalemia causes muscle weakness and arrhythmias. Ionized Ca2+ falls, causing perioral numbness, paresthesias, carpopedal spasm, Chvostek and Trousseau signs, and tetany. Arrhythmias come from low K+ and low Ca2+, and vessels constrict, including in the brain. The oxygen dissociation curve shifts left, releasing less O2 to tissues, and patients may have confusion, delirium, seizures, muscle cramps, and weakness.

Some findings point to the cause. Hypotension, tachycardia, and dry mucous membranes suggest volume loss from vomiting or diuretics, and hypertension with low K+ suggests aldosterone excess or its mimics. Failure to thrive, polyuria, and vomiting in a child suggest Bartter or Gitelman syndrome, and recent ventilation for COPD suggests an alkalosis left behind after hypercapnia.

Diagnosis

On an ABG or VBG, metabolic alkalosis is a pH above 7.45 with a high HCO3−, which moves the same way as the pH, and the compensation is checked as set out in the review of ; the urine Cl− then sorts the causes.

In metabolic alkalosis, urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive alkalosis, in which the kidney holds on to Cl− because volume and Cl− are low: vomiting and NG tube suction, diuretics that have worn off, chloride-losing diarrhea, cystic fibrosis, and the period after hypercapnia. Urine Cl− above 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-resistant alkalosis: diuretics still acting, aldosterone excess and its mimics, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, and severe hypokalemia. Urine Cl− is high while a diuretic acts and low once it wears off, so a changing result suggests hidden diuretic use. Vomiting and diuretics give low or normal BP with high renin and aldosterone and K+ lost mostly in urine. Primary hyperaldosteronism gives high BP, low renin, high aldosterone, and a high aldosterone-to-renin ratio; secondary hyperaldosteronism gives high BP with both high; and apparent mineralocorticoid excess gives high BP with both low. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes give low or normal BP with high renin and aldosterone, with high urine Ca2+ in Bartter syndrome and low urine Ca2+ with low Mg2+ in Gitelman syndrome. An alkali load gives variable values, with high Ca2+ in milk-alkali syndrome.

Management

Chloride-responsive alkalosis, from vomiting, NG tube suction, or after hypercapnia, is treated with 0.9% saline with KCl, which restores volume and Cl− so the kidney can excrete HCO3−, and the cause of vomiting is treated. Diuretic-induced alkalosis is managed by stopping or reducing the diuretic, replacing KCl, or adding a potassium-sparing diuretic, with acetazolamide if the patient is fluid overloaded. Primary hyperaldosteronism is treated with spironolactone or eplerenone, or adrenalectomy for a unilateral adenoma, and secondary hyperaldosteronism by treating its cause, such as renal artery stenosis or a renin-secreting tumor. In apparent mineralocorticoid excess, Cushing syndrome is treated, licorice is stopped, and Liddle syndrome is treated with amiloride, since its ENaC does not need aldosterone. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes need KCl and Mg2+, with potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, ACEi, or ARBs if K+ stays low. An alkali load is treated by stopping the source, with saline for milk-alkali syndrome. When saline cannot be given, as in heart failure, acetazolamide is used, and severe or refractory alkalosis is treated with dialysis against a low-bicarbonate bath or dilute HCl.

Metabolic acid-base disorders: a shared approach to the blood gas, compensation, and the sorting tests

Metabolic acidosis and metabolic alkalosis are mirror images in which a primary fall or rise in plasma HCO3− moves the pH in the same direction and the lungs compensate by changing pCO2. A single approach serves both: the pH and HCO3− identify a metabolic disorder, an expected pCO2 tests whether a respiratory disorder coexists, and one further test sorts the causes, the anion gap in acidosis and the urine Cl− in alkalosis. Here, we compare the two disorders, describe the stepwise workup and its calculations, and set out the principles of correction, including when HCO3− or acid should be given.

Key points

  • A metabolic disorder is present when HCO3− moves in the same direction as the pH.
  • Expected pCO2 is 1.5 × HCO3− + 8 (± 2) in acidosis (Winter formula) and 0.7 × HCO3− + 20 (± 5) in alkalosis; a pCO2 outside this range means a respiratory disorder as well.
  • The anion gap, (Na+ + K+) − (Cl− + HCO3−), is normally 12–16 mmol/L and falls by about 2.5 for each 10 g/L fall in albumin.
  • A delta-delta ratio below 1 adds a NAGMA, and above 2 a metabolic alkalosis; a high osmolal gap with an AGMA suggests a toxic alcohol.
  • Urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive alkalosis, corrected by saline with KCl.
  • HCO3− is given when it has truly been lost, as in RTA and CKD, but not routinely in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis.

Comparison

A metabolic acidosis follows a gain of acid or a loss of HCO3−, and the lungs hyperventilate to lower pCO2; a metabolic alkalosis follows a loss of H+ or a gain of HCO3−, and the lungs hypoventilate to raise pCO2, rarely above 55 mmHg. The disorders have opposite effects beyond the pH: acidosis moves K+ out of cells, raises ionized Ca2+, and shifts the O2 dissociation curve to the right, whereas alkalosis moves K+ into cells, lowers ionized Ca2+ as albumin binds more of it, and shifts the curve to the left. Acidosis causes Kussmaul breathing, and alkalosis shallow, slow breathing. Plasma K+ in acidosis is often high, but less so in organic acidoses, and low in RTA types 1 and 2 and diarrhea.

Diagnosis

An ABG or VBG first shows whether the pH is low or high, and a HCO3− that has moved the same way identifies a metabolic disorder. The expected pCO2 is then calculated, by the Winter formula, 1.5 × HCO3− + 8 (± 2), in acidosis, or 0.7 × HCO3− + 20 (± 5) in alkalosis; a higher pCO2 than expected means a respiratory acidosis as well, and a lower one a respiratory alkalosis, as in aspirin poisoning.

In acidosis, the anion gap, (Na+ + K+) − (Cl− + HCO3−), normally 12–16 mmol/L or 8–12 mmol/L without K+, separates NAGMA from AGMA. The gap is mostly albumin, so each 10 g/L fall in albumin lowers it by about 2.5 and can hide an AGMA. In an AGMA, the delta-delta ratio, (AG − 16) ÷ (24 − HCO3−), is 1–2 when the acidosis is pure, below 1 when a NAGMA coexists, and above 2 when a metabolic alkalosis coexists, as in DKA with vomiting. An osmolal gap, measured osmolality − (2 × Na+ + glucose + urea), above 15 with an AGMA suggests methanol or ethylene glycol; it can be high without acidosis early after ingestion or with isopropyl alcohol, and it closes as the alcohol is metabolized, so a normal value late does not exclude poisoning. In a NAGMA, a negative urine AG shows a normal renal response to HCO3− lost from the gut or to saline, and a positive one points to RTA or CKD. In alkalosis, urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive disorder and above 20 mmol/L a chloride-resistant one, which BP, renin, and aldosterone then separate.

Management

Both disorders are corrected by treating the cause and replacing volume and K+. HCO3− is given when it has truly been lost, as oral alkali in RTA and CKD, but not routinely in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis, whose anions are metabolized back into HCO3− once the cause is treated, so that added HCO3− can overshoot into alkalosis; its risks are hypernatremia, fluid overload, low ionized Ca2+, and hypokalemia. A chloride-responsive alkalosis is corrected by 0.9% saline with KCl, which lets the kidney excrete the extra HCO3−; acetazolamide is used when saline cannot be given, and dialysis or dilute HCl for severe or refractory alkalosis.

Hypovolemia

Pathophysiology

Losing salt and water from the ECF faster than they are replaced shrinks the circulating volume and lowers tissue perfusion, until the sympathetic system, RAAS, and ADH make the kidney retain Na+ and water to restore it.

How the loss changes the compartments

Where the fluid comes from depends on what is lost; the compartments themselves are described on Fluids.

  • Isotonic lossBleeding, vomiting, and diarrhea remove fluid with about as much Na+ as plasma, so only the ECF shrinks and plasma Na+ is unchanged
  • More water than Na+Sweat and osmotic diuresis concentrate the ECF, which draws water out of cells, so both compartments shrink and plasma Na+ rises
  • Third spacingFluid leaves plasma for the interstitium or a body cavity; total body water is unchanged, but the circulating volume falls

Note: Hypovolemia is a loss of salt and water, whereas dehydration is a loss of water alone, which raises plasma Na+ (see Hypernatremia).

How the body responds

  1. Less stretchBaroreceptors, the atria, and the afferent arteriole sense a lower volume
  2. Sympathetic system, RAAS, ADHFaster heart rate and vasoconstriction; angiotensin II and aldosterone retain Na+; ADH retains water
  3. RetentionConcentrated urine with Na+ below 20 mmol/L, until volume is restored

Each step is described on Volume and Osmolality Regulation.

Hypovolemia: where the fluid goes, how the body compensates, and how to replace it

Hypovolemia is a loss of salt and water from the ECF that lowers the circulating volume and tissue perfusion. The sympathetic system, RAAS, and ADH respond by raising heart rate, constricting vessels, and making the kidney retain Na+ and water, which explains its tachycardia, poor skin perfusion, and concentrated, Na+-poor urine. It arises from renal loss, extra-renal loss, and third spacing, and urine Na+ separates the kidney from the other sources. Here, we review the mechanisms, causes, presentation, assessment, and treatment of hypovolemia, including the choice of IV fluid, and consider its relationship to kidney injury and to disorders of sodium, potassium, acid-base balance, and aldosterone.

Key points

  • Isotonic losses shrink only the ECF; losses with more water than Na+ also draw water from cells and raise Na+; third spacing moves fluid out of plasma without changing total body water.
  • Hypovolemia comes from renal loss (diuretics, osmotic diuresis, aldosterone deficiency, cerebral salt wasting, and salt-wasting nephropathy), the big 5 extra-renal losses (vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, burns, and bleeding), and third spacing through cytokine-driven capillary leak.
  • Tachycardia from the baroreceptor reflex, poor skin perfusion, and a rising lactate from anaerobic glycolysis mark hypovolemia, and prerenal AKI gives a BUN:creatinine ratio above 20, urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L, and FENa below 1%.
  • Urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L points to extra-renal loss or third spacing, and above 20 mmol/L to renal loss.
  • Isotonic crystalloid restores volume, leaving about 250 mL of each liter in plasma; bleeding needs blood products, and half-normal saline and D5W are not used for resuscitation.

Pathophysiology

Hypovolemia is a loss of salt and water from the ECF faster than it is replaced, so total body Na+ is low. Because Na+ content sets the ECF volume and water balance sets the Na+ concentration, hypovolemia differs from dehydration, a loss of water alone that raises plasma Na+. Isotonic losses such as bleeding, vomiting, and diarrhea shrink only the ECF and leave plasma Na+ unchanged. Losses with more water than Na+, such as sweat and osmotic diuresis, concentrate the ECF, which draws water out of cells, so both compartments shrink and Na+ rises. In third spacing, fluid moves from plasma into the interstitium or a body cavity, leaving total body water unchanged but the circulating volume reduced.

A fall in volume reduces stretch on baroreceptors, the atria, and the afferent arteriole. The sympathetic system raises heart rate and constricts vessels, angiotensin II and aldosterone make the kidney retain Na+, and ADH retains water, so the urine becomes concentrated with Na+ below 20 mmol/L until volume is restored.

Etiology and mechanisms

Renal loss. Loop diuretics block NKCC2 and thiazides block NCC, so Na+ and water are lost in urine, particularly with excessive doses or continued use during poor intake. In osmotic diuresis, glucose in DKA and HHS, mannitol, and urea hold water in the tubule, and Na+ is lost with it, though more water than Na+ is lost. In adrenal insufficiency and hypoaldosteronism, the collecting duct cannot reclaim Na+, so Na+ is lost while K+ is retained, with salt craving, and skin pigmentation in primary adrenal insufficiency. In cerebral salt wasting, after SAH or other brain injury, the kidney wastes Na+, possibly through BNP or reduced sympathetic drive to the kidney rather than through low aldosterone; unlike SIADH, the patient is hypovolemic. Salt-wasting nephropathy includes kidney disease that cannot reclaim Na+ and Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, which behave like lifelong loop and thiazide diuretics.

Extra-renal loss. The big 5 are vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, burns, and bleeding. Vomiting and NG tube suction remove gastric fluid, causing metabolic alkalosis and hypokalemia. The gut secretes 3–6 L a day and normally reabsorbs it, so diarrhea, high-output stomas, and fistulas lose large volumes, causing a NAGMA and hypokalemia. Exercise in heat can lose 1–2 L of sweat an hour, with less Na+ than plasma. Burns lose fluid from the injured surface, and histamine and free radicals make capillaries leak, so burns also cause third spacing. Bleeding, most often from trauma and then from GI bleeding, surgery, ruptured aneurysm, and postpartum hemorrhage, loses whole blood, red cells with the plasma.

Third spacing. In pancreatitis, IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α make capillaries leak, and fluid moves into the interstitium and retroperitoneum. In peritonitis, the inflamed peritoneum leaks fluid into the peritoneal cavity and bowel wall. In sepsis, cytokines released throughout the body make capillaries leak everywhere, while vasodilation adds a distributive shock. Fluid also pools in obstructed bowel and in inflamed tissue after surgery.

Clinical presentation and complications

Early hypovolemia causes thirst, fatigue, muscle cramps, postural dizziness, and reduced urine output while BP is maintained. Tachycardia, often the first sign, reflects the baroreceptor reflex. Hypotension appears first on standing and then lying down, once about 20–25% of the effective arterial volume is lost, and it is a late sign in children and young adults. Sympathetic vasoconstriction produces cool, pale, clammy, or mottled skin with capillary refill above 2 seconds, alongside dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, and a low JVP. When too little oxygen reaches tissues, anaerobic glycolysis converts pyruvate to lactate, producing a type A lactic acidosis with fast breathing; lactate is used to judge severity and response. Hypoperfusion of the brain, gut, heart, and kidneys causes confusion, abdominal pain, chest pain, and oliguria. Low renal perfusion lowers GFR, causing prerenal AKI: BUN and creatinine rise, and because the intact tubules reclaim Na+, water, and with them urea, the BUN:creatinine ratio exceeds 20, urine Na+ is below 20 mmol/L, and FENa is below 1%; prolonged ischemia leads to ATN. Hypovolemic shock progresses from a compensated stage at about 10% loss to shock at 20–25% and then irreversible organ damage.

The accompanying laboratory findings point to the cause. A metabolic alkalosis with low K+ suggests vomiting or diuretics; a NAGMA with low K+, diarrhea, stomas, or fistulas; high K+ and low Na+ with salt craving, aldosterone deficiency; high glucose with polyuria, osmotic diuresis in DKA or HHS; low Na+ with high urine Na+ after SAH, cerebral salt wasting; a falling hemoglobin, bleeding; and edema or ascites with low BP, third spacing or low albumin.

Diagnosis

Volume status is assessed by combining the history, vital signs including orthostatic BP, examination, weight trend, and fluid balance, since no single sign is reliable; the bedside signs and the shared tests, including bloods, FENa, and tests of fluid responsiveness, are set out in the review of .

Bleeding is sought first, with hemoglobin, coagulation, and crossmatch, and its source is found and controlled. Otherwise, urine Na+ separates the causes: below 20 mmol/L, the kidney is retaining Na+ appropriately, pointing to extra-renal loss or third spacing, whereas above 20 mmol/L, the kidney is the source, pointing to diuretics, kidney disease, aldosterone deficiency, or cerebral salt wasting; urine Na+ is also high in metabolic alkalosis, where Na+ leaves with HCO3−.

Management

Treatment restores volume while addressing the cause and watching for fluid overload. Bleeding is treated with source control and blood products, plasma, platelets, and red cells in a 1:1:1 ratio, keeping crystalloid to a minimum. Other causes are treated with boluses of isotonic crystalloid, reassessing after each, with care in HF, CKD, and cirrhosis. The cause is treated by stopping diuretics, giving antiemetics, insulin for DKA, and ABX and source control for sepsis. Vasopressors are used only when hypotension persists despite adequate fluid, and once the patient is stable, maintenance fluid covers ongoing losses, or fluid is stopped.

Isotonic crystalloid is used for resuscitation and hypotonic fluids are not; the composition, distribution, uses, and risks of NS, LR, albumin, ½NS, and D5W are compared in the review of . KCl is added when K+ is low or about to fall, as in vomiting, diarrhea, diuretic use, and DKA once insulin starts; HCO3− replaces losses in diarrhea and fistulas with a NAGMA; and dextrose is added to NS or ½NS when glucose falls in DKA so insulin can continue until the ketoacidosis clears. Monitoring covers heart rate, BP, urine output, mental state, and lactate; crackles, edema, and JVP for overload; daily weight and fluid balance; and Na+, K+, Cl−, glucose, and creatinine, raising a low Na+ by no more than 8 mmol/L in 24 hours.

Hypervolemia

Pathophysiology

Salt and water retained or given faster than they are excreted expand the ECF, and because most of the extra fluid crosses into the interstitium, it appears as edema; in HF and cirrhosis, the kidney keeps retaining because the arteries seem underfilled.

Why the kidney keeps retaining

  • Effective arterial blood volumeThe volume the arterial sensors detect, which is not the same as total volume: in HF (low output) and cirrhosis (splanchnic vasodilation) the arteries are underfilled despite extra fluid, so the same steps drive retention and the edema keeps growing
  • Natriuretic peptidesStretched atria and ventricles release ANP and BNP, which inhibit Na+ reabsorption and renin release; BNP is high in HF, but the peptides cannot overcome RAAS there
  • Low GFRIn CKD and AKI, too little Na+ is filtered to excrete the daily intake, whatever the arteries sense

Why the extra fluid becomes edema

Retained or infused isotonic fluid spreads through the ECF, about three parts to the interstitium for each part kept in plasma; the Starling forces behind this are described on Fluids.

  • High capillary pressureThe expanded ECF and raised venous pressure push fluid out: HF, CKD, AKI, and portal hypertension
  • Low albuminLess oncotic pressure holds fluid in: nephrotic syndrome and cirrhosis
  • Lymph overflowFiltration outruns lymphatic return, as in the splanchnic bed in cirrhosis

Hypervolemia: underfilled arteries, a failing filter, and the stepwise removal of fluid

Hypervolemia is a gain of salt and water that expands the ECF, most of which crosses into the interstitium as edema. The kidney retains salt and water either because the arteries seem underfilled, as in HF and cirrhosis, where RAAS, the sympathetic system, and ADH drive retention despite excess total fluid, or because a low GFR in CKD and AKI leaves too little Na+ filtered to excrete intake; fluid given to patients who cannot excrete it adds to both. Here, we review the mechanisms, causes, presentation, assessment, and treatment of hypervolemia, including the stepwise use of diuretics and dialysis, and consider its relationship to kidney injury and to disorders of sodium, acid-base balance, and aldosterone.

Key points

  • In HF and cirrhosis, the arteries are underfilled despite excess total fluid, so RAAS, the sympathetic system, and ADH keep the kidney retaining Na+ and water; natriuretic peptides cannot overcome them.
  • In CKD and AKI, a low GFR leaves too little Na+ filtered to excrete intake.
  • Of each liter of isotonic fluid retained or infused, about 250 mL stays in plasma and the rest enters the interstitium, so most of it becomes edema.
  • Pulmonary edema, effusions, JVD, ascites, pitting edema, dilutional hyponatremia, and cardiorenal or hepatorenal syndrome follow, depending on the cause.
  • Treatment is Na+ restriction and a loop diuretic, adding a thiazide, potassium-sparing diuretic, or acetazolamide, except in cirrhosis, where spironolactone comes first, and dialysis by the AEIOU indications when overload is refractory.

Pathophysiology

Hypervolemia is a retention or administration of salt and water faster than it is excreted, so total body Na+ is high. Retained or infused isotonic fluid spreads through the ECF in a ratio of about three parts interstitium to one part plasma, so most of it becomes edema. High capillary pressure from the expanded ECF and raised venous pressure, low albumin, and lymph production exceeding reabsorption all favor its accumulation in the tissues.

The kidney retains salt and water for one of two reasons. The arterial sensors respond to the effective arterial blood volume rather than total volume: in HF, from low output, and in cirrhosis, from splanchnic vasodilation, the arteries are underfilled despite excess fluid, so RAAS, the sympathetic system, and ADH drive continued retention. Stretched atria and ventricles release ANP and BNP, which inhibit Na+ reabsorption and renin release, but in HF they cannot overcome RAAS. In CKD and AKI, by contrast, too little Na+ is filtered to excrete the daily intake, whatever the arteries sense.

Etiology and mechanisms

In HF, low cardiac output underfills the arteries; baroreceptors and the afferent arteriole sense less stretch, RAAS, the sympathetic system, and ADH make the kidney retain Na+ and water, and the raised venous pressure produces pulmonary and peripheral edema while output stays low, so the cycle continues. In cirrhosis, scarring raises resistance to portal flow, nitric oxide dilates the splanchnic arteries, and the underfilled systemic arteries trigger the same retention, with ADH also lowering Na+; high splanchnic capillary pressure, low albumin, and lymph production exceeding reabsorption send the fluid into the peritoneum as ascites. In CKD of G3 or worse, too few nephrons filter too little Na+ to excrete the daily intake, RAAS activity adds to the retention, and the expanded ECF causes hypertension and edema, most evident in G4 and G5. In AKI, whether prerenal, intrinsic, or postrenal, a sudden fall in GFR, especially in the oliguric phase of ATN, leaves intake and infused fluid in the body. Among primary causes of renal Na+ retention, nephrotic syndrome lowers albumin and may make the tubule retain Na+ directly, nephritic syndrome causes edema, hypertension, and low urine output, and hyperaldosteronism retains Na+ independently of renin and presents mainly as resistant hypertension with hypokalemia. Finally, IV fluid given faster than it can be excreted causes overload, especially in HF, CKD, cirrhosis, low albumin, and oliguric AKI.

Clinical presentation and complications

Hypervolemia causes pulmonary edema, with dyspnea, orthopnea, crackles, hypoxia, and pink frothy sputum, from raised pulmonary capillary pressure in left-sided HF, AKI, CKD, or after IV fluid, with an S3 in HF. HF also causes pleural effusions. JVD, with a JVP above 3 cm over the sternal angle and hepatojugular reflux, reflects right-sided or biventricular HF or overload from CKD or AKI. Ascites, with distension, shifting dullness, and a fluid thrill, is mostly a complication of cirrhosis but also occurs in HF and nephrotic syndrome. Pitting edema collects in the ankles and shins, or the sacrum in bedbound patients, and in the face and eyelids in nephrotic syndrome. Weight gain and hypertension accompany CKD, AKI, glomerular disease, and hyperaldosteronism, and dilutional hyponatremia occurs in HF and cirrhosis, where ADH retains water. Prerenal AKI that fluid does not correct takes two forms: cardiorenal syndrome in HF, where low output and high venous pressure lower GFR, and hepatorenal syndrome in cirrhosis, where the afferent arterioles constrict severely, a process sepsis and NSAIDs can trigger.

Diagnosis

Volume status is assessed by combining the history, vital signs, examination, weight trend, and fluid balance, as set out in the review of , which also explains why urine Na+ is low in HF, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome despite overload.

Examination, oxygen saturation, CXR, and lung ultrasound show whether fluid is in the lungs, and the next step is to find the retaining organ. BNP, released by stretched ventricles, makes HF unlikely below 100 pg/mL and likely above 500 pg/mL, and echo shows heart function; LFTs, albumin, ultrasound, and an ascitic tap assess cirrhosis; and creatinine with its trend, urinalysis, and urine protein assess CKD, AKI, and glomerular disease. Cardiogenic edema on CXR shows an enlarged heart, central edema, Kerley B lines, peribronchial cuffing, and effusions, and B-lines on lung ultrasound show interstitial fluid.

Management

Hypervolemia is treated with Na+ restriction to about 2 g a day, treatment of the cause, and diuretics: a loop diuretic first, which blocks NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb, then a thiazide such as metolazone for sequential nephron blockade, a potassium-sparing diuretic to add natriuresis and offset K+ loss, and acetazolamide to add diuresis and correct a diuretic alkalosis. The site, use, and side effects of each are compared in the review of .

Cirrhosis is the exception to starting with a loop diuretic: spironolactone comes first, because aldosterone drives the retention, with furosemide added in a 100:40 mg ratio, alongside Na+ restriction and abstinence from alcohol; tense ascites is drained by large-volume paracentesis with albumin, and fluid is restricted only when Na+ is low. CKD and AKI need higher doses of loop diuretics and Na+ restriction, glomerular disease and hyperaldosteronism are treated at their source, with spironolactone or surgery for hyperaldosteronism, and overload from IV fluid is managed by stopping or slowing the fluid and giving a loop diuretic. Monitoring covers daily weight, urine output, BP, K+, Mg2+, Na+, and creatinine, since too much diuresis causes hypovolemia and prerenal AKI. When overload does not respond in severe CKD or AKI, dialysis removes fluid by ultrafiltration, following the AEIOU indications.

Disorders of extracellular volume: bedside assessment, tests, and the tools of correction

Hypovolemia and hypervolemia are a loss and a gain of salt and water in the ECF, and plasma Na+ can remain normal in both because Na+ moves with water. Neither has a single reliable sign, so volume status is judged by combining the history, vital signs, examination, weight, and fluid balance, and tests then find the cause: bleeding and the urine Na+ in hypovolemia, and the lungs and the retaining organ in hypervolemia. Here, we compare the two states, describe a shared workup, and review the fluids, diuretics, and indications for dialysis used to correct them.

Key points

  • No single sign is reliable; in older adults sudden weight loss is the most specific sign of hypovolemia, and in children hypotension appears late.
  • Urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L means the kidney is retaining Na+: after extra-renal loss in hypovolemia, and in HF, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome despite overload.
  • FENa below 1% suggests prerenal AKI, but diuretics raise it, so FEUrea below 35% is used instead.
  • A passive leg raise with echo is the most accurate test of fluid responsiveness.
  • About 250 mL of each litre of NS or LR stays in plasma, and only about 1/12 of D5W, so hypotonic fluids are not used for resuscitation.
  • Loop diuretics are first line for overload in HF, CKD, and AKI, and potassium-sparing diuretics in cirrhosis; dialysis follows the AEIOU indications.

Comparison

Hypovolemia presents with thirst, postural dizziness, tachycardia, orthostatic and then supine hypotension, cool skin with slow capillary refill and dry mucous membranes, a low JVP, falling weight, and little concentrated urine. Hypervolemia presents with dyspnea, orthopnea, pitting edema, crackles, effusions, ascites, a raised JVP in HF and kidney overload, and rising weight; BP is often normal, raised in kidney disease, and low in advanced HF or cirrhosis. Because no single sign is reliable, findings are combined.

Diagnosis

In hypovolemia, bleeding is sought first with hemoglobin, coagulation, and crossmatch. Otherwise, a urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L shows extra-renal loss or third spacing, and above 20 mmol/L a renal loss. Bloods include electrolytes, glucose, BUN and creatinine, lactate, FBC, and albumin; hematocrit and albumin rise as plasma is concentrated. FENa below 1% suggests prerenal AKI and above 1% ATN in oliguric AKI, with FEUrea below 35% used after diuretics. A passive leg raise with echo is the most accurate test of fluid responsiveness; IVC and pulse pressure variation hold only in ventilated patients without arrhythmias, and central venous pressure is unreliable. In hypervolemia, the lungs are examined and imaged, and the retaining organ is sought; urine Na+ is often below 20 mmol/L despite overload because the kidney senses underfilled arteries.

Management

NS and LR distribute through the ECF, leaving about 250 mL of each litre in plasma; NS in large volumes causes a hyperchloremic NAGMA, while LR, preferred in sepsis and large volumes, can worsen alkalosis and is slightly hypotonic, so it is avoided in brain injury. Albumin is second line, notably in cirrhosis after large-volume paracentesis. ½NS and D5W act partly or wholly as free water and are not used for resuscitation. Loop diuretics block NKCC2 and are first line for overload in HF, CKD, and AKI; thiazides are added for a poor response, potassium-sparing diuretics are first line in cirrhosis and offset K+ loss, and acetazolamide helps in HF with a metabolic alkalosis. Dialysis is indicated for acidosis, electrolytes, intoxication, overload, and uremia (AEIOU).

Hyponatremia

Pathophysiology

Na+ cannot move without water, but water can move without Na+, so a low plasma Na+ almost always means too much water for the Na+ in the body: water held by ADH, taken in faster than the kidney can excrete it, or left behind when the kidney cannot dilute urine.

  • Na+ moves with waterVolume changes but Na+ concentration does not: hypovolemia and hypervolemia
  • Water moves aloneNa+ concentration changes: hyponatremia when water is gained, hypernatremia when it is lost

How the kidney excretes water

  • AWater follows Na+The proximal tubule and descending limb reabsorb about 70% and 20% of filtered water along with or after solute, so they cannot make free water
  • BThe diluting segmentsThe thick ascending limb (NKCC2) and distal tubule (NCC) remove Na+ without water, making dilute fluid; NKCC2 also builds the medullary gradient that later concentrates urine
  • CADHReleased when plasma is concentrated, or when effective volume falls even though plasma is dilute; it inserts aquaporin-2 so the collecting duct reabsorbs water down the medullary gradient
  • DSolute carries water outUrine can be diluted only to about 50 mOsm/kg, so the water excreted is limited by the solute excreted, mostly urea and salt; a normal diet gives 600–900 mOsm a day

Hyponatremia: water, ADH, and the pace of correction

Hyponatremia is an excess of water relative to Na+. Because Na+ cannot move without water but water can move without Na+, a low plasma Na+ almost always reflects water retained by ADH, taken in faster than the kidney can excrete it, or left behind when the kidney cannot dilute urine, while total body Na+ may be low, normal, or high. Serum osmolality first separates true, hypotonic hyponatremia from hypertonic hyponatremia and pseudohyponatremia; urine osmolality then shows whether ADH is acting, and volume status with urine Na+ locates the cause. Here, we review the renal handling of water, the causes of hypovolemic, euvolemic, and hypervolemic hyponatremia, its effects on the brain, its diagnosis, and its treatment, in which the speed of correction matters as much as its direction, and consider its relationship to disorders of volume, ADH, aldosterone, and the thyroid and adrenal glands.

Key points

  • Water is retained when ADH is released for low effective volume, when it is released inappropriately, when intake exceeds what the kidney can excrete, or when the kidney cannot dilute or excrete urine.
  • Serum osmolality comes first: high in hyperglycemia or after mannitol, normal in pseudohyponatremia, and low in true hyponatremia.
  • Urine osmolality below 100 mOsm/kg means ADH is suppressed, as in primary polydipsia and low solute intake; above 100, urine Na+ below 30 mmol/L means the kidney senses low effective volume, and above 30 points to diuretics, mineralocorticoid deficiency, cerebral salt wasting, SIADH, hypothyroidism, secondary adrenal insufficiency, or kidney failure.
  • SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion that requires hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency to be ruled out.
  • Acute hyponatremia causes cerebral edema and herniation; chronic hyponatremia causes few symptoms because the brain adapts, which is why correcting it faster than 6–8 mmol/L in 24 hours risks osmotic demyelination.
  • Severe symptoms are treated with boluses of 3% saline to raise Na+ by 4–6 mmol/L; otherwise, NS treats hypovolemic forms, fluid restriction, urea, salt tablets with a loop diuretic, or vaptans treat SIADH, and water and salt restriction with loop diuretics treat hypervolemic forms.

Pathophysiology

When Na+ moves with water, the volume of the ECF changes but its Na+ concentration does not, as in hypovolemia and hypervolemia; when water moves alone, the concentration changes, falling when water is gained. Hyponatremia is therefore an excess of water relative to Na+, and total body Na+ can be low, normal, or high, giving its hypovolemic, euvolemic, and hypervolemic forms.

The proximal tubule and descending limb reabsorb about 70% and 20% of filtered water along with or after solute, so they cannot make free water. The thick ascending limb, through NKCC2, and the distal tubule, through NCC, remove Na+ without water and make dilute fluid, and NKCC2 also builds the medullary gradient that later concentrates urine. ADH, released when plasma is concentrated or when effective volume falls even though plasma is dilute, inserts aquaporin-2 so the collecting duct reabsorbs water down that gradient. Because urine can be diluted only to about 50 mOsm/kg, the water excreted is limited by the solute excreted, mostly urea and salt, of which a normal diet provides 600–900 mOsm a day.

Water is retained in four ways. ADH released appropriately for low effective volume, with urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg, causes hyponatremia in hypovolemia and in HF and cirrhosis, where RAAS retains salt and water together and ADH adds the extra water. ADH released without a volume or osmotic trigger, also with urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg, causes SIADH, and hypothyroidism and cortisol deficiency behave the same way. When intake exceeds excretion, ADH is suppressed and urine osmolality is below 100 mOsm/kg, as in low solute intake and primary polydipsia. Finally, the kidney cannot dilute urine when thiazides act at the diluting segment, or excrete water in advanced CKD and AKI.

Etiology and mechanisms

Hypovolemic hyponatremia. Loop diuretics lose Na+ and water, and the volume loss releases ADH, but they cause hyponatremia less often than thiazides, because blocking NKCC2 also weakens the medullary gradient that ADH needs to concentrate urine. Thiazides block NCC in the distal tubule, a diluting segment, so urine cannot be made dilute while the intact gradient lets ADH keep concentrating it; hyponatremia usually appears within the first 2–3 weeks of treatment. In primary adrenal insufficiency, loss of aldosterone and cortisol causes Na+ wasting with hyperkalemia, salt craving, and skin pigmentation; hypoaldosteronism acts similarly, and ACEi and ARBs are an uncommon cause of low Na+. In cerebral salt wasting, after SAH or other brain injury, the kidney wastes Na+, possibly through BNP or reduced sympathetic drive, leaving the patient hypovolemic, unlike SIADH. Extra-renal causes are vomiting, in which nausea itself also releases ADH, diarrhea, sweating with salt replaced by water alone, burns, bleeding, and third spacing in pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, and low albumin.

Euvolemic hyponatremia. In SIADH, ADH is released or acts without a volume or osmotic trigger, from CNS disorders, lung disease, small cell lung cancer through ectopic ADH, drugs such as SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide, and MDMA, pain, nausea, and surgery. Hypothyroidism raises ADH through low output, usually only with marked thyroid failure. Cortisol normally inhibits ADH release, so in secondary adrenal insufficiency, cortisol deficiency releases ADH, while aldosterone is preserved by RAAS, so there is neither Na+ wasting nor hyperkalemia. Both must be excluded before SIADH is diagnosed. Tea and toast diets and beer potomania provide too little protein and salt, so at 50 mOsm/kg the kidney can excrete only a few liters of water a day, and beer's carbohydrate further limits protein breakdown and urea. Primary polydipsia exceeds what normal kidneys can excrete, about 18 L a day, and is most often psychogenic, particularly in schizophrenia; exercise-associated hyponatremia combines water intake with ADH released during exercise.

Hypervolemic hyponatremia. In HF, low output, and in cirrhosis, splanchnic vasodilation, underfill the arteries, so RAAS retains Na+ and water and ADH, released for low effective volume, retains extra water. In advanced CKD and AKI, too few working nephrons filter too little water to excrete intake, whatever ADH does, and nephrotic syndrome can also cause hypervolemic hyponatremia.

Clinical presentation and complications

Symptoms depend on how fast Na+ fell more than on how low it is. When plasma becomes dilute, water moves into brain cells, and an acute fall, within 48 hours, causes cerebral edema with headache, nausea, confusion, and seizures; raised ICP can lead to uncal herniation, coma, and death. Within about 48 hours, brain cells shed osmolytes and lose the extra water, so chronic hyponatremia causes few symptoms, but these adapted cells then lose water quickly if Na+ is corrected too fast. Mild hyponatremia, 130–135 mmol/L, causes fatigue, weakness, poor attention and memory, gait problems, falls, fractures, and osteoporosis; moderate hyponatremia, 125–129 mmol/L, adds drowsiness, cramps, nausea, and vomiting; and severe hyponatremia, below 125 mmol/L, causes confusion, somnolence, seizures, decreased consciousness, and cardiorespiratory collapse.

Osmotic demyelination syndrome follows overly rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia and damages pontine white matter, often in two phases: the encephalopathy improves as Na+ rises, then 3–5 days later dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriparesis, or locked-in syndrome appear, usually irreversibly. The risk is highest with chronic hyponatremia, Na+ below 120 mmol/L, alcohol use, malnutrition, hypokalemia, and liver disease. Untreated severe hyponatremia can also cause rhabdomyolysis.

Diagnosis

The workup, set out in the review of , first confirms with serum osmolality that the hyponatremia is hypotonic, excluding hypertonic hyponatremia and pseudohyponatremia, then reads the urine osmolality, volume status, and urine Na+.

Urine osmolality below 100 mOsm/kg points to primary polydipsia and low solute intake. With a higher urine osmolality, urine Na+ below 30 mmol/L points to extra-renal loss and third spacing, HF, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome, and above 30 mmol/L to diuretics, which make urine Na+ unreliable while they act, mineralocorticoid deficiency, with high K+, low glucose, and pigmentation in primary adrenal insufficiency, cerebral salt wasting after brain injury, SIADH, hypothyroidism, with high TSH and low thyroxine, secondary adrenal insufficiency, with low cortisol and ACTH but normal K+, and kidney failure. SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion, made only after hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, diuretics, and heart, liver, and kidney disease are excluded. Further tests include TSH and cortisol, with ACTH if secondary adrenal insufficiency is suspected; glucose, lipids, protein, and protein electrophoresis; creatinine, LFTs, and albumin; and CXR or CT of the chest and CT of the head to find the cause of SIADH.

Management

Treatment depends on symptoms, how fast Na+ fell, and volume status, and in chronic hyponatremia Na+ is raised by no more than 6–8 mmol/L in 24 hours. Severe symptoms, such as seizures, obtundation, or delirium, are treated with boluses of 3% saline to raise Na+ by 4–6 mmol/L, enough to relieve cerebral edema whatever the starting value, after which correction slows to stay within the limit, with frequent Na+ checks; D5W or desmopressin brings Na+ back down if it overshoots.

Hypovolemic hyponatremia is treated with NS, which restores volume and removes the stimulus for ADH, along with treatment of the cause; once volume is restored, ADH switches off and a water diuresis can raise Na+ too fast. Diuretics are stopped; primary adrenal insufficiency is treated with NS, hydrocortisone, and fludrocortisone; and cerebral salt wasting with NS, adding 3% saline if Na+ is very low. SIADH is treated by addressing its cause and restricting fluid, then with oral urea, which adds solute that carries water out, salt tablets with a loop diuretic, or a vaptan; NS can worsen SIADH, because the kidney excretes the salt in concentrated urine and keeps the water. Hypothyroidism is treated with levothyroxine and secondary adrenal insufficiency with hydrocortisone. In low solute intake, solute and food are restored gradually, because the risk of overcorrection is very high once new solute lets the kidney excrete the extra water, and primary polydipsia is treated by restricting water, after which Na+ can rise quickly. Hypervolemic hyponatremia is treated with water and salt restriction, a loop diuretic, which weakens the medullary gradient so that more free water is lost, and treatment of the cause, with dialysis in advanced kidney failure.

The mechanisms, uses, and risks of each treatment are compared in the review of ; vaptans are not used in cirrhosis, and tolvaptan is hepatotoxic.

Hypernatremia: a water deficit that thirst normally prevents

Hypernatremia is a deficit of water relative to Na+, from water lost faster than it is replaced, ADH missing or ignored, or Na+ gained. Because a rise in osmolality drives powerful thirst, it develops mainly in people who cannot sense thirst or reach water: infants, older adults, and those with neurologic impairment or in hospital. Urine osmolality separates diabetes insipidus from renal and extra-renal water loss and Na+ gain, and treatment restores volume, replaces the water deficit slowly enough to avoid cerebral edema, and addresses the cause. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes, presentation, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider its relationship to diabetes insipidus, hyponatremia, volume disorders, and aldosterone excess.

Key points

  • Thirst, triggered at an osmolality of about 295 mOsm/kg, is the main defense, so hypernatremia almost always needs impaired thirst or no access to water.
  • Hypovolemic hypernatremia follows renal losses (osmotic diuresis, loop diuretics, kidney disease) or extra-renal losses with more water than Na+ (sweating, burns, vomiting, diarrhea); isotonic losses such as bleeding do not raise Na+.
  • Euvolemic hypernatremia follows pure water loss: insensible losses, central or nephrogenic DI, and too little intake; hypervolemic hypernatremia follows Na+ gain, usually from hypertonic saline or NaHCO3.
  • Urine osmolality below 300 mOsm/kg points to DI, 300–600 to osmotic diuresis, loop diuretics, kidney disease, or partial DI, and above 600 to extra-renal loss, low intake, or Na+ gain, which urine Na+ then separates.
  • Chronic hypernatremia is lowered by no more than 10–12 mmol/L in 24 hours, with the water deficit replaced over 48–72 hours, to avoid cerebral edema.
  • Central DI is treated with desmopressin, and nephrogenic DI by removing its cause and with a thiazide and a low-salt, low-protein diet.

Pathophysiology

When plasma Na+ and osmolality rise, hypothalamic osmoreceptors drive thirst, at about 295 mOsm/kg, and the posterior pituitary releases ADH, which inserts aquaporin-2 in the collecting duct so water returns to plasma and urine becomes concentrated. Hypernatremia therefore arises when water is lost faster than it is replaced, through the kidney or through the skin, gut, and lungs; when ADH is missing, in central DI, or ignored, in nephrogenic DI; or when Na+ is gained faster than the kidney excretes it. Because thirst is so powerful, it almost always also needs impaired thirst, infancy, old age, altered mental state, intubation, or no access to water; with intact thirst and access, even DI usually leaves Na+ near normal, at the cost of polyuria and polydipsia. Urine osmolality reflects the mechanism: below 300 mOsm/kg when ADH is missing or ignored, 300–600 with renal water loss, and above 600 with extra-renal loss, low intake, or Na+ gain.

Etiology and mechanisms

Hypovolemic hypernatremia. Water and Na+ are both lost, water more than Na+. Renal causes are osmotic diuresis, in which glucose in DKA and HHS, mannitol, or urea after an obstruction is relieved holds water in the tubule; loop diuretics, which weaken the medullary gradient so urine cannot be concentrated, whereas thiazides instead cause hyponatremia; and intrinsic kidney disease. Extra-renal causes are sweating from exercise, fever, or heat, since sweat has less Na+ than plasma; burns, with large evaporative losses; prolonged vomiting or NG drainage not replaced with water; and diarrhea from gastroenteritis, especially in infants given too little water. Isotonic losses such as bleeding do not raise Na+.

Euvolemic hypernatremia. Pure water loss comes mostly from cells, so the ECF is little changed. Insensible losses rise with fever, fast breathing, and heat. Central DI, too little ADH, is idiopathic in 30–50% and also follows head trauma, pituitary surgery, craniopharyngioma, and infiltrative disease such as sarcoidosis. In nephrogenic DI, the collecting duct ignores ADH, from inherited X-linked forms in children and lithium and hypercalcemia in adults, as well as severe hypokalemia, foscarnet, and demeclocycline. Too little water intake follows impaired thirst from hypothalamic damage, ineffective breastfeeding in infants, and older age, neurologic impairment, altered mental state, intubation, or lack of access to water.

Hypervolemic hypernatremia. Na+ is gained, usually in hospital from 3% saline or IV NaHCO3, and otherwise from improperly mixed infant formula, salt tablets, NaHCO3 ingestion, or seawater. Hyperaldosteronism retains Na+ but usually raises it only to the high-normal range, because thirst and ADH still act.

Clinical presentation and complications

Symptoms come from the brain and are most marked when Na+ rises quickly or exceeds about 160 mmol/L. As plasma becomes concentrated, water leaves brain cells and the brain shrinks, causing irritability and agitation, then lethargy, somnolence, coma, and seizures, with increased tone, brisk reflexes, and myoclonus. Alert patients are intensely thirsty unless thirst is impaired, infants have a high-pitched cry, the skin can feel doughy or velvety, and hypovolemic forms cause orthostatic hypotension and tachycardia, although dehydration is easily underestimated in children because water moves out of cells into the ECF. DI causes polyuria above 3 L a day with polydipsia. The most serious complication is intracranial bleeding, as the shrinking brain tears bridging veins and causes subarachnoid or subdural hemorrhage, and dural sinus thrombosis can occur. Over about 48 hours, brain cells gain solutes and regain water, so lowering Na+ too fast in chronic hypernatremia floods them and causes cerebral edema, seizures, and permanent brain damage. Infants, older adults, and anyone who depends on others for water are most at risk.

Diagnosis

The cause is usually clear from the history and examination, including losses, intake and access to water, drugs such as lithium and diuretics, and Na+ given; urine osmolality and urine Na+ confirm it, and the water deficit guides replacement, as set out in the review of . When DI is suspected, water deprivation followed by desmopressin separates central DI, in which urine osmolality rises, by over 100% when complete, from nephrogenic DI, in which it barely changes, and copeptin, a stable fragment released with ADH, is easier to measure than ADH itself. Glucose, Ca2+, K+, a lithium level, creatinine, urine volume, and renin and aldosterone complete the workup.

Management

Treatment addresses the cause, restores volume first with NS or LR if the patient is shocked, and then replaces free water, by mouth or feeding tube when possible and otherwise as D5W or half-normal saline, the fluids compared in the review of . Chronic hypernatremia is lowered by no more than 10–12 mmol/L in 24 hours, about 0.5 mmol/L an hour, with Na+ checked every 2–4 hours, whereas acute hypernatremia, under 48 hours, can be corrected faster; seizures during correction signal cerebral edema and mean the hypotonic fluid is stopped.

Osmotic diuresis is treated with fluids and insulin in DKA and HHS or by stopping mannitol, loop diuretics are stopped or reduced, and extra-renal losses are treated with isotonic fluid if volume is depleted and then hypotonic fluid. Insensible losses and low intake need free water and access to water. Central DI is treated with desmopressin by mouth or nose, at the risk of water intoxication and hyponatremia. Nephrogenic DI is treated by stopping lithium where possible and correcting Ca2+ and K+, with a thiazide, which causes mild volume loss so that more water is reabsorbed proximally and less reaches the collecting duct, a low-salt, low-protein diet, and NSAIDs. Na+ gain is treated by stopping the source and giving D5W with a loop diuretic to remove the Na+, with dialysis when the kidneys fail or the water needed would overload the patient, and hyperaldosteronism by treating the aldosterone excess.

Disorders of plasma sodium: tonicity, the urine, and the pace of correction

Plasma Na+ reflects water rather than total body Na+: hyponatremia means too much water for the Na+ in the body and hypernatremia too little. Both disorders are approached by asking what the kidney is doing with water, using serum and urine osmolality, volume status, and urine Na+, and both are corrected at a limited rate once the brain has adapted, because rapid correction causes osmotic demyelination or cerebral edema. Here, we compare the two disorders, describe the workup of each, and review the fluids and drugs that raise or lower plasma Na+.

Key points

  • Serum osmolality separates true, hypotonic hyponatremia from hypertonic hyponatremia and pseudohyponatremia.
  • In hyponatremia, urine osmolality below 100 mOsm/kg means ADH is suppressed, as in primary polydipsia and low solute intake.
  • Urine Na+ below 30 mmol/L in hyponatremia means the kidney is holding Na+ because it senses low effective volume.
  • In hypernatremia, urine osmolality below 300 mOsm/kg suggests DI, 300–600 a renal water loss, and above 600 a loss elsewhere or Na+ gained.
  • Water deficit = total body water × (Na+ ÷ 140 − 1), replaced over 48–72 hours with ongoing losses.
  • Chronic hyponatremia is raised by no more than 6–8 mmol/L in 24 hours, and chronic hypernatremia lowered by no more than 10–12 mmol/L.

Comparison

Because Na+ cannot move without water but water can move without Na+, a change in plasma Na+ concentration reflects a change in water. In hyponatremia, plasma becomes dilute, water enters brain cells, and cerebral edema follows; in hypernatremia, water leaves brain cells and the brain shrinks, so bridging veins can tear. Over about 48 hours, brain cells adapt by shedding or gaining solutes, which is why chronic disorders cause fewer symptoms and why correcting them too fast causes osmotic demyelination syndrome after hyponatremia and cerebral edema after hypernatremia.

Diagnosis

In hyponatremia, serum osmolality, 2 × Na+ + glucose + urea, normally 275–295 mOsm/kg, is checked first: it is high when glucose, mannitol, or radiocontrast draw water out of cells, normal in pseudohyponatremia from very high lipids or proteins, and low in true hyponatremia. Urine osmolality below 100 mOsm/kg then shows that ADH is suppressed and intake outruns excretion, while a higher value shows that ADH is acting. Volume status and urine Na+ complete the workup: urine Na+ below 30 mmol/L means the kidney is holding Na+, after extra-renal loss or in HF and cirrhosis, while a higher value points to renal loss, SIADH, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, or kidney failure.

In hypernatremia, the cause is usually clear from the history and volume status. Urine osmolality below 300 mOsm/kg means ADH is missing or ignored, as in DI; 300–600 mOsm/kg means a solute, a loop diuretic, kidney disease, or partial DI; and above 600 mOsm/kg means the kidney is concentrating, so water has been lost elsewhere or not taken in, or Na+ has been gained, which urine Na+ separates. The water deficit, total body water × (Na+ ÷ 140 − 1), guides replacement.

Management

Low Na+ is raised with 3% saline for severe symptoms, NS for hypovolemic forms, and water restriction, salt restriction, loop diuretics, vaptans, oral urea, or salt tablets according to the cause; desmopressin or D5W bring the Na+ back down if correction is too fast. High Na+ is lowered with water by mouth or tube where possible, otherwise D5W or ½NS, after NS or LR to restore volume in shock; desmopressin treats central DI, a thiazide nephrogenic DI, and a loop diuretic with D5W removes gained Na+. Na+ is checked often during correction.

Hypokalemia: shifts into cells, losses through the gut and kidney, and a hyperpolarized membrane

Hypokalemia, a plasma K+ below 3.5 mmol/L, follows a shift of K+ into cells, too little intake, or loss through the gut or, most often, the kidney. Because the resting membrane potential depends on the K+ gradient, a low K+ hyperpolarizes skeletal muscle, smooth muscle, and nerve, causing weakness, ileus, and paralysis, while it slows cardiac repolarization and predisposes to tachyarrhythmias. The cause is usually clear from the history, and urine K+, the acid-base state, and blood pressure with renin and aldosterone sort the remainder. Treatment replaces K+ and Mg2+ and addresses the cause. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes, presentation, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider its relationships to hyperkalemia, the acid-base disorders, aldosterone excess, and hypovolemia.

Key points

  • Hypokalemia is mild below 3.5 mmol/L, moderate below 3.0, and severe below 2.5.
  • Insulin and β2 agonists activate the Na+/K+-ATPase and move K+ into cells; alkalosis does so through H+ leaving cells.
  • Renal K+ loss follows more Na+ and flow reaching the collecting duct, more aldosterone or a mimic of it, or a low Mg2+; most K+ lost after vomiting is lost in the urine.
  • The ECG shows flat T waves, ST depression, U waves, and a long QU interval, with a risk of TdP and digoxin toxicity.
  • Urine K+ below 15 mmol/L points to loss outside the kidney or low intake; above it, the blood gas and BP separate RTA, vomiting and diuretics, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, and aldosterone excess.
  • K+ is replaced by mouth when possible and IV for severe or symptomatic hypokalemia, with Mg2+ corrected, since a low Mg2+ keeps K+ low.

Pathophysiology

Plasma K+ is a small extracellular share of a large intracellular store, so it falls when K+ moves into cells or when the body loses more than it takes in. Insulin activates the Na+/K+-ATPase in muscle and liver, β2 agonists activate the same pump through cAMP, and in alkalosis H+ leaves cells to buffer the blood while K+ enters in its place, although this shift is small beside the renal loss that accompanies metabolic alkalosis. In the collecting duct, Na+ taken up through ENaC leaves the lumen negative, and K+ is secreted through ROMK down that gradient; more Na+ and flow reaching the duct, as with diuretics, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, or osmotic diuresis, more aldosterone, which adds ENaC channels and pumps, and a low Mg2+, which removes a brake on ROMK, all increase K+ loss. A low K+ in turn increases H+ secretion and NH4+ production and so sustains a metabolic alkalosis. Severity is graded by plasma K+: mild below 3.5 mmol/L, often without symptoms, moderate below 3.0, and severe below 2.5, when paralysis, rhabdomyolysis, ileus, and arrhythmias occur.

Etiology and mechanisms

Shifts into cells. Insulin treatment, especially of DKA or HHS, and refeeding after starvation, which also lowers phosphate and Mg2+, move K+ into cells, as do high-dose salbutamol, adrenaline, the stress of acute illness, thyrotoxicosis, and alkalosis. Hypokalemic periodic paralysis, familial or with thyrotoxicosis, causes attacks of weakness with a normal K+ between them, and rapid blood cell production, as when megaloblastic anemia is treated, and hypothermia also lower K+.

Intake and the gut. Poor intake from eating disorders, restrictive diets, or alcohol use rarely causes hypokalemia alone, because the kidney can cut its excretion, but it adds to other causes. Diarrhea is the most common cause outside the kidney: diarrhea, laxative abuse, fistulas, and bowel diversion lose K+ with HCO3− and cause a NAGMA. Vomiting and NG suction lose little K+ directly; most is lost in the urine, because HCO3− carries Na+ to the collecting duct while volume loss raises aldosterone.

The kidney. Loop and thiazide diuretics are the usual cause of hypokalemia in HF and cirrhosis. Primary hyperaldosteronism, from an adrenal adenoma or hyperplasia, causes hypertension with low renin, and secondary hyperaldosteronism, from renal artery stenosis, a renin-secreting tumor, or malignant hypertension, causes it with high renin. Licorice, severe Cushing syndrome, and some forms of CAH let other steroids act on the MR, and in Liddle syndrome ENaC stays open; these cause hypertension with low renin and low aldosterone. RTA type 1, in which the collecting duct cannot secrete H+, and type 2, in which HCO3− escapes the proximal tubule, cause a hypokalemic NAGMA. Hypomagnesemia from alcohol use, chronic diarrhea such as Crohn disease, diuretics, or PPIs increases K+ loss and makes the hypokalemia refractory. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, inherited loss of NKCC2 or NCC, resemble lifelong loop or thiazide use, with normal BP and high renin, and Gitelman syndrome also lowers Mg2+ and urine Ca2+. Osmotic diuresis, amphotericin B, and high-dose penicillins complete the list.

Clinical presentation and complications

Symptoms usually appear below 3.0 mmol/L and are worse when K+ falls quickly. Hyperpolarized skeletal muscle and nerve cause weakness, cramps, low reflexes, and, when severe, ascending paralysis and respiratory failure; smooth muscle causes constipation and ileus. In the heart, slowed repolarization lengthens the action potential and early afterdepolarizations trigger PVCs, PSVT, A-fib, VT, VF, and TdP. The ECG shows flat or inverted T waves, ST depression, U waves that can exceed the T wave, and a long QU interval that can resemble a long QT. Arrhythmias are most dangerous with heart disease, an MI, or digoxin, whose effect a low K+ increases. Severe hypokalemia causes rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuria and AKI, and chronic hypokalemia causes nephrogenic DI, increased NH4+ production, tubulointerstitial disease and cysts, and glucose intolerance from reduced insulin release.

Diagnosis

The ECG and Mg2+ are checked first. The history of diuretics, vomiting, diarrhea, laxatives, insulin, salbutamol, or licorice usually gives the cause, and the workup set out in the review of sorts the rest. A shift leaves urine K+ low, and TSH is checked when attacks of weakness suggest thyrotoxic periodic paralysis. After diarrhea, urine K+ is low with a NAGMA; after vomiting, urine K+ is high but urine Cl− low, with an alkalosis; with current diuretics, urine K+ and Cl− are both high, and a urine diuretic screen detects hidden use. Renin and aldosterone separate primary hyperaldosteronism, with high aldosterone and low renin, from secondary, with both high, and from apparent mineralocorticoid excess and Liddle syndrome, with both low. RTA shows a NAGMA with an abnormal urine pH or urine anion gap, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes high renin and aldosterone with normal BP, and osmotic diuresis a high glucose or urine osmolality.

Management

K+ is replaced by mouth when possible and IV for severe hypokalemia, paralysis, or arrhythmias, with Mg2+ replaced at the same time, as set out in the review of . Shifts from insulin or β2 agonists are replaced cautiously, because K+ returns from cells when the cause stops, and periodic paralysis carries a particular risk of rebound hyperkalemia. Diarrhea is treated with K+ citrate or bicarbonate when there is acidosis and otherwise KCl, and vomiting with 0.9% saline and KCl, which restores volume and Cl− so the kidney stops losing K+. Diuretic-induced loss is managed by reducing the dose, adding KCl, or adding spironolactone, eplerenone, or amiloride. Primary hyperaldosteronism is treated with spironolactone or eplerenone or by adrenalectomy for a unilateral adenoma; licorice is stopped and Cushing syndrome treated, and Liddle syndrome is treated with amiloride. RTA is treated with oral K+ citrate, replacing K+ before or with alkali, hypomagnesemia with Mg2+, IV MgSO4 when severe, and Bartter and Gitelman syndromes with KCl and Mg2+, adding potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, ACEi, or ARBs if K+ stays low.

Hyperkalemia

Pathophysiology

A plasma K+ above 5.5 mmol/L means K+ has moved out of cells or the kidney cannot excrete what is taken in, from a low GFR, too little aldosterone, or resistance to it; a high K+ depolarizes cells, which first makes them more excitable and then, as Na+ channels stay inactivated, less excitable, so conduction slows and the heart can stop.

Hyperkalemia: shifts out of cells, failed excretion, and a depolarized heart

Hyperkalemia, a plasma K+ above 5.5 mmol/L, follows a shift of K+ out of cells or excretion that cannot keep up with intake, from a low GFR, too little aldosterone, or resistance to it, and a falsely high result from K+ released in the sample is its most common apparent cause. A high K+ depolarizes cells and inactivates Na+ channels, so conduction slows, and the ECG progresses from peaked T waves to loss of P waves, a wide QRS, a sine wave, and arrest. Management begins with the ECG, stabilizes the heart, shifts K+ into cells, removes it from the body, and treats the cause. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes, presentation, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider its relationships to hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis, kidney disease, and hypoaldosteronism.

Key points

  • Hyperkalemia is mild above 5.5 mmol/L, moderate above 6.0, and severe above 6.5, but the rate of rise matters more than the level.
  • Pseudohyperkalemia, from hemolysis, a long tourniquet, fist clenching, or very high white cells or platelets, is excluded by repeating the sample when the ECG is normal.
  • K+ leaves cells with insulin deficiency and hyperosmolality, acidosis, β-blockers, digoxin, succinylcholine, and cell lysis.
  • The kidney fails to excrete K+ in AKI and CKD, with low renin (diabetic nephropathy, NSAIDs, β-blockers), low aldosterone (ACEi, ARBs, heparin, adrenal insufficiency), or aldosterone resistance (spironolactone, amiloride, trimethoprim, CNIs).
  • The ECG shows peaked T waves, flat P waves, a long PR interval, and a wide QRS, progressing to a sine wave, VF, or asystole.
  • An abnormal ECG, K+ of 6.5 mmol/L or more, or weakness needs immediate Ca2+, insulin with glucose, and salbutamol, followed by removal of K+.

Pathophysiology

Hyperkalemia arises when K+ leaves cells or when the kidney cannot excrete what is taken in. Without insulin, the Na+/K+-ATPase is less active, and in DKA and HHS high glucose also draws water out of cells, carrying K+ with it; β-blockers remove β2-driven uptake and digoxin blocks the pump; in acidosis, H+ enters cells and K+ leaves, more so in a NAGMA than in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis; and cell lysis releases the large intracellular store. As extracellular K+ rises, the K+ gradient narrows and the resting potential becomes less negative, bringing cells closer to threshold and briefly more excitable; K+ channels conduct more, speeding repolarization, but Na+ channels stay inactivated, so fewer are available, the upstroke is slower, and conduction slows until the heart stops. Severity is graded as mild above 5.5 mmol/L, moderate above 6.0, and severe above 6.5, but the rate of rise matters more than the level, so chronic hyperkalemia can leave the ECG nearly normal.

Etiology and mechanisms

Pseudohyperkalemia. The most common cause of a high result is K+ released in the tube by hemolysis from a difficult draw, a syringe, a long tourniquet, or fist clenching, or by very high white cell or platelet counts.

Shifts out of cells. Insulin deficiency and hyperosmolality in DKA and HHS raise K+ despite a total body deficit, and mannitol acts the same way. Acidosis, β-blockers, digoxin toxicity, and succinylcholine, dangerous in neuromuscular disease, move K+ out of cells, and hemolysis, rhabdomyolysis from crush injury or extreme exercise, TLS after chemotherapy, burns, and massive transfusion release it. Hyperkalemic periodic paralysis causes attacks of weakness with a high K+.

Failed excretion and intake. AKI and CKD leave too little filtration and flow, and CKD often needs a drug or load to tip it into hyperkalemia. In hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism, or RTA type 4, diabetic nephropathy, NSAIDs, and β-blockers lower renin, so aldosterone falls and a hyperkalemic NAGMA follows. ACEi, ARBs, and ARNI block angiotensin II, heparin and ketoconazole reduce aldosterone synthesis, and adrenal insufficiency and some forms of CAH make too little. Spironolactone and eplerenone block the MR, amiloride, triamterene, trimethoprim, and pentamidine block ENaC, and CNIs such as tacrolimus activate NCC, so less Na+ reaches ENaC. A K+ load from the diet, supplements, IV fluids, TPN, or transfusion rarely causes hyperkalemia alone, and most cases need both a load and impaired excretion.

Clinical presentation and complications

Many patients have no symptoms until the heart is affected. The earliest ECG change is a tall, narrow, symmetric T wave, from about 5.5–6.5 mmol/L; P waves flatten and can disappear from about 6.5–7.5, the PR interval lengthens, and the QRS widens from about 7–8, merging with the T wave into a sine wave at 8–10 before VF or asystole. Bradycardia and AV block reflect slowed conduction, and arrest can come without warning when K+ rises fast. Depolarized muscle cannot fire, so weakness ascends from the legs to paralysis. A low Ca2+, as in kidney failure, worsens the cardiac effects, a high K+ reduces NH4+ production and adds to the acidosis of RTA type 4, and fear of hyperkalemia leads to ACEi, ARBs, and spironolactone being withheld in HF and CKD, where they help.

Diagnosis

An ECG comes first, and treatment starts at once if it is abnormal. Otherwise the K+ is repeated from a free-flowing vein without a tourniquet or fist clenching, with an FBC for very high white cells or platelets, before a shift, a load, and the drugs and kidney function that limit excretion are sought, as in the review of . Glucose, ketones, and a blood gas identify DKA, HHS, and acidosis, and a digoxin level identifies toxicity. Low haptoglobin with high LDH and bilirubin indicates hemolysis, CK and urine myoglobin rhabdomyolysis, and high urate and phosphate with low Ca2+ TLS. Urea, creatinine, urinalysis, and urine K+, Na+, and osmolality assess the kidney, and the drug list, renin and aldosterone, and cortisol identify hypoaldosteronism and adrenal insufficiency.

Management

An abnormal ECG, a K+ of 6.5 mmol/L or more, or weakness calls for immediate treatment: K+ sources and causative drugs are stopped, Ca2+ stabilizes the heart, insulin with glucose and salbutamol shift K+ into cells, and K+ is then removed, as set out in the review of . Pseudohyperkalemia needs only a repeat sample. DKA and HHS are treated with insulin and fluids, with K+ monitored and replaced as it falls to normal; metabolic acidosis is treated by its cause, with an IV NaHCO3 infusion; and digoxin toxicity with digoxin-specific antibody fragments. Rhabdomyolysis and TLS need IV fluids and dialysis when the kidneys fail. In CKD, a low-K+ diet, K+ binders, and a loop diuretic in patients passing urine are used, with dialysis for kidney failure or refractory hyperkalemia. NSAIDs, ACEi, ARBs, spironolactone, trimethoprim, and heparin are stopped or reduced, although a binder can let a needed ACEi or spironolactone continue, and hypoaldosteronism is treated with fludrocortisone unless there is hypertension or fluid overload, with hydrocortisone in adrenal insufficiency.

Disorders of plasma potassium: the ECG, the urine, and stabilize, shift, and remove

Plasma K+ is a small extracellular share of a large intracellular store, so it changes when K+ moves across cell membranes or when renal excretion fails to match intake. Hypokalemia hyperpolarizes cells and causes tachyarrhythmias, and hyperkalemia depolarizes them and causes conduction block, so the ECG decides urgency in both. Hypokalemia is worked up by Mg2+, shifts, urine K+, the acid-base state, and blood pressure with renin and aldosterone, and hyperkalemia by excluding a spurious result, then shifts, kidney function, drugs, and renin and aldosterone. A low K+ is replaced at a limited rate, and a high K+ is treated by stabilizing the heart, shifting K+ into cells, and removing it. Here, we compare the two disorders and review their shared workup and treatment.

Key points

  • Hypokalemia is below 3.5 mmol/L and hyperkalemia above 5.5; the ECG, not the level alone, decides urgency.
  • In hypokalemia, urine K+ below 15 mmol/L points to loss outside the kidney or low intake, and above it to renal loss, which the blood gas, urine Cl−, BP, renin, and aldosterone sort.
  • In hyperkalemia, a spurious result from K+ released in the tube is excluded first, then shifts, kidney function, drugs, and renin and aldosterone are assessed.
  • Oral KCl is first line for hypokalemia; IV KCl runs at usually 10 and at most 20 mmol an hour, in saline rather than dextrose, with Mg2+ corrected.
  • Hyperkalemia with ECG changes is treated with IV Ca2+, then insulin with glucose and salbutamol, then removal by diuretics, binders, or dialysis.
  • Shifts reverse, so K+ is checked often during treatment of either disorder.

Comparison

Hypokalemia, below 3.5 mmol/L and severe below 2.5, hyperpolarizes cells, so skeletal and smooth muscle weaken, with cramps, ileus, and rhabdomyolysis, while slowed repolarization causes PVCs, PSVT, A-fib, VT, and TdP, with flat T waves, ST depression, U waves, and a long QU interval. Hyperkalemia, above 5.5 mmol/L and severe above 6.5, depolarizes cells and inactivates Na+ channels, causing weakness ascending to paralysis and bradycardia and AV block before VF or asystole, with peaked T waves, flat P waves, a long PR interval, a wide QRS, and a sine wave. Insulin, β2 agonists, and alkalosis move K+ into cells, and insulin deficiency, β-blockers, acidosis, and cell lysis move it out. Diuretics, aldosterone excess, and a low Mg2+ are the main renal causes of hypokalemia, and AKI, CKD, ACEi, ARBs, and potassium-sparing diuretics of hyperkalemia. A spurious result is rare with a low K+ but common with a high one.

Diagnosis

In hypokalemia, urgency is judged by the ECG, symptoms, and a K+ below 2.5 mmol/L, and Mg2+ is checked because a low Mg2+ keeps K+ low. After shifts from insulin, salbutamol, alkalosis, thyrotoxicosis, or periodic paralysis are considered, a spot urine K+ above 15 mmol/L, or over 30 mmol a day, indicates renal loss. A lower urine K+ points to diarrhea or laxatives, with a NAGMA, poor intake, or a diuretic since stopped. With renal loss, an acidosis points to RTA types 1 and 2 or DKA; an alkalosis with a urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L to vomiting or past diuretics, and with a higher urine Cl− and normal BP to current diuretics or Bartter or Gitelman syndrome; and hypertension to aldosterone excess, separated by renin and aldosterone into primary hyperaldosteronism, with high aldosterone and low renin, secondary hyperaldosteronism, with both high, and apparent mineralocorticoid excess or Liddle syndrome, with both low.

In hyperkalemia, the ECG comes first, and changes, weakness, or a K+ of 6.5 mmol/L or more are treated before anything else. The sample is repeated from a free-flowing vein, looking for hemolysis or very high white cells or platelets. Shifts are sought with glucose, ketones, a blood gas, CK and urine myoglobin for rhabdomyolysis, urate, phosphate, and Ca2+ for TLS, haptoglobin, LDH, and bilirubin for hemolysis, and a digoxin level. Urea and creatinine identify AKI or CKD, usually with a drug or load added, and when the GFR does not explain the K+, the drug list and renin and aldosterone separate hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism, with both low, drugs and adrenal insufficiency, with high renin and low aldosterone, and aldosterone resistance, with both high.

Management

A low K+ is replaced with oral KCl, 60–80 mmol a day, for mild and moderate hypokalemia, or with K+ citrate or bicarbonate when there is acidosis. IV KCl, usually at 10 mmol an hour and at most 20 with monitoring, in saline rather than dextrose, whose glucose releases insulin, is used for severe or symptomatic hypokalemia, arrhythmias, digoxin toxicity, or no oral route; faster infusion risks arrhythmia, arrest, and rebound hyperkalemia. K+ is checked every 2–4 hours during IV replacement. Each 0.3 mmol/L fall reflects about 100 mmol lost, so the deficit is replaced over days, and less is given when a shift is the cause. Mg2+ is corrected, and spironolactone, eplerenone, or amiloride reduce renal loss.

A high K+ with ECG changes is first treated with IV calcium gluconate, which protects the heart without lowering K+; calcium chloride holds more Ca2+ but damages veins, and Ca2+ is never given in the same line as NaHCO3. K+ is then shifted into cells with 10 units of regular insulin and 50 mL of D50, with a D10 infusion to lower the risk of hypoglycemia, with nebulized salbutamol at higher doses than for asthma, and with an IV NaHCO3 infusion only in metabolic acidosis. Fludrocortisone replaces a missing aldosterone.

Hypocalcemia: too little PTH or calcitriol, bound Ca2+, and an excitable membrane

Hypocalcemia, a low ionized or albumin-corrected Ca2+, follows deficient or ineffective PTH, too little calcitriol, binding or precipitation of Ca2+ in plasma, or its uptake into bone. Because a low ionized Ca2+ lowers the threshold for depolarization, it causes paresthesias, tetany, Chvostek and Trousseau signs, seizures, and a long QT. The diagnosis is confirmed with corrected or ionized Ca2+, and Mg2+, phosphate, PTH, and vitamin D identify the cause. Treatment gives IV calcium gluconate for severe or symptomatic hypocalcemia, oral Ca2+ with vitamin D or calcitriol otherwise, and Mg2+ first when it is low. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes, presentation, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider its relationships to hypercalcemia, hypomagnesemia, CKD, and alkalosis.

Key points

  • Total Ca2+ is corrected by adding 0.02 mmol/L for each 1 g/L that albumin falls below 40 g/L, and ionized Ca2+ is measured when pH is abnormal, because alkalosis lowers it.
  • A low ionized Ca2+ lowers the threshold for depolarization, causing paresthesias, tetany, Chvostek and Trousseau signs, laryngospasm, seizures, and a long QT.
  • Hypoparathyroidism, about 75% postsurgical, gives low Ca2+, high phosphate, and low or inappropriately normal PTH.
  • Vitamin D deficiency and CKD raise PTH but leave Ca2+ low; vitamin D deficiency has low or normal phosphate, and CKD high phosphate.
  • A low Mg2+ causes PTH resistance and, when severe, stops PTH release, so Mg2+ is corrected before Ca2+.
  • Severe or symptomatic hypocalcemia, or a long QT, is treated with IV calcium gluconate; chronic hypocalcemia with oral Ca2+ and vitamin D or calcitriol.

Pathophysiology

Plasma Ca2+ is about half ionized and half bound, mostly to albumin, so total Ca2+ is about 2.1–2.6 mmol/L and ionized Ca2+ about 1.16–1.31 mmol/L. A low albumin lowers total Ca2+ without changing the ionized fraction, and total Ca2+ is corrected by adding 0.02 mmol/L for each 1 g/L that albumin falls below 40 g/L; alkalosis binds more Ca2+ to albumin, so ionized Ca2+ is measured when pH is abnormal. Hypocalcemia follows deficient or ineffective PTH, too little calcitriol to absorb Ca2+, binding or precipitation of Ca2+ in plasma, or its uptake into bone. A low ionized Ca2+ lowers the threshold for depolarization, so Na+ channels open closer to the resting potential and nerves fire on their own, causing tingling, cramps, and tetany, and, with severe or rapid falls, laryngospasm, seizures, and a long QT; symptoms follow the speed of the fall as much as its depth.

Etiology and mechanisms

Too little PTH, or PTH that cannot act. Postsurgical hypoparathyroidism, after thyroidectomy, parathyroidectomy, or radical neck surgery, accounts for about 75% of hypoparathyroidism, and most patients recover within a month. Autoimmune polyglandular syndrome type 1, with mucocutaneous candidiasis and adrenal insufficiency, DiGeorge syndrome, infiltration by hemochromatosis, Wilson disease, or amyloidosis, and neck irradiation are rarer causes. A low Mg2+ causes PTH resistance and, when severe, stops PTH release, and a very high Mg2+ suppresses PTH through the CaSR.

High PTH, but Ca2+ still low. Vitamin D deficiency, from low intake, malabsorption, little sun, or liver disease, reduces Ca2+ absorption and causes secondary hyperparathyroidism. In CKD, the kidney retains phosphate and makes too little calcitriol, so PTH rises without restoring Ca2+. Pseudohypoparathyroidism is inherited resistance to PTH, with low Ca2+, high phosphate, and high PTH.

Bone, drugs, and binding. After parathyroidectomy for severe hyperparathyroidism, bone takes up Ca2+ rapidly, the hungry bone syndrome, with low phosphate and Mg2+, and osteoblastic metastases such as prostate cancer act similarly. Bisphosphonates and denosumab block bone resorption, cinacalcet lowers PTH, cisplatin acts through low Mg2+, and foscarnet binds ionized Ca2+. A low albumin causes pseudohypocalcemia, alkalosis lowers ionized Ca2+, citrate from massive transfusion or CRRT binds it, acute hyperphosphatemia from TLS, rhabdomyolysis, or kidney failure precipitates it, acute pancreatitis binds it to fatty acids, and sepsis lowers it by several mechanisms. Surgery, CKD, vitamin D deficiency, low Mg2+, and pancreatitis are the most common causes.

Clinical presentation and complications

Mild hypocalcemia is often silent. Symptoms include perioral and fingertip paresthesias, cramps, tetany, and laryngospasm; the Chvostek sign, facial twitching on tapping the facial nerve, and the Trousseau sign, carpopedal spasm with a cuff inflated above systolic pressure for 2–3 minutes, reveal neuromuscular excitability. Seizures, anxiety, depression, and emotional lability may occur, and the heart shows a long QT, from a long ST segment with a normal T wave, with a risk of TdP and heart failure. Chronic hypocalcemia causes dry skin, thin hair, and brittle nails, and poorly controlled hypoparathyroidism and long-term high-dose treatment cause kidney stones and CKD. Falls below about 1.75 mmol/L, or rapid falls, can cause seizures and arrhythmias.

Diagnosis

Hypocalcemia is confirmed with albumin-corrected or ionized Ca2+, the ECG is checked, and Mg2+, phosphate, PTH, and vitamin D identify the cause, as set out in the review of . Hypoparathyroidism gives low Ca2+, high phosphate, and low or inappropriately normal PTH on two measurements; vitamin D deficiency gives low 25-hydroxyvitamin D, low or normal phosphate, and high PTH; CKD gives a low eGFR, high phosphate, and high PTH; and pseudohypoparathyroidism gives high phosphate and high PTH with normal kidney function. Management

IV calcium gluconate is given for severe symptoms, a long QT, or acute symptomatic hypocalcemia, and oral Ca2+ with vitamin D otherwise; Mg2+ is replaced first or with Ca2+, which will not correct otherwise. Hypercalcemia: PTH, PTHrP, calcitriol, and a sluggish membrane

Pathophysiology

Hypercalcemia occurs when Ca2+ enters the blood from bone or gut faster than the kidney excretes it. PTH and PTHrP increase bone resorption and renal Ca2+ reabsorption, calcitriol increases gut absorption, bone metastases and immobility release Ca2+ from bone, and thiazides and volume loss reduce excretion. A high ionized Ca2+ raises the threshold for depolarization, so nerve and muscle are harder to fire, causing weakness, low reflexes, constipation, and confusion. In the kidney, it reduces Na+ reabsorption and aquaporin-2, so urine cannot be concentrated, and its vasoconstriction and the resulting volume loss lower the GFR, which reduces Ca2+ excretion and raises Ca2+ further; this loop is why saline comes first. Hypercalcemia is mild at 2.6–3.0 mmol/L, moderate at 3.0–3.5, and a crisis above 3.5.

Etiology and mechanisms

PTH-mediated. Lithium raises the PTH set point, and teriparatide and abaloparatide cause transient rises.

Not PTH-mediated. In malignancy, PTHrP acting on PTH receptors is the most common mechanism, and osteolytic metastases release Ca2+ locally. Lymphoma and granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis and TB make calcitriol without control, and excess vitamin D or calcitriol supplements raise Ca2+ directly. Thiazides increase distal Ca2+ reabsorption, excess calcium carbonate causes milk-alkali syndrome with metabolic alkalosis and kidney injury, and immobilization and hyperthyroidism increase bone resorption. Adrenal insufficiency, pheochromocytoma, and vitamin A derivatives are rarer causes. Primary hyperparathyroidism and malignancy account for about 90% of cases.

Pathophysiology

A plasma Mg2+ below 0.7 mmol/L means too little is taken in, Mg2+ is lost through the gut or kidney, or it has moved into cells or bone; because Mg2+ is needed to release PTH and to limit K+ loss through ROMK, a low Mg2+ also lowers Ca2+ and K+, and those three together make nerve, muscle, and heart irritable.

  1. Mg2+ fallsSymptoms usually start below 0.5 mmol/L

Hypomagnesemia: losses from the gut and kidney, and the Ca2+ and K+ that follow

Hypomagnesemia, a plasma Mg2+ below 0.7 mmol/L, follows poor intake, gut or renal loss, or shifts into cells and bone, and is common in hospital and in critical illness. Mg2+ is needed to release PTH and to limit K+ secretion through ROMK, so its deficiency causes hypocalcemia and hypokalemia that resist correction, along with tetany, seizures, and arrhythmias including TdP. The cause is usually clear from the history, and the fractional excretion of Mg2+ separates renal from gut loss. Treatment replaces Mg2+, intravenously when severe, and addresses the cause. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes, presentation, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider its relationships to hypermagnesemia, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, and metabolic alkalosis.

Key points

  • Hypomagnesemia is a plasma Mg2+ below 0.7 mmol/L; symptoms usually start below 0.5, and a normal level does not exclude a deficit, because serum holds about 1% of body Mg2+.

Pathophysiology

Hypomagnesemia, a plasma Mg2+ below 0.7 mmol/L, follows poor intake, loss through the gut or kidney, or a shift into cells and bone. Mg2+ is needed by the adenylyl cyclase that releases PTH, so a low Mg2+ reduces PTH release and action and lowers Ca2+; it also normally limits K+ secretion through ROMK, so its loss lets more K+ escape in the collecting duct, causing hypokalemia in about 60%. These three disturbances together make nerve, muscle, and heart irritable, and tetany can appear before Ca2+ falls. Because serum holds about 1% of body Mg2+, a normal level does not exclude a deficit, and symptoms usually start below 0.5 mmol/L.

Etiology and mechanisms

Poor intake is common in alcohol use disorder, in about 30%, and also follows starvation, anorexia nervosa, and TPN without enough Mg2+. Gut losses come from acute or chronic diarrhea, including Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis, laxative abuse, long-term PPIs, which reduce absorption, and malabsorption or gastric bypass. Mg2+ moves into cells with insulin for DKA, refeeding, correction of acidosis, alcohol withdrawal, and acute pancreatitis, and into bone after parathyroidectomy or thyroidectomy. Renal losses follow loop diuretics, which remove the lumen-positive voltage that drives Mg2+ reabsorption in the thick ascending limb, and thiazides; aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, pentamidine, cisplatin, ciclosporin, and EGFR antibodies such as cetuximab; inherited disorders, including Gitelman syndrome, Bartter syndrome, and familial hypomagnesemia with hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis from claudin-16 or claudin-19 defects; and recovering tubules, after ATN, a relieved obstruction, or a kidney transplant.

Hypermagnesemia: an Mg2+ load the kidney cannot excrete

Hypermagnesemia is uncommon and almost always needs impaired kidney function, usually combined with an Mg2+ load from laxatives, antacids, bowel preparations, or IV MgSO4 for eclampsia. Mg2+ acts as a physiologic Ca2+ blocker, so a high level blocks ACh release at the neuromuscular junction and depresses the heart and vessels, causing loss of reflexes, then paralysis, hypotension, bradycardia, AV block, and cardiorespiratory arrest. Treatment stops every source, gives IV calcium to oppose its effects, and removes Mg2+ with saline and a loop diuretic or dialysis. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes, presentation, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider its relationships to hypomagnesemia, CKD, hyperkalemia, and hypocalcemia.

Key points

  • The kidney excretes an Mg2+ load easily until creatinine clearance falls below about 20 mL/min, so hypermagnesemia almost always needs kidney failure or a large load.
  • Causes are kidney failure, Mg2+-containing laxatives, antacids, and bowel preparations, IV MgSO4 for eclampsia, and release from hemolysis, TLS, and rhabdomyolysis.
  • Mg2+ blocks Ca2+ entry and ACh release, so loss of reflexes comes first, then flaccid paralysis, slow breathing, hypotension, bradycardia, and AV block.
  • Coma and arrest occur above about 6.2 mmol/L.
  • Treatment is to stop Mg2+, give IV calcium gluconate, and excrete Mg2+ with saline and a loop diuretic or remove it by hemodialysis.

Pathophysiology

Most filtered Mg2+ is reabsorbed passively in the thick ascending limb, and the kidney excretes an Mg2+ load readily until creatinine clearance falls below about 20 mL/min, so diet alone cannot raise Mg2+ when the kidneys work. Hypermagnesemia therefore needs impaired excretion, usually with a load. Mg2+ acts as a physiologic Ca2+ blocker: a high level blocks the Ca2+ entry that releases ACh at the neuromuscular junction, so reflexes are lost first, followed by weakness and flaccid paralysis, and it depresses the heart and vessels, causing hypotension, bradycardia, a long PR and QRS, and AV block. It is mild below about 2.9 mmol/L, moderate at about 2.9–4.9, and severe above about 4.9, with coma and arrest above about 6.2.

Etiology and mechanisms

AKI and CKD are the main causes, and hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, lithium, FHH, and hyperparathyroidism also reduce excretion. Loads come from magnesium oxide and magnesium citrate laxatives, antacids, and bowel preparations, especially in older adults with impaired kidneys or slow gut motility from opioids or anticholinergics; from IV MgSO4 for eclampsia, whose therapeutic level is 1.7–3.5 mmol/L and which can also affect the newborn; and from milk-alkali syndrome. Mg2+ is released from cells in hemolysis, because red cells hold about 3 times the Mg2+ of plasma, and in TLS, rhabdomyolysis, and acidosis such as DKA.

Clinical presentation and complications

Mild hypermagnesemia may cause only weakness, nausea, dizziness, and confusion. Higher levels cause low reflexes, drowsiness, flushing, headache, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision, mild hypotension, and bradycardia, and severe hypermagnesemia causes flaccid paralysis, slow breathing, hypotension, AV block, and lethargy, with coma and cardiorespiratory arrest above about 6.2 mmol/L. A very high Mg2+ suppresses PTH through the CaSR and can cause hypocalcemia, with choreiform movements and seizures, and coexisting hyperkalemia adds to the risk of arrest.

Diagnosis

Mg2+ is not measured routinely and its features are nonspecific, so it is checked when weakness, lost reflexes, or bradycardia appear in kidney failure or after Mg2+ has been given, with creatinine, urine output, K+, Ca2+, phosphate, a blood gas, and an ECG, as in the review of . The history identifies laxatives, antacids, bowel preparations, and MgSO4, and TSH, cortisol, a lithium level, a hemolysis screen, CK, urate, and phosphate identify the rarer causes.

Management

Every source of Mg2+ is stopped; mild hypermagnesemia with normal kidney function needs nothing more, given a half-life of about 28 hours. Symptomatic patients receive IV calcium gluconate, which opposes Mg2+ at the heart and neuromuscular junction, and saline with a loop diuretic to increase excretion, with monitoring of Ca2+ and Mg2+. Hemodialysis lowers Mg2+ by up to half in 3–4 hours in kidney failure or severe symptoms, though it can also lower Ca2+. During MgSO4 for eclampsia, the infusion is stopped if urine output falls below 80 mL in 4 hours, reflexes disappear, or breathing falls below 12 a minute, and 10 mL of 10% calcium gluconate serves as an antidote.

Disorders of calcium and magnesium: reading Ca2+ against PTH, and Mg2+ first

Ca2+ and Mg2+ are held mostly in bone, reabsorbed largely in the thick ascending limb, and set the excitability of nerve and muscle, so their disorders share a workup. Ca2+ is confirmed with albumin-corrected or ionized values and read against PTH, which separates parathyroid from other causes in both directions, and Mg2+ is checked whenever Ca2+ or K+ is low. Treatment replaces low Ca2+ and Mg2+, lowers high Ca2+ by restoring volume and blocking bone resorption, and opposes high Mg2+ with calcium while removing it. Here, we compare the four disorders and review their shared workup and treatment.

Key points

  • Total Ca2+ is corrected for albumin, and ionized Ca2+ is measured when pH is abnormal.
  • In hypocalcemia, a low or normal PTH points to the parathyroids or a low Mg2+, and a high PTH to vitamin D deficiency, CKD, or PTH resistance, separated by phosphate and eGFR.
  • In hypercalcemia, a high or normal PTH points to hyperparathyroidism or FHH, separated by urine Ca2+, and a suppressed PTH to PTHrP, calcitriol, vitamin D, or bone involvement.
  • An FEMg above 2% with normal kidney function indicates renal Mg2+ loss.
  • IV calcium gluconate treats severe hypocalcemia and symptomatic hypermagnesemia; saline, calcitonin, and a bisphosphonate or denosumab treat hypercalcemia; IV MgSO4 treats severe hypomagnesemia.
  • Mg2+ is corrected before Ca2+ and K+.

Comparison

Hypocalcemia lowers the threshold for firing, causing paresthesias, tetany, Chvostek and Trousseau signs, seizures, and a long QT, and its common causes are surgery, CKD, vitamin D deficiency, low Mg2+, and pancreatitis; hypercalcemia raises it, causing polyuria, stones, bone pain, constipation, confusion, and a short QT, and is caused mostly by primary hyperparathyroidism and malignancy. Hypomagnesemia, common in hospital, increases excitability, causes arrhythmias including TdP, and brings low Ca2+ and K+ that resist correction; hypermagnesemia, mostly confined to kidney failure with an Mg2+ load, depresses excitability and causes lost reflexes, paralysis, bradycardia, and AV block.

Diagnosis

In hypocalcemia, the value is confirmed with albumin-corrected or ionized Ca2+, the ECG is checked, Mg2+ is measured and replaced first, and PTH and phosphate, with vitamin D and creatinine, identify the cause: a low or normal PTH with high phosphate indicates hypoparathyroidism, and with a low Mg2+ a functional hypoparathyroidism, whereas a high PTH with low or normal phosphate indicates vitamin D deficiency, with high phosphate and a low eGFR CKD, and with high phosphate and a normal eGFR pseudohypoparathyroidism.

In hypercalcemia, the value is confirmed, dehydration and drugs are considered, and PTH divides the causes. A high or inappropriately normal PTH with high urine Ca2+ indicates primary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism, with low urine Ca2+, a CCCR below 0.01, FHH, and with lithium a raised set point; a suppressed PTH leads to PTHrP for malignancy, calcitriol for lymphoma or granulomas, 25-hydroxyvitamin D for toxicity, and electrophoresis, ALP, imaging, and TSH for the rest. In hypomagnesemia, the history usually gives the cause and an FEMg above 2% with normal kidney function indicates renal loss; in hypermagnesemia, kidney function and the history of Mg2+ intake are assessed.

Management

A low Ca2+ is raised with IV calcium gluconate, 1–2 g over 10–20 minutes, for severe symptoms, a long QT, or acute symptomatic hypocalcemia, never in a line with HCO3− or phosphate, and otherwise with 1500–2000 mg of oral elemental Ca2+ a day, using calcium citrate in PPI users, plus vitamin D or calcitriol; PTH analogs are used in poorly controlled hypoparathyroidism. A high Ca2+ is lowered with IV saline, then calcitonin, which works within about 2 hours but lasts only 4–7 days, and zoledronic acid, which takes about 3 days, or denosumab, which suits kidney impairment; glucocorticoids block calcitriol production in lymphoma and granulomatous disease, cinacalcet lowers PTH when surgery is not possible, and dialysis treats severe hypercalcemia with kidney failure.

A low Mg2+ is corrected with IV MgSO4, 1–2 g over about 15 minutes when unstable or in TdP and over 1 hour when symptomatic, or 4–8 g over 12–24 hours otherwise, at half the dose in kidney failure and for about 2 days after the level normalizes, and with oral Mg2+ when mild; amiloride reduces renal loss. A high Mg2+ is opposed with IV calcium gluconate and removed with saline and a loop diuretic or hemodialysis. Mg2+ is corrected before Ca2+ and K+.
