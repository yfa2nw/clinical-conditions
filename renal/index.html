Metabolic Acidosis and Alkalosis

Pathophysiology

A gain of acid or a loss of HCO3− lowers plasma HCO3− and pH (metabolic acidosis), and a loss of H+ or a gain of HCO3− raises them (metabolic alkalosis); the lungs then change pCO2 to pull the pH back toward normal.

H+ + HCO3− ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ CO2 + H2O
FeatureNAGMAAGMAMetabolic alkalosis
What happensHCO3− is lost from the kidney or gut, or the kidney fails to make new HCO3−An acid is added; H+ uses up HCO3−, and the acid's anion stays in the bloodH+ is lost from the stomach or kidney, or HCO3− is gained
HCO3− is replaced byCl−, so it is also called hyperchloremic acidosisAn unmeasured anion: lactate, ketoanions, formate, glycolate, sulfate, or phosphateNothing is replaced; Cl− often falls too
Anion gapNormalHighNormal
LungsHyperventilate to lower pCO2Hyperventilate to lower pCO2Hypoventilate to raise pCO2
Main sourcesKidney: CKD, RTA. Gut: diarrhea, fistulasGOLDMARK: glycols, oxoproline, L-lactate, D-lactate, methanol, aspirin, renal failure, ketoacidosisGut: vomiting, NG tube suction. Kidney: diuretics, aldosterone excess

What a change in pH does

  • AH+ moves into cellsCells buffer excess H+, which softens the fall in plasma pH
  • BK+ moves outK+ leaves cells to keep the charge balanced → hyperkalemia, most marked when the acid is a mineral acid such as HCl or NH4Cl
  • COrganic acids enter with their anionLactate and ketoanions cross into cells together with H+, so little K+ has to leave; organic acidoses shift less K+
  • DAlkalosis reverses the shiftH+ leaves cells and K+ enters them → hypokalemia, made worse by K+ lost in urine
  • EAlbumin binds more Ca2+In alkalosis, albumin gives up H+ and binds more Ca2+ in its place → ionized Ca2+ falls while total Ca2+ is unchanged → tetany

Exceptions to the H+ and K+ shift

  • Organic acidosesLactic acidosis and ketoacidosis raise K+ less than a mineral acid does, because the anion enters cells with its H+ (C)
  • DKAK+ is high mainly from insulin deficiency and high osmolality, although total body K+ is low; insulin moves K+ back into cells and can cause hypokalemia
  • RTA types 1 and 2, and diarrheaK+ is low despite acidosis, because K+ is lost in urine or stool faster than it shifts out of cells
  • Correcting acidosisK+ moves back into cells, so treating acidosis can unmask a low total body K+

Why metabolic alkalosis lasts

Healthy kidneys excrete extra HCO3− quickly, so an alkalosis lasts only while something keeps the kidney reabsorbing it.

  1. GenerationH+ lost from the stomach or kidney, or HCO3− added
  2. Low volumeAngiotensin II increases proximal HCO3− reabsorption; aldosterone increases distal H+ and K+ secretion
  3. Low Cl−Too little Cl− reaches the collecting duct to swap for HCO3−, so HCO3− cannot be secreted
  4. Low K+Increases H+ secretion and NH4+ production, which makes new HCO3−
  5. Maintained alkalosisLasts until volume, Cl−, and K+ are replaced

Etiology

Metabolic acidosis

  • NAGMA: kidney, low GFR
  • 1CKDFewer nephrons make less NH4+, so less acid is excreted and less new HCO3− is made; a NAGMA in earlier CKD becomes an AGMA (14) as GFR falls further
  • NAGMA: kidney, normal GFR
  • 2RTA type 1 (distal)α-intercalated cells cannot secrete H+, so urine cannot be acidified; autoimmune disease (Sjögren syndrome, SLE), lithium, amphotericin B, and toluene
  • 3RTA type 2 (proximal)The proximal tubule, which normally reclaims 85–90% of filtered HCO3−, reclaims less, so HCO3− spills into urine until plasma HCO3− falls below the lowered threshold; multiple myeloma, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide, topiramate), tenofovir, and Fanconi syndrome
  • 4RTA type 4 (hypoaldosteronism) Most common RTAToo little aldosterone, or resistance to it, cuts H+ and K+ secretion, and the high K+ suppresses NH4+ production; diabetic nephropathy, ACEi, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, heparin, trimethoprim, and adrenal insufficiency
  • NAGMA: gut and other
  • 5DiarrheaIntestinal fluid beyond the stomach is rich in HCO3−, so losing it lowers HCO3−, and the kidney keeps Cl− in its place; K+ is lost too; secretory diarrhea loses more HCO3− than inflammatory diarrhea
  • 6Pancreatic fistulas and other bicarbonate-rich lossesPancreatic, biliary, and small bowel fluid drains outside the body; a ureterosigmoidostomy lets the colon swap urinary Cl− for HCO3−
  • 7Cl− load and drugsLarge volumes of 0.9% saline, which has 154 mmol/L Cl− and no HCO3−; TPN without acetate; cholestyramine
  • AGMA: GOLDMARK
  • 8GlycolsEthylene glycol in antifreeze is made into glycolic acid, which causes the acidosis, and oxalic acid, which forms calcium oxalate crystals in the tubules → AKI and hypocalcemia; propylene glycol is made into lactate
  • 9Oxoproline5-oxoproline (pyroglutamic acid) builds up with chronic paracetamol use, especially with malnutrition or chronic illness
  • 10L-lactateType A: too little oxygen delivered (shock, sepsis, ischemia) → anaerobic glycolysis. Type B: no hypoxia, but mitochondria cannot use pyruvate (metformin, liver failure, thiamine deficiency)
  • 11D-lactateIn short bowel syndrome, colonic bacteria ferment unabsorbed carbohydrate into D-lactate, which humans metabolize poorly
  • 12MethanolWindscreen washer fluid, solvents, and contaminated spirits; made into formic acid, which damages the retina and basal ganglia
  • 13AspirinSalicylates stimulate the respiratory center first (respiratory alkalosis), then uncouple oxidative phosphorylation → lactate and ketoacids
  • 14Renal failure (uremic acidosis)Advanced CKD or AKI retains sulfate, phosphate, urate, and hippurate; rhabdomyolysis releases acids and phosphate
  • 15KetoacidosisLow insulin and high glucagon → lipolysis → β-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate; DKA, AKA after a binge followed by vomiting and poor intake, and starvation

Metabolic alkalosis

  • Gut
  • 16Vomiting and NG tube suctionLosing HCl leaves the HCO3− made by parietal cells in the blood; the volume and Cl− loss then keeps the kidney reabsorbing HCO3−; also chloride-losing diarrhea (villous adenoma, congenital chloridorrhea) and Cl− lost in the sweat of cystic fibrosis
  • Kidney
  • 17Loop and thiazide diureticsMore Na+ reaches the collecting duct, where aldosterone, raised by the volume loss, swaps it for H+ and K+; the extracellular fluid also shrinks around the same amount of HCO3− (contraction alkalosis)
  • 18Primary hyperaldosteronismAn adrenal adenoma (Conn syndrome) or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia makes aldosterone regardless of renin → Na+ retention, hypertension, H+ and K+ loss
  • 19Secondary hyperaldosteronismHigh renin drives aldosterone: renal artery stenosis and renin-secreting tumors; low effective volume (heart failure, cirrhosis) does the same and keeps other alkaloses going
  • 20Apparent mineralocorticoid excessThe collecting duct acts as if aldosterone were high: Cushing syndrome (cortisol on the mineralocorticoid receptor), licorice (blocks 11β-HSD2, which normally inactivates cortisol), and Liddle syndrome (overactive ENaC)
  • 21Bartter and Gitelman syndromesInherited loss of NKCC2 in the loop (Bartter, like a lifelong loop diuretic) or NCC in the distal tubule (Gitelman, like a lifelong thiazide)
  • Other
  • 22Alkali loadMilk-alkali syndrome from calcium carbonate, HCO3− tablets or infusions, citrate from massive transfusion, and acetate in TPN; lasts mainly when the kidney cannot excrete HCO3− (CKD, low volume)
  • 23After hypercapniaIn chronic respiratory acidosis (COPD), the kidney retains HCO3−; when ventilation lowers pCO2 quickly, the extra HCO3− is left behind

4. Anion gap

Plasma has as many positive as negative charges; the gap is the negative charge that is not measured, mostly albumin. An acid with an unmeasured anion widens it, while HCO3− lost and replaced by Cl− leaves it unchanged.

AG = (Na+ + K+) − (Cl− + HCO3−); normal 12–16 mmol/L
  • Without K+AG = Na+ − (Cl− + HCO3−); normal 8–12 mmol/L
  • Low albuminEach 10 g/L fall in albumin lowers the gap by about 2.5, which can hide an AGMA; some add 2.5 for each 10 g/L below 40

Treatment

Treat the cause, and replace volume, K+, and Cl− as needed; HCO3− or acid is given only in particular situations.

Giving HCO3−

  • NAGMAHCO3− has truly been lost, so oral alkali (NaHCO3 or potassium citrate) is used for RTA and CKD
  • AGMA from lactate or ketoacidsNot routine: once the cause is treated, lactate and ketoanions are metabolized back into HCO3−, so added HCO3− can overshoot into alkalosis
  • RisksHypernatremia, fluid overload, low ionized Ca2+, and hypokalemia as K+ moves into cells

Review article

Metabolic acidosis and alkalosis: the anion gap, the urine, and what keeps a disorder going

Metabolic acidosis is a fall in plasma HCO3− from a gain of acid or a loss of HCO3−, and metabolic alkalosis is a rise in HCO3− from a loss of H+ or a gain of HCO3−; in both, the lungs compensate by changing pCO2. The anion gap divides acidosis into a normal gap form (NAGMA), in which HCO3− lost from the kidney or gut is replaced by Cl−, and a high gap form (AGMA), in which an added acid leaves its anion in the blood. The urine anion gap, plasma K+, and urine pH separate the renal causes of NAGMA, the delta-delta ratio and osmolal gap refine AGMA, and urine Cl− divides alkalosis into chloride-responsive and chloride-resistant forms. Here, we review the mechanisms, causes, consequences, diagnosis, and management of both disorders, and consider their relationship to disorders of potassium, aldosterone, volume, and kidney function.

Key points

  • The anion gap, (Na+ + K+) − (Cl− + HCO3−), is normally 12–16 mmol/L; it is high when an acid adds an unmeasured anion and normal when HCO3− is replaced by Cl−.
  • Expected compensation is pCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3− + 8 (± 2) in metabolic acidosis and 0.7 × HCO3− + 20 in metabolic alkalosis; a mismatch means a second, respiratory disorder.
  • A negative urine anion gap points to gut losses, and a positive one to RTA types 1 and 4 or CKD; K+ is low in RTA types 1 and 2 and high in type 4, and urine pH stays above 5.5 only in type 1.
  • The causes of AGMA follow GOLDMARK; an osmolal gap above 15 with an AGMA suggests methanol or ethylene glycol.
  • Urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive alkalosis that corrects with saline and KCl; above 20, BP, renin, and aldosterone separate the causes.
  • HCO3− is replaced in NAGMA but is not routine in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis, whose anions are metabolized back into HCO3− once the cause is treated.

Pathophysiology

Plasma pH is set by the balance H+ + HCO3− ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ CO2 + H2O, with the kidney regulating HCO3− and the lungs regulating CO2. In NAGMA, HCO3− is lost from the kidney or gut, or the kidney fails to make new HCO3−, and Cl− takes its place, giving a hyperchloremic acidosis with a normal anion gap. In AGMA, an added acid uses up HCO3−, and its anion (lactate, ketoanions, formate, glycolate, sulfate, or phosphate) stays in the blood and widens the gap. In metabolic alkalosis, H+ is lost from the stomach or kidney, or HCO3− is gained, and Cl− often falls too. The lungs hyperventilate to lower pCO2 in acidosis and hypoventilate to raise it in alkalosis.

A change in pH moves H+ and K+ across cell membranes. In acidosis, cells take up H+, which softens the fall in pH, and K+ leaves them to keep the charge balanced, causing hyperkalemia that is most marked with mineral acids such as HCl and NH4Cl. Organic acids such as lactate and ketoacids enter cells together with their H+, so they shift less K+. Alkalosis reverses the shift, moving K+ into cells, and makes albumin give up H+ and bind more Ca2+, so ionized Ca2+ falls while total Ca2+ is unchanged. There are exceptions to the usual shift: in DKA, K+ is high mainly from insulin deficiency and high osmolality although total body K+ is low, and insulin moves K+ back into cells; in RTA types 1 and 2 and in diarrhea, K+ is low despite acidosis because it is lost in urine or stool faster than it shifts out of cells; and correcting any acidosis moves K+ back into cells and can unmask a low total body K+.

Healthy kidneys excrete extra HCO3− quickly, so a metabolic alkalosis lasts only while something keeps the kidney reabsorbing it. After the alkalosis is generated, low volume raises angiotensin II, which increases proximal HCO3− reabsorption, and aldosterone, which increases distal H+ and K+ secretion. Low Cl− leaves too little Cl− in the collecting duct to exchange for HCO3−, so HCO3− cannot be secreted, and low K+ increases H+ secretion and NH4+ production, which makes new HCO3−. The alkalosis lasts until volume, Cl−, and K+ are replaced.

Etiology and mechanisms

NAGMA. In CKD, fewer nephrons make less NH4+, so less acid is excreted and less new HCO3− is made; the NAGMA of earlier CKD becomes an AGMA as GFR falls further. With a normal GFR, the renal causes are the RTAs. In RTA type 1 (distal), α-intercalated cells cannot secrete H+, so urine cannot be acidified; causes include autoimmune disease (Sjögren syndrome and SLE), lithium, amphotericin B, and toluene. In RTA type 2 (proximal), the proximal tubule, which normally reclaims 85–90% of filtered HCO3−, reclaims less, so HCO3− spills into urine until plasma HCO3− falls below the lowered threshold; causes include multiple myeloma, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide and topiramate), tenofovir, and Fanconi syndrome. RTA type 4, the most common RTA, results from too little aldosterone or resistance to it, which cuts H+ and K+ secretion, while the resulting high K+ suppresses NH4+ production; causes include diabetic nephropathy, ACEi, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, heparin, trimethoprim, and adrenal insufficiency. Among gut causes, intestinal fluid beyond the stomach is rich in HCO3−, so diarrhea lowers HCO3−, the kidney keeps Cl− in its place, and K+ is lost too; secretory diarrhea loses more HCO3− than inflammatory diarrhea. Pancreatic fistulas and other bicarbonate-rich losses act in the same way, as pancreatic, biliary, or small bowel fluid drains outside the body, and a ureterosigmoidostomy lets the colon swap urinary Cl− for HCO3−. A Cl− load causes NAGMA when large volumes of 0.9% saline, with 154 mmol/L Cl− and no HCO3−, are given, and TPN without acetate and cholestyramine can do the same.

AGMA. The causes follow GOLDMARK. Ethylene glycol in antifreeze is made into glycolic acid, which causes the acidosis, and oxalic acid, which forms calcium oxalate crystals in the tubules, causing AKI and hypocalcemia; propylene glycol is made into lactate. 5-oxoproline (pyroglutamic acid) builds up with chronic paracetamol use, especially with malnutrition or chronic illness. L-lactic acidosis is type A when too little oxygen is delivered, as in shock, sepsis, and ischemia, driving anaerobic glycolysis, and type B when there is no hypoxia but mitochondria cannot use pyruvate, as with metformin, liver failure, and thiamine deficiency. D-lactate forms in short bowel syndrome, when colonic bacteria ferment unabsorbed carbohydrate into an isomer that humans metabolize poorly. Methanol, found in windscreen washer fluid, solvents, and contaminated spirits, is made into formic acid, which damages the retina and basal ganglia. Salicylates first stimulate the respiratory center, causing a respiratory alkalosis, then uncouple oxidative phosphorylation, producing lactate and ketoacids. Renal failure, in advanced CKD or AKI, retains sulfate, phosphate, urate, and hippurate, and rhabdomyolysis releases acids and phosphate. In ketoacidosis, low insulin and high glucagon drive lipolysis and the production of β-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate, as in DKA, AKA after a binge followed by vomiting and poor intake, and starvation.

Metabolic alkalosis. Vomiting and NG tube suction remove HCl, leaving the HCO3− made by parietal cells in the blood, and the volume and Cl− loss then keeps the kidney reabsorbing HCO3−; chloride-losing diarrhea (villous adenoma and congenital chloridorrhea) and the Cl− lost in the sweat of cystic fibrosis act similarly. Loop and thiazide diuretics deliver more Na+ to the collecting duct, where aldosterone, raised by the volume loss, exchanges it for H+ and K+, and the extracellular fluid shrinks around the same amount of HCO3−, a contraction alkalosis. In primary hyperaldosteronism, an adrenal adenoma (Conn syndrome) or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia makes aldosterone regardless of renin, causing Na+ retention, hypertension, and H+ and K+ loss. In secondary hyperaldosteronism, high renin drives aldosterone, as in renal artery stenosis and renin-secreting tumors, and low effective volume in heart failure and cirrhosis does the same and keeps other alkaloses going. In apparent mineralocorticoid excess, the collecting duct behaves as if aldosterone were high: in Cushing syndrome, cortisol acts on the mineralocorticoid receptor; licorice blocks 11β-HSD2, which normally inactivates cortisol; and in Liddle syndrome, ENaC is overactive. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes are inherited losses of NKCC2 in the loop, like a lifelong loop diuretic, and of NCC in the distal tubule, like a lifelong thiazide. An alkali load, from calcium carbonate in milk-alkali syndrome, HCO3− tablets or infusions, citrate from massive transfusion, or acetate in TPN, lasts mainly when the kidney cannot excrete HCO3−, as in CKD or low volume. Finally, in chronic respiratory acidosis from COPD, the kidney retains HCO3−, and when ventilation lowers pCO2 quickly, the extra HCO3− is left behind.

Clinical presentation and complications

Metabolic acidosis causes Kussmaul breathing, deep and rapid breaths that blow off CO2 and can tire the respiratory muscles. K+ is often high, but not always: organic acidoses shift less K+, and RTA types 1 and 2 and diarrhea cause hypokalemia. Ionized Ca2+ rises, and chronic acidosis draws on bone to buffer acid, causing osteomalacia, rickets, and stones. The heart contracts more weakly, vessels dilate, and the response to catecholamines is poor, causing hypotension, with arrhythmias. The oxygen dissociation curve shifts right, releasing more O2 to tissues. Patients have headache, lethargy, confusion, and coma, with nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and fatigue; acidosis causes insulin resistance, and in the long term, muscle wasting and faster CKD progression.

Metabolic alkalosis causes shallow, slow breathing that is usually mild but can worsen hypercapnia and hypoxemia in COPD or obesity hypoventilation. Hypokalemia causes muscle weakness and arrhythmias. Ionized Ca2+ falls, causing perioral numbness, paresthesias, carpopedal spasm, Chvostek and Trousseau signs, and tetany. Arrhythmias come from low K+ and low Ca2+, and vessels constrict, including in the brain. The oxygen dissociation curve shifts left, releasing less O2 to tissues, and patients may have confusion, delirium, seizures, muscle cramps, and weakness.

Some findings point to the cause. Hypotension, tachycardia, and dry mucous membranes suggest volume loss from diarrhea, vomiting, or diuretics, or adrenal insufficiency, and hypertension with low K+ suggests aldosterone excess or its mimics. Fruity breath, polyuria, and polydipsia suggest DKA; blurred vision after drinking, methanol; inebriation, flank pain, hematuria, and tetany, ethylene glycol; and tinnitus with fast breathing, aspirin. Calcium phosphate stones and nephrocalcinosis suggest RTA type 1, with alkaline urine, high urine Ca2+, and low urine citrate, and bone pain and osteomalacia with glycosuria suggest RTA type 2 with Fanconi syndrome. Pruritus, nocturia, oliguria, and asterixis suggest renal failure, and failure to thrive, polyuria, and vomiting in a child suggest Bartter or Gitelman syndrome.

Diagnosis

Normal values are pH 7.35–7.45, pCO2 35–45 mmHg, HCO3− 22–26 mmol/L, and an anion gap of 12–16 mmol/L. On an ABG or VBG, the first step is whether the pH is low (acidemia, below 7.35) or high (alkalemia, above 7.45). The second is which value explains it: HCO3− moving the same way as pH means a metabolic disorder, and pCO2 moving the opposite way means a respiratory one. In metabolic disorders, pCO2 compensates within hours, falling in acidosis and rising in alkalosis, whereas in respiratory disorders HCO3− compensates over days, rising in respiratory acidosis as the kidney retains it and falling in respiratory alkalosis as the kidney excretes it.

The third step is whether compensation is as expected. In metabolic acidosis, the Winter formula predicts pCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3− + 8 (± 2); other rules are that pCO2 is about the last two digits of the pH, or falls about 1.2 mmHg for each 1 mmol/L fall in HCO3−. In metabolic alkalosis, expected pCO2 = 0.7 × HCO3− + 20 (± 5), or rises 0.6–0.7 mmHg for each 1 mmol/L rise in HCO3− above 24; it rarely passes 55 mmHg, because hypoxia drives breathing. A pCO2 higher than expected means a respiratory acidosis as well, and one lower than expected a respiratory alkalosis as well, as in aspirin poisoning.

In metabolic acidosis, the anion gap is calculated as (Na+ + K+) − (Cl− + HCO3−), normally 12–16 mmol/L. Plasma has as many positive as negative charges, and the gap is the negative charge that is not measured, mostly albumin, so an acid with an unmeasured anion widens it, while HCO3− lost and replaced by Cl− leaves it unchanged. Without K+, the gap is Na+ − (Cl− + HCO3−), normally 8–12 mmol/L. Each 10 g/L fall in albumin lowers the gap by about 2.5, which can hide an AGMA, and some add 2.5 for each 10 g/L below 40.

In NAGMA, the urine anion gap (urine Na+ + urine K+ − urine Cl−) separates kidney from gut. A negative gap means the kidney is excreting plenty of NH4+, which leaves with Cl−, a normal renal response to HCO3− lost elsewhere, as in diarrhea, fistulas, or saline. A positive gap means the kidney cannot excrete NH4+, as in RTA types 1 and 4 or CKD. RTA type 1 has a normal GFR, low K+, a urine pH above 5.5 even in severe acidosis, plasma HCO3− that can fall below 10 mmol/L, and a positive urine anion gap, with high urine Ca2+, low urine citrate, calcium phosphate stones, and nephrocalcinosis. RTA type 2 has a normal GFR, low K+, a urine pH below 5.5 once HCO3− is below the threshold but above 5.5 when HCO3− is given, plasma HCO3− usually 12–18 mmol/L, and a variable urine anion gap, with Fanconi syndrome (glycosuria with normal blood glucose, phosphaturia, aminoaciduria, and low urate) and bone disease. RTA type 4 has a normal or mildly low GFR, high K+, a urine pH below 5.5, plasma HCO3− usually above 17 mmol/L, and a positive urine anion gap, with diabetes or culprit drugs and high urine Na+ despite a low-salt diet. CKD has a low GFR, normal or high K+, a urine pH usually below 5.5, HCO3− that falls as GFR falls, a positive urine anion gap, and high urea and creatinine. Before diagnosing an RTA, a UTI with a urease-producing organism, which raises urine pH, and low volume, which limits distal H+ secretion, are excluded; urine Na+ should be above 25 mmol/L.

In AGMA, the delta-delta ratio, (AG − 16) ÷ (24 − HCO3−), shows whether a second metabolic disorder is present, using 12 in place of 16 when K+ is left out of the gap. In a pure AGMA, each H+ that uses up one HCO3− leaves one unmeasured anion behind, so the rise in the gap roughly matches the fall in HCO3−. A ratio below 1 means HCO3− fell more than the gap rose, an AGMA with a NAGMA, as in DKA with diarrhea; 1–2 is a pure AGMA; and above 2 means HCO3− fell less than the gap rose, an AGMA with a metabolic alkalosis, as in DKA with vomiting, or a high HCO3− from before. The osmolal gap is measured osmolality minus calculated osmolality, 2 × Na+ + glucose + urea, normally below 10 and concerning above 15; other formulas are 2 × (Na+ + K+) + glucose + urea and, in US units, 2 × Na+ + glucose ÷ 18 + BUN ÷ 2.8, and some add ethanol when it is present. A high osmolal gap with an AGMA suggests methanol or ethylene glycol. A high osmolal gap without acidosis suggests an early toxic alcohol, before it is metabolized, or isopropyl alcohol, which is made into acetone, an uncharged ketone that causes ketosis without acidosis. A normal osmolal gap late after ingestion does not exclude a toxic alcohol, because as the alcohol becomes acid, the osmolal gap closes and the anion gap widens.

Each AGMA cause has its own findings. Glycols give a high osmolal gap early, low Ca2+, rising creatinine, calcium oxalate crystals, and hematuria, after inebriation followed by flank pain and oliguria. Oxoproline gives normal lactate, no ketones, and 5-oxoproline in urine, with chronic paracetamol use and malnutrition. L-lactate is above 4 mmol/L in shock, sepsis, ischemia, metformin use, and liver failure, and D-lactate is high in short bowel syndrome. Methanol gives a high osmolal gap early, with blurred vision, dilated pupils, and papilledema. Aspirin gives a respiratory alkalosis with an AGMA and a measurable salicylate level, with tinnitus, nausea, and fast breathing. Renal failure gives high urea, creatinine, and phosphate, with reduced urine output. DKA gives high glucose and β-hydroxybutyrate, with K+ normal or high, and ketones and glucose in urine, after missed insulin, infection, or new diabetes. AKA and starvation give low or normal glucose with high β-hydroxybutyrate, and the urine dipstick, which detects acetoacetate, can show few ketones.

In metabolic alkalosis, urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive alkalosis, in which the kidney holds on to Cl− because volume and Cl− are low: vomiting and NG tube suction, diuretics that have worn off, chloride-losing diarrhea, cystic fibrosis, and the period after hypercapnia. Urine Cl− above 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-resistant alkalosis: diuretics still acting, aldosterone excess and its mimics, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, and severe hypokalemia. Urine Cl− is high while a diuretic acts and low once it wears off, so a changing result suggests hidden diuretic use. Vomiting and diuretics give low or normal BP with high renin and aldosterone and K+ lost mostly in urine. Primary hyperaldosteronism gives high BP, low renin, high aldosterone, and a high aldosterone-to-renin ratio; secondary hyperaldosteronism gives high BP with both high; and apparent mineralocorticoid excess gives high BP with both low. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes give low or normal BP with high renin and aldosterone, with high urine Ca2+ in Bartter syndrome and low urine Ca2+ with low Mg2+ in Gitelman syndrome. An alkali load gives variable values, with high Ca2+ in milk-alkali syndrome.

Management

Treatment is aimed at the cause, with volume, K+, and Cl− replaced as needed; HCO3− or acid is given only in particular situations. In NAGMA, HCO3− has truly been lost, so oral alkali, NaHCO3 or potassium citrate, is used for RTA and CKD. In AGMA from lactate or ketoacids, HCO3− is not routine: once the cause is treated, lactate and ketoanions are metabolized back into HCO3−, so added HCO3− can overshoot into alkalosis. Its risks include hypernatremia, fluid overload, low ionized Ca2+, and hypokalemia as K+ moves into cells.

In CKD, oral NaHCO3 protects bone and muscle and slows progression, and dialysis is needed when the disease is advanced. RTA type 1 responds to oral alkali in modest doses, with potassium citrate also replacing K+ and preventing stones, alongside treatment of the cause. RTA type 2 needs large doses of alkali with K+, because HCO3− given above the threshold is lost in urine and carries K+ with it; a thiazide can help by shrinking volume, and phosphate and vitamin D treat Fanconi bone disease. RTA type 4 is managed by stopping culprit drugs, a low-potassium diet, and a loop or thiazide diuretic, with fludrocortisone for aldosterone deficiency without hypertension or edema. Diarrhea and fistulas are managed by treating the cause, replacing fluid, K+, and HCO3−, and controlling the fistula, and saline-induced acidosis by switching to a balanced crystalloid such as Hartmann solution or Plasma-Lyte. Toxic alcohols are treated with fomepizole, or ethanol if it is unavailable, to block alcohol dehydrogenase, with hemodialysis for severe acidosis, visual change, AKI, or instability, and thiamine and pyridoxine for ethylene glycol. Oxoproline acidosis is treated by stopping paracetamol. Type A lactic acidosis is treated by restoring perfusion with fluids, vasopressors, ABX, and source control, and type B with thiamine and by stopping metformin, with hemodialysis if severe. D-lactic acidosis is treated by addressing the short bowel cause and limiting carbohydrate reaching the colon. Aspirin poisoning is treated with activated charcoal, IV NaHCO3 to alkalinize the urine and trap salicylate there, and hemodialysis if severe. Renal failure is treated with dialysis. DKA is treated with IV fluids, then insulin once K+ is at least 3.5 mmol/L, with K+ replacement, continuing insulin until the gap closes, and AKA and starvation with IV fluids with dextrose and thiamine and gradual feeding, watching for refeeding syndrome.

Chloride-responsive alkalosis, from vomiting, NG tube suction, or after hypercapnia, is treated with 0.9% saline with KCl, which restores volume and Cl− so the kidney can excrete HCO3−, and the cause of vomiting is treated. Diuretic-induced alkalosis is managed by stopping or reducing the diuretic, replacing KCl, or adding a potassium-sparing diuretic, with acetazolamide if the patient is fluid overloaded. Primary hyperaldosteronism is treated with spironolactone or eplerenone, or adrenalectomy for a unilateral adenoma, and secondary hyperaldosteronism by treating its cause, such as renal artery stenosis or a renin-secreting tumor. In apparent mineralocorticoid excess, Cushing syndrome is treated, licorice is stopped, and Liddle syndrome is treated with amiloride, since its ENaC does not need aldosterone. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes need KCl and Mg2+, with potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, ACEi, or ARBs if K+ stays low. An alkali load is treated by stopping the source, with saline for milk-alkali syndrome. When saline cannot be given, as in heart failure, acetazolamide is used, and severe or refractory alkalosis is treated with dialysis against a low-bicarbonate bath or dilute HCl.

Relationships to other conditions

. Acid-base and K+ disorders travel together: acidosis usually raises K+ and alkalosis lowers it, and hypokalemia itself maintains an alkalosis by increasing H+ secretion and NH4+ production. A low K+ in an acidosis points to RTA types 1 or 2 or diarrhea, whereas a high K+ points to RTA type 4, CKD, or DKA before insulin.

. Aldosterone excess causes a hypokalemic, chloride-resistant alkalosis with hypertension, and renin separates primary from secondary causes, while too little aldosterone causes the hyperkalemic NAGMA of RTA type 4. Apparent mineralocorticoid excess resembles primary hyperaldosteronism but has low aldosterone, and Bartter and Gitelman syndromes resemble diuretic use with high renin but normal or low BP.

. Volume loss maintains a metabolic alkalosis through angiotensin II and aldosterone, and saline corrects a chloride-responsive alkalosis by restoring it. In volume overload, where saline would worsen fluid retention, acetazolamide is used instead.

. CKD causes a NAGMA early, as NH4+ production falls, and an AGMA late, as sulfate and phosphate are retained, so it appears on both sides of the anion gap; acidosis in turn speeds CKD progression, and oral NaHCO3 is used to slow it.

Gastrointestinal and endocrine disorders. Fluid lost below the stomach, as in diarrhea and pancreatic fistulas, causes a NAGMA, whereas fluid lost from the stomach, as in vomiting and gastric outlet obstruction, causes a metabolic alkalosis, and both cause hypokalemia. DKA is an AGMA with hyperkalemia despite total body K+ depletion, adrenal insufficiency gives the picture of RTA type 4, and Cushing syndrome gives a hypertensive, chloride-resistant alkalosis.
