Review article

Metabolic acidosis: the anion gap, the urine, and the acid behind it

Metabolic acidosis is a fall in plasma HCO3− from a gain of acid or a loss of HCO3−, to which the lungs respond by hyperventilating to lower pCO2. The anion gap divides it into a normal gap form (NAGMA), in which HCO3− lost from the kidney or gut is replaced by Cl−, and a high gap form (AGMA), in which an added acid leaves its anion in the blood. The urine anion gap, plasma K+, and urine pH separate the renal causes of NAGMA, and the delta-delta ratio and osmolal gap refine AGMA. Here, we review the mechanisms, causes, consequences, diagnosis, and management of metabolic acidosis, and consider its relationship to disorders of potassium, aldosterone, kidney function, and metabolic alkalosis.

Key points

  • The anion gap separates NAGMA, in which HCO3− is lost and replaced by Cl−, from AGMA, in which an acid adds an unmeasured anion; the gap, compensation, and delta-delta calculations are on the comparison and workup page.
  • A negative urine anion gap points to gut losses, and a positive one to RTA types 1 and 4 or CKD; K+ is low in RTA types 1 and 2 and high in type 4, and urine pH stays above 5.5 only in type 1.
  • The causes of AGMA follow GOLDMARK; an osmolal gap above 15 with an AGMA suggests methanol or ethylene glycol.
  • HCO3− is replaced in NAGMA but is not routine in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis, whose anions are metabolized back into HCO3− once the cause is treated.

Pathophysiology

Plasma pH is set by the balance H+ + HCO3− ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ CO2 + H2O, with the kidney regulating HCO3− and the lungs regulating CO2. In NAGMA, HCO3− is lost from the kidney or gut, or the kidney fails to make new HCO3−, and Cl− takes its place, giving a hyperchloremic acidosis with a normal anion gap. In AGMA, an added acid uses up HCO3−, and its anion (lactate, ketoanions, formate, glycolate, sulfate, or phosphate) stays in the blood and widens the gap. In both, the lungs hyperventilate to lower pCO2.

In acidosis, cells take up H+, which softens the fall in pH, and K+ leaves them to keep the charge balanced, causing hyperkalemia that is most marked with mineral acids such as HCl and NH4Cl. Organic acids such as lactate and ketoacids enter cells together with their H+, so they shift less K+. There are exceptions to the usual shift: in DKA, K+ is high mainly from insulin deficiency and high osmolality although total body K+ is low, and insulin moves K+ back into cells; in RTA types 1 and 2 and in diarrhea, K+ is low despite acidosis because it is lost in urine or stool faster than it shifts out of cells; and correcting any acidosis moves K+ back into cells and can unmask a low total body K+.

Etiology and mechanisms

NAGMA. In CKD, fewer nephrons make less NH4+, so less acid is excreted and less new HCO3− is made; the NAGMA of earlier CKD becomes an AGMA as GFR falls further. With a normal GFR, the renal causes are the RTAs. In RTA type 1 (distal), α-intercalated cells cannot secrete H+, so urine cannot be acidified; causes include autoimmune disease (Sjögren syndrome and SLE), lithium, amphotericin B, and toluene. In RTA type 2 (proximal), the proximal tubule, which normally reclaims 85–90% of filtered HCO3−, reclaims less, so HCO3− spills into urine until plasma HCO3− falls below the lowered threshold; causes include multiple myeloma, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide and topiramate), tenofovir, and Fanconi syndrome. RTA type 4, the most common RTA, results from too little aldosterone or resistance to it, which cuts H+ and K+ secretion, while the resulting high K+ suppresses NH4+ production; causes include diabetic nephropathy, ACEi, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, heparin, trimethoprim, and adrenal insufficiency. Among gut causes, intestinal fluid beyond the stomach is rich in HCO3−, so diarrhea lowers HCO3−, the kidney keeps Cl− in its place, and K+ is lost too; secretory diarrhea loses more HCO3− than inflammatory diarrhea. Pancreatic fistulas and other bicarbonate-rich losses act in the same way, as pancreatic, biliary, or small bowel fluid drains outside the body, and a ureterosigmoidostomy lets the colon swap urinary Cl− for HCO3−. A Cl− load causes NAGMA when large volumes of 0.9% saline, with 154 mmol/L Cl− and no HCO3−, are given, and TPN without acetate and cholestyramine can do the same.

AGMA. The causes follow GOLDMARK. Ethylene glycol in antifreeze is made into glycolic acid, which causes the acidosis, and oxalic acid, which forms calcium oxalate crystals in the tubules, causing AKI and hypocalcemia; propylene glycol is made into lactate. 5-oxoproline (pyroglutamic acid) builds up with chronic paracetamol use, especially with malnutrition or chronic illness. L-lactic acidosis is type A when too little oxygen is delivered, as in shock, sepsis, and ischemia, driving anaerobic glycolysis, and type B when there is no hypoxia but mitochondria cannot use pyruvate, as with metformin, liver failure, and thiamine deficiency. D-lactate forms in short bowel syndrome, when colonic bacteria ferment unabsorbed carbohydrate into an isomer that humans metabolize poorly. Methanol, found in windscreen washer fluid, solvents, and contaminated spirits, is made into formic acid, which damages the retina and basal ganglia. Salicylates first stimulate the respiratory center, causing a respiratory alkalosis, then uncouple oxidative phosphorylation, producing lactate and ketoacids. Renal failure, in advanced CKD or AKI, retains sulfate, phosphate, urate, and hippurate, and rhabdomyolysis releases acids and phosphate. In ketoacidosis, low insulin and high glucagon drive lipolysis and the production of β-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate, as in DKA, AKA after a binge followed by vomiting and poor intake, and starvation.

Clinical presentation and complications

Metabolic acidosis causes Kussmaul breathing, deep and rapid breaths that blow off CO2 and can tire the respiratory muscles. K+ is often high, but not always: organic acidoses shift less K+, and RTA types 1 and 2 and diarrhea cause hypokalemia. Ionized Ca2+ rises, and chronic acidosis draws on bone to buffer acid, causing osteomalacia, rickets, and stones. The heart contracts more weakly, vessels dilate, and the response to catecholamines is poor, causing hypotension, with arrhythmias. The oxygen dissociation curve shifts right, releasing more O2 to tissues. Patients have headache, lethargy, confusion, and coma, with nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and fatigue; acidosis causes insulin resistance, and in the long term, muscle wasting and faster CKD progression.

Some findings point to the cause. Hypotension, tachycardia, and dry mucous membranes suggest volume loss from diarrhea or adrenal insufficiency. Fruity breath, polyuria, and polydipsia suggest DKA; blurred vision after drinking, methanol; inebriation, flank pain, hematuria, and tetany, ethylene glycol; and tinnitus with fast breathing, aspirin. Calcium phosphate stones and nephrocalcinosis suggest RTA type 1, with alkaline urine, high urine Ca2+, and low urine citrate, and bone pain and osteomalacia with glycosuria suggest RTA type 2 with Fanconi syndrome. Pruritus, nocturia, oliguria, and asterixis suggest renal failure.

Diagnosis

On an ABG or VBG, metabolic acidosis is a pH below 7.35 with a low HCO3−, which moves the same way as the pH. The expected compensation, the anion gap and its correction for low albumin, the delta-delta ratio, and the osmolal gap are calculated as set out in the review of ; a high osmolal gap with an AGMA suggests methanol or ethylene glycol, and the anion gap sorts the causes below.

In NAGMA, the urine anion gap (urine Na+ + urine K+ − urine Cl−) separates kidney from gut. A negative gap means the kidney is excreting plenty of NH4+, which leaves with Cl−, a normal renal response to HCO3− lost elsewhere, as in diarrhea, fistulas, or saline. A positive gap means the kidney cannot excrete NH4+, as in RTA types 1 and 4 or CKD. RTA type 1 has a normal GFR, low K+, a urine pH above 5.5 even in severe acidosis, plasma HCO3− that can fall below 10 mmol/L, and a positive urine anion gap, with high urine Ca2+, low urine citrate, calcium phosphate stones, and nephrocalcinosis. RTA type 2 has a normal GFR, low K+, a urine pH below 5.5 once HCO3− is below the threshold but above 5.5 when HCO3− is given, plasma HCO3− usually 12–18 mmol/L, and a variable urine anion gap, with Fanconi syndrome (glycosuria with normal blood glucose, phosphaturia, aminoaciduria, and low urate) and bone disease. RTA type 4 has a normal or mildly low GFR, high K+, a urine pH below 5.5, plasma HCO3− usually above 17 mmol/L, and a positive urine anion gap, with diabetes or culprit drugs and high urine Na+ despite a low-salt diet. CKD has a low GFR, normal or high K+, a urine pH usually below 5.5, HCO3− that falls as GFR falls, a positive urine anion gap, and high urea and creatinine. Before diagnosing an RTA, a UTI with a urease-producing organism, which raises urine pH, and low volume, which limits distal H+ secretion, are excluded; urine Na+ should be above 25 mmol/L.

Each AGMA cause has its own findings. Glycols give a high osmolal gap early, low Ca2+, rising creatinine, calcium oxalate crystals, and hematuria, after inebriation followed by flank pain and oliguria. Oxoproline gives normal lactate, no ketones, and 5-oxoproline in urine, with chronic paracetamol use and malnutrition. L-lactate is above 4 mmol/L in shock, sepsis, ischemia, metformin use, and liver failure, and D-lactate is high in short bowel syndrome. Methanol gives a high osmolal gap early, with blurred vision, dilated pupils, and papilledema. Aspirin gives a respiratory alkalosis with an AGMA and a measurable salicylate level, with tinnitus, nausea, and fast breathing. Renal failure gives high urea, creatinine, and phosphate, with reduced urine output. DKA gives high glucose and β-hydroxybutyrate, with K+ normal or high, and ketones and glucose in urine, after missed insulin, infection, or new diabetes. AKA and starvation give low or normal glucose with high β-hydroxybutyrate, and the urine dipstick, which detects acetoacetate, can show few ketones.

Management

Treatment is aimed at the cause, with volume and K+ replaced as needed. HCO3− is given when it has truly been lost, as oral alkali in RTA and CKD, but not routinely in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis, for the reasons set out in the review of .

In CKD, oral NaHCO3 protects bone and muscle and slows progression, and dialysis is needed when the disease is advanced. RTA type 1 responds to oral alkali in modest doses, with potassium citrate also replacing K+ and preventing stones, alongside treatment of the cause. RTA type 2 needs large doses of alkali with K+, because HCO3− given above the threshold is lost in urine and carries K+ with it; a thiazide can help by shrinking volume, and phosphate and vitamin D treat Fanconi bone disease. RTA type 4 is managed by stopping culprit drugs, a low-potassium diet, and a loop or thiazide diuretic, with fludrocortisone for aldosterone deficiency without hypertension or edema. Diarrhea and fistulas are managed by treating the cause, replacing fluid, K+, and HCO3−, and controlling the fistula, and saline-induced acidosis by switching to a balanced crystalloid such as Hartmann solution or Plasma-Lyte. Toxic alcohols are treated with fomepizole, or ethanol if it is unavailable, to block alcohol dehydrogenase, with hemodialysis for severe acidosis, visual change, AKI, or instability, and thiamine and pyridoxine for ethylene glycol. Oxoproline acidosis is treated by stopping paracetamol. Type A lactic acidosis is treated by restoring perfusion with fluids, vasopressors, ABX, and source control, and type B with thiamine and by stopping metformin, with hemodialysis if severe. D-lactic acidosis is treated by addressing the short bowel cause and limiting carbohydrate reaching the colon. Aspirin poisoning is treated with activated charcoal, IV NaHCO3 to alkalinize the urine and trap salicylate there, and hemodialysis if severe. Renal failure is treated with dialysis. DKA is treated with IV fluids, then insulin once K+ is at least 3.5 mmol/L, with K+ replacement, continuing insulin until the gap closes, and AKA and starvation with IV fluids with dextrose and thiamine and gradual feeding, watching for refeeding syndrome.

Relationships to other conditions

and . Acidosis usually raises K+, but the K+ in an acidosis points to its cause: a low K+ points to RTA types 1 or 2 or diarrhea, whereas a high K+ points to RTA type 4, CKD, or DKA before insulin. Correcting the acidosis moves K+ into cells, so K+ is replaced before or with alkali.

. Too little aldosterone, or resistance to it, causes the hyperkalemic NAGMA of RTA type 4, as in diabetic nephropathy, with ACEi, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim, and heparin, and in adrenal insufficiency.

. CKD causes a NAGMA early, as NH4+ production falls, and an AGMA late, as sulfate and phosphate are retained, so it appears on both sides of the anion gap; acidosis in turn speeds CKD progression, and oral NaHCO3 is used to slow it.

. Fluid lost below the stomach, as in diarrhea and pancreatic fistulas, causes a NAGMA, whereas fluid lost from the stomach causes a metabolic alkalosis. The two can coexist, as in DKA with vomiting, which the delta-delta ratio reveals as a value above 2.

Why metabolic alkalosis lasts

Healthy kidneys excrete extra HCO3− quickly, so an alkalosis lasts only while something keeps the kidney reabsorbing it.

Metabolic alkalosis: generation, maintenance, and the urine chloride

Metabolic alkalosis is a rise in plasma HCO3− from a loss of H+ or a gain of HCO3−, to which the lungs respond by hypoventilating to raise pCO2. Because healthy kidneys excrete extra HCO3− quickly, an alkalosis persists only while low volume, Cl−, or K+, or a mineralocorticoid effect, keeps the kidney reabsorbing it. Urine Cl− divides alkalosis into chloride-responsive forms, which correct with saline, and chloride-resistant forms, which BP, renin, and aldosterone then separate. Here, we review the mechanisms, causes, consequences, diagnosis, and management of metabolic alkalosis, and consider its relationship to disorders of potassium, aldosterone, volume, and metabolic acidosis.

Key points

  • Vomiting and NG tube suction remove HCl and generate an alkalosis; low volume, Cl−, and K+ maintain it.
  • Urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive alkalosis that corrects with saline and KCl; above 20, BP, renin, and aldosterone separate the causes.
  • Alkalosis moves K+ into cells and makes albumin bind more Ca2+, so hypokalemia and tetany with a normal total calcium are typical.
  • Acetazolamide treats an alkalosis when saline cannot be given, as in heart failure.

Pathophysiology

Plasma pH is set by the balance H+ + HCO3− ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ CO2 + H2O, with the kidney regulating HCO3− and the lungs regulating CO2. In metabolic alkalosis, H+ is lost from the stomach or kidney, or HCO3− is gained, and Cl− often falls too; the lungs hypoventilate to raise pCO2. In a chloride-responsive alkalosis, H+ and Cl− are lost from the stomach or by a diuretic that has worn off, and low volume, Cl−, and K+ keep it going; in a chloride-resistant alkalosis, the kidney loses H+ and K+ because aldosterone, or something acting like it, is high, or a diuretic is still acting.

Alkalosis moves H+ out of cells and K+ into them, causing hypokalemia that urinary K+ loss makes worse, and makes albumin give up H+ and bind more Ca2+, so ionized Ca2+ falls while total Ca2+ is unchanged.

Healthy kidneys excrete extra HCO3− quickly, so a metabolic alkalosis lasts only while something keeps the kidney reabsorbing it. After the alkalosis is generated, low volume raises angiotensin II, which increases proximal HCO3− reabsorption, and aldosterone, which increases distal H+ and K+ secretion. Low Cl− leaves too little Cl− in the collecting duct to exchange for HCO3−, so HCO3− cannot be secreted, and low K+ increases H+ secretion and NH4+ production, which makes new HCO3−. The alkalosis lasts until volume, Cl−, and K+ are replaced.

Etiology and mechanisms

Vomiting and NG tube suction remove HCl, leaving the HCO3− made by parietal cells in the blood, and the volume and Cl− loss then keeps the kidney reabsorbing HCO3−; chloride-losing diarrhea (villous adenoma and congenital chloridorrhea) and the Cl− lost in the sweat of cystic fibrosis act similarly. Loop and thiazide diuretics deliver more Na+ to the collecting duct, where aldosterone, raised by the volume loss, exchanges it for H+ and K+, and the extracellular fluid shrinks around the same amount of HCO3−, a contraction alkalosis. In primary hyperaldosteronism, an adrenal adenoma (Conn syndrome) or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia makes aldosterone regardless of renin, causing Na+ retention, hypertension, and H+ and K+ loss. In secondary hyperaldosteronism, high renin drives aldosterone, as in renal artery stenosis and renin-secreting tumors, and low effective volume in heart failure and cirrhosis does the same and keeps other alkaloses going. In apparent mineralocorticoid excess, the collecting duct behaves as if aldosterone were high: in Cushing syndrome, cortisol acts on the mineralocorticoid receptor; licorice blocks 11β-HSD2, which normally inactivates cortisol; and in Liddle syndrome, ENaC is overactive. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes are inherited losses of NKCC2 in the loop, like a lifelong loop diuretic, and of NCC in the distal tubule, like a lifelong thiazide. An alkali load, from calcium carbonate in milk-alkali syndrome, HCO3− tablets or infusions, citrate from massive transfusion, or acetate in TPN, lasts mainly when the kidney cannot excrete HCO3−, as in CKD or low volume. Finally, in chronic respiratory acidosis from COPD, the kidney retains HCO3−, and when ventilation lowers pCO2 quickly, the extra HCO3− is left behind.

Clinical presentation and complications

Metabolic alkalosis causes shallow, slow breathing that is usually mild but can worsen hypercapnia and hypoxemia in COPD or obesity hypoventilation. Hypokalemia causes muscle weakness and arrhythmias. Ionized Ca2+ falls, causing perioral numbness, paresthesias, carpopedal spasm, Chvostek and Trousseau signs, and tetany. Arrhythmias come from low K+ and low Ca2+, and vessels constrict, including in the brain. The oxygen dissociation curve shifts left, releasing less O2 to tissues, and patients may have confusion, delirium, seizures, muscle cramps, and weakness.

Some findings point to the cause. Hypotension, tachycardia, and dry mucous membranes suggest volume loss from vomiting or diuretics, and hypertension with low K+ suggests aldosterone excess or its mimics. Failure to thrive, polyuria, and vomiting in a child suggest Bartter or Gitelman syndrome, and recent ventilation for COPD suggests an alkalosis left behind after hypercapnia.

Diagnosis

On an ABG or VBG, metabolic alkalosis is a pH above 7.45 with a high HCO3−, which moves the same way as the pH, and the compensation is checked as set out in the review of ; the urine Cl− then sorts the causes.

In metabolic alkalosis, urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive alkalosis, in which the kidney holds on to Cl− because volume and Cl− are low: vomiting and NG tube suction, diuretics that have worn off, chloride-losing diarrhea, cystic fibrosis, and the period after hypercapnia. Urine Cl− above 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-resistant alkalosis: diuretics still acting, aldosterone excess and its mimics, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, and severe hypokalemia. Urine Cl− is high while a diuretic acts and low once it wears off, so a changing result suggests hidden diuretic use. Vomiting and diuretics give low or normal BP with high renin and aldosterone and K+ lost mostly in urine. Primary hyperaldosteronism gives high BP, low renin, high aldosterone, and a high aldosterone-to-renin ratio; secondary hyperaldosteronism gives high BP with both high; and apparent mineralocorticoid excess gives high BP with both low. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes give low or normal BP with high renin and aldosterone, with high urine Ca2+ in Bartter syndrome and low urine Ca2+ with low Mg2+ in Gitelman syndrome. An alkali load gives variable values, with high Ca2+ in milk-alkali syndrome.

Management

Chloride-responsive alkalosis, from vomiting, NG tube suction, or after hypercapnia, is treated with 0.9% saline with KCl, which restores volume and Cl− so the kidney can excrete HCO3−, and the cause of vomiting is treated. Diuretic-induced alkalosis is managed by stopping or reducing the diuretic, replacing KCl, or adding a potassium-sparing diuretic, with acetazolamide if the patient is fluid overloaded. Primary hyperaldosteronism is treated with spironolactone or eplerenone, or adrenalectomy for a unilateral adenoma, and secondary hyperaldosteronism by treating its cause, such as renal artery stenosis or a renin-secreting tumor. In apparent mineralocorticoid excess, Cushing syndrome is treated, licorice is stopped, and Liddle syndrome is treated with amiloride, since its ENaC does not need aldosterone. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes need KCl and Mg2+, with potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, ACEi, or ARBs if K+ stays low. An alkali load is treated by stopping the source, with saline for milk-alkali syndrome. When saline cannot be given, as in heart failure, acetazolamide is used, and severe or refractory alkalosis is treated with dialysis against a low-bicarbonate bath or dilute HCl.

Metabolic acid-base disorders: a shared approach to the blood gas, compensation, and the sorting tests

Metabolic acidosis and metabolic alkalosis are mirror images in which a primary fall or rise in plasma HCO3− moves the pH in the same direction and the lungs compensate by changing pCO2. A single approach serves both: the pH and HCO3− identify a metabolic disorder, an expected pCO2 tests whether a respiratory disorder coexists, and one further test sorts the causes, the anion gap in acidosis and the urine Cl− in alkalosis. Here, we compare the two disorders, describe the stepwise workup and its calculations, and set out the principles of correction, including when HCO3− or acid should be given.

Key points

  • A metabolic disorder is present when HCO3− moves in the same direction as the pH.
  • Expected pCO2 is 1.5 × HCO3− + 8 (± 2) in acidosis (Winter formula) and 0.7 × HCO3− + 20 (± 5) in alkalosis; a pCO2 outside this range means a respiratory disorder as well.
  • The anion gap, (Na+ + K+) − (Cl− + HCO3−), is normally 12–16 mmol/L and falls by about 2.5 for each 10 g/L fall in albumin.
  • A delta-delta ratio below 1 adds a NAGMA, and above 2 a metabolic alkalosis; a high osmolal gap with an AGMA suggests a toxic alcohol.
  • Urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive alkalosis, corrected by saline with KCl.
  • HCO3− is given when it has truly been lost, as in RTA and CKD, but not routinely in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis.

Comparison

A metabolic acidosis follows a gain of acid or a loss of HCO3−, and the lungs hyperventilate to lower pCO2; a metabolic alkalosis follows a loss of H+ or a gain of HCO3−, and the lungs hypoventilate to raise pCO2, rarely above 55 mmHg. The disorders have opposite effects beyond the pH: acidosis moves K+ out of cells, raises ionized Ca2+, and shifts the O2 dissociation curve to the right, whereas alkalosis moves K+ into cells, lowers ionized Ca2+ as albumin binds more of it, and shifts the curve to the left. Acidosis causes Kussmaul breathing, and alkalosis shallow, slow breathing. Plasma K+ in acidosis is often high, but less so in organic acidoses, and low in RTA types 1 and 2 and diarrhea.

Diagnosis

An ABG or VBG first shows whether the pH is low or high, and a HCO3− that has moved the same way identifies a metabolic disorder. The expected pCO2 is then calculated, by the Winter formula, 1.5 × HCO3− + 8 (± 2), in acidosis, or 0.7 × HCO3− + 20 (± 5) in alkalosis; a higher pCO2 than expected means a respiratory acidosis as well, and a lower one a respiratory alkalosis, as in aspirin poisoning.

In acidosis, the anion gap, (Na+ + K+) − (Cl− + HCO3−), normally 12–16 mmol/L or 8–12 mmol/L without K+, separates NAGMA from AGMA. The gap is mostly albumin, so each 10 g/L fall in albumin lowers it by about 2.5 and can hide an AGMA. In an AGMA, the delta-delta ratio, (AG − 16) ÷ (24 − HCO3−), is 1–2 when the acidosis is pure, below 1 when a NAGMA coexists, and above 2 when a metabolic alkalosis coexists, as in DKA with vomiting. An osmolal gap, measured osmolality − (2 × Na+ + glucose + urea), above 15 with an AGMA suggests methanol or ethylene glycol; it can be high without acidosis early after ingestion or with isopropyl alcohol, and it closes as the alcohol is metabolized, so a normal value late does not exclude poisoning. In a NAGMA, a negative urine AG shows a normal renal response to HCO3− lost from the gut or to saline, and a positive one points to RTA or CKD. In alkalosis, urine Cl− below 20 mmol/L marks a chloride-responsive disorder and above 20 mmol/L a chloride-resistant one, which BP, renin, and aldosterone then separate.

Management

Both disorders are corrected by treating the cause and replacing volume and K+. HCO3− is given when it has truly been lost, as oral alkali in RTA and CKD, but not routinely in lactic acidosis or ketoacidosis, whose anions are metabolized back into HCO3− once the cause is treated, so that added HCO3− can overshoot into alkalosis; its risks are hypernatremia, fluid overload, low ionized Ca2+, and hypokalemia. A chloride-responsive alkalosis is corrected by 0.9% saline with KCl, which lets the kidney excrete the extra HCO3−; acetazolamide is used when saline cannot be given, and dialysis or dilute HCl for severe or refractory alkalosis.

Hypovolemia

Pathophysiology

Losing salt and water from the ECF faster than they are replaced shrinks the circulating volume and lowers tissue perfusion, until the sympathetic system, RAAS, and ADH make the kidney retain Na+ and water to restore it.

How the loss changes the compartments

Where the fluid comes from depends on what is lost; the compartments themselves are described on Fluids.

  • Isotonic lossBleeding, vomiting, and diarrhea remove fluid with about as much Na+ as plasma, so only the ECF shrinks and plasma Na+ is unchanged
  • More water than Na+Sweat and osmotic diuresis concentrate the ECF, which draws water out of cells, so both compartments shrink and plasma Na+ rises
  • Third spacingFluid leaves plasma for the interstitium or a body cavity; total body water is unchanged, but the circulating volume falls

Note: Hypovolemia is a loss of salt and water, whereas dehydration is a loss of water alone, which raises plasma Na+ (see Hypernatremia).

Hypovolemia: where the fluid goes, how the body compensates, and how to replace it

Hypovolemia is a loss of salt and water from the ECF that lowers the circulating volume and tissue perfusion. The sympathetic system, RAAS, and ADH respond by raising heart rate, constricting vessels, and making the kidney retain Na+ and water, which explains its tachycardia, poor skin perfusion, and concentrated, Na+-poor urine. It arises from renal loss, extra-renal loss, and third spacing, and urine Na+ separates the kidney from the other sources. Here, we review the mechanisms, causes, presentation, assessment, and treatment of hypovolemia, including the choice of IV fluid, and consider its relationship to kidney injury and to disorders of sodium, potassium, acid-base balance, and aldosterone.

Key points

  • Isotonic losses shrink only the ECF; losses with more water than Na+ also draw water from cells and raise Na+; third spacing moves fluid out of plasma without changing total body water.
  • Hypovolemia comes from renal loss (diuretics, osmotic diuresis, aldosterone deficiency, cerebral salt wasting, and salt-wasting nephropathy), the big 5 extra-renal losses (vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, burns, and bleeding), and third spacing through cytokine-driven capillary leak.
  • Tachycardia from the baroreceptor reflex, poor skin perfusion, and a rising lactate from anaerobic glycolysis mark hypovolemia, and prerenal AKI gives a BUN:creatinine ratio above 20, urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L, and FENa below 1%.
  • Urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L points to extra-renal loss or third spacing, and above 20 mmol/L to renal loss.
  • Isotonic crystalloid restores volume, leaving about 250 mL of each liter in plasma; bleeding needs blood products, and half-normal saline and D5W are not used for resuscitation.

Pathophysiology

Hypovolemia is a loss of salt and water from the ECF faster than it is replaced, so total body Na+ is low. Because Na+ content sets the ECF volume and water balance sets the Na+ concentration, hypovolemia differs from dehydration, a loss of water alone that raises plasma Na+. Isotonic losses such as bleeding, vomiting, and diarrhea shrink only the ECF and leave plasma Na+ unchanged. Losses with more water than Na+, such as sweat and osmotic diuresis, concentrate the ECF, which draws water out of cells, so both compartments shrink and Na+ rises. In third spacing, fluid moves from plasma into the interstitium or a body cavity, leaving total body water unchanged but the circulating volume reduced.

A fall in volume reduces stretch on baroreceptors, the atria, and the afferent arteriole. The sympathetic system raises heart rate and constricts vessels, angiotensin II and aldosterone make the kidney retain Na+, and ADH retains water, so the urine becomes concentrated with Na+ below 20 mmol/L until volume is restored.

Etiology and mechanisms

Renal loss. Loop diuretics block NKCC2 and thiazides block NCC, so Na+ and water are lost in urine, particularly with excessive doses or continued use during poor intake. In osmotic diuresis, glucose in DKA and HHS, mannitol, and urea hold water in the tubule, and Na+ is lost with it, though more water than Na+ is lost. In adrenal insufficiency and hypoaldosteronism, the collecting duct cannot reclaim Na+, so Na+ is lost while K+ is retained, with salt craving, and skin pigmentation in primary adrenal insufficiency. In cerebral salt wasting, after SAH or other brain injury, the kidney wastes Na+, possibly through BNP or reduced sympathetic drive to the kidney rather than through low aldosterone; unlike SIADH, the patient is hypovolemic. Salt-wasting nephropathy includes kidney disease that cannot reclaim Na+ and Bartter and Gitelman syndromes, which behave like lifelong loop and thiazide diuretics.

Extra-renal loss. The big 5 are vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, burns, and bleeding. Vomiting and NG tube suction remove gastric fluid, causing metabolic alkalosis and hypokalemia. The gut secretes 3–6 L a day and normally reabsorbs it, so diarrhea, high-output stomas, and fistulas lose large volumes, causing a NAGMA and hypokalemia. Exercise in heat can lose 1–2 L of sweat an hour, with less Na+ than plasma. Burns lose fluid from the injured surface, and histamine and free radicals make capillaries leak, so burns also cause third spacing. Bleeding, most often from trauma and then from GI bleeding, surgery, ruptured aneurysm, and postpartum hemorrhage, loses whole blood, red cells with the plasma.

Third spacing. In pancreatitis, IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α make capillaries leak, and fluid moves into the interstitium and retroperitoneum. In peritonitis, the inflamed peritoneum leaks fluid into the peritoneal cavity and bowel wall. In sepsis, cytokines released throughout the body make capillaries leak everywhere, while vasodilation adds a distributive shock. Fluid also pools in obstructed bowel and in inflamed tissue after surgery.

Clinical presentation and complications

Early hypovolemia causes thirst, fatigue, muscle cramps, postural dizziness, and reduced urine output while BP is maintained. Tachycardia, often the first sign, reflects the baroreceptor reflex. Hypotension appears first on standing and then lying down, once about 20–25% of the effective arterial volume is lost, and it is a late sign in children and young adults. Sympathetic vasoconstriction produces cool, pale, clammy, or mottled skin with capillary refill above 2 seconds, alongside dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, and a low JVP. When too little oxygen reaches tissues, anaerobic glycolysis converts pyruvate to lactate, producing a type A lactic acidosis with fast breathing; lactate is used to judge severity and response. Hypoperfusion of the brain, gut, heart, and kidneys causes confusion, abdominal pain, chest pain, and oliguria. Low renal perfusion lowers GFR, causing prerenal AKI: BUN and creatinine rise, and because the intact tubules reclaim Na+, water, and with them urea, the BUN:creatinine ratio exceeds 20, urine Na+ is below 20 mmol/L, and FENa is below 1%; prolonged ischemia leads to ATN. Hypovolemic shock progresses from a compensated stage at about 10% loss to shock at 20–25% and then irreversible organ damage.

The accompanying laboratory findings point to the cause. A metabolic alkalosis with low K+ suggests vomiting or diuretics; a NAGMA with low K+, diarrhea, stomas, or fistulas; high K+ and low Na+ with salt craving, aldosterone deficiency; high glucose with polyuria, osmotic diuresis in DKA or HHS; low Na+ with high urine Na+ after SAH, cerebral salt wasting; a falling hemoglobin, bleeding; and edema or ascites with low BP, third spacing or low albumin.

Diagnosis

Volume status is assessed by combining the history, vital signs including orthostatic BP, examination, weight trend, and fluid balance, since no single sign is reliable; the bedside signs and the shared tests, including bloods, FENa, and tests of fluid responsiveness, are set out in the review of .

Bleeding is sought first, with hemoglobin, coagulation, and crossmatch, and its source is found and controlled. Otherwise, urine Na+ separates the causes: below 20 mmol/L, the kidney is retaining Na+ appropriately, pointing to extra-renal loss or third spacing, whereas above 20 mmol/L, the kidney is the source, pointing to diuretics, kidney disease, aldosterone deficiency, or cerebral salt wasting; urine Na+ is also high in metabolic alkalosis, where Na+ leaves with HCO3−.

Management

Treatment restores volume while addressing the cause and watching for fluid overload. Bleeding is treated with source control and blood products, plasma, platelets, and red cells in a 1:1:1 ratio, keeping crystalloid to a minimum. Other causes are treated with boluses of isotonic crystalloid, reassessing after each, with care in HF, CKD, and cirrhosis. The cause is treated by stopping diuretics, giving antiemetics, insulin for DKA, and ABX and source control for sepsis. Vasopressors are used only when hypotension persists despite adequate fluid, and once the patient is stable, maintenance fluid covers ongoing losses, or fluid is stopped.

Isotonic crystalloid is used for resuscitation and hypotonic fluids are not; the composition, distribution, uses, and risks of NS, LR, albumin, ½NS, and D5W are compared in the review of . KCl is added when K+ is low or about to fall, as in vomiting, diarrhea, diuretic use, and DKA once insulin starts; HCO3− replaces losses in diarrhea and fistulas with a NAGMA; and dextrose is added to NS or ½NS when glucose falls in DKA so insulin can continue until the ketoacidosis clears. Monitoring covers heart rate, BP, urine output, mental state, and lactate; crackles, edema, and JVP for overload; daily weight and fluid balance; and Na+, K+, Cl−, glucose, and creatinine, raising a low Na+ by no more than 8 mmol/L in 24 hours.

Hypervolemia: underfilled arteries, a failing filter, and the stepwise removal of fluid

Hypervolemia is a gain of salt and water that expands the ECF, most of which crosses into the interstitium as edema. The kidney retains salt and water either because the arteries seem underfilled, as in HF and cirrhosis, where RAAS, the sympathetic system, and ADH drive retention despite excess total fluid, or because a low GFR in CKD and AKI leaves too little Na+ filtered to excrete intake; fluid given to patients who cannot excrete it adds to both. Here, we review the mechanisms, causes, presentation, assessment, and treatment of hypervolemia, including the stepwise use of diuretics and dialysis, and consider its relationship to kidney injury and to disorders of sodium, acid-base balance, and aldosterone.

Key points

  • In HF and cirrhosis, the arteries are underfilled despite excess total fluid, so RAAS, the sympathetic system, and ADH keep the kidney retaining Na+ and water; natriuretic peptides cannot overcome them.
  • In CKD and AKI, a low GFR leaves too little Na+ filtered to excrete intake.
  • Of each liter of isotonic fluid retained or infused, about 250 mL stays in plasma and the rest enters the interstitium, so most of it becomes edema.
  • Pulmonary edema, effusions, JVD, ascites, pitting edema, dilutional hyponatremia, and cardiorenal or hepatorenal syndrome follow, depending on the cause.
  • Treatment is Na+ restriction and a loop diuretic, adding a thiazide, potassium-sparing diuretic, or acetazolamide, except in cirrhosis, where spironolactone comes first, and dialysis by the AEIOU indications when overload is refractory.

Pathophysiology

Hypervolemia is a retention or administration of salt and water faster than it is excreted, so total body Na+ is high. Retained or infused isotonic fluid spreads through the ECF in a ratio of about three parts interstitium to one part plasma, so most of it becomes edema. High capillary pressure from the expanded ECF and raised venous pressure, low albumin, and lymph production exceeding reabsorption all favor its accumulation in the tissues.

The kidney retains salt and water for one of two reasons. The arterial sensors respond to the effective arterial blood volume rather than total volume: in HF, from low output, and in cirrhosis, from splanchnic vasodilation, the arteries are underfilled despite excess fluid, so RAAS, the sympathetic system, and ADH drive continued retention. Stretched atria and ventricles release ANP and BNP, which inhibit Na+ reabsorption and renin release, but in HF they cannot overcome RAAS. In CKD and AKI, by contrast, too little Na+ is filtered to excrete the daily intake, whatever the arteries sense.

Etiology and mechanisms

In HF, low cardiac output underfills the arteries; baroreceptors and the afferent arteriole sense less stretch, RAAS, the sympathetic system, and ADH make the kidney retain Na+ and water, and the raised venous pressure produces pulmonary and peripheral edema while output stays low, so the cycle continues. In cirrhosis, scarring raises resistance to portal flow, nitric oxide dilates the splanchnic arteries, and the underfilled systemic arteries trigger the same retention, with ADH also lowering Na+; high splanchnic capillary pressure, low albumin, and lymph production exceeding reabsorption send the fluid into the peritoneum as ascites. In CKD of G3 or worse, too few nephrons filter too little Na+ to excrete the daily intake, RAAS activity adds to the retention, and the expanded ECF causes hypertension and edema, most evident in G4 and G5. In AKI, whether prerenal, intrinsic, or postrenal, a sudden fall in GFR, especially in the oliguric phase of ATN, leaves intake and infused fluid in the body. Among primary causes of renal Na+ retention, nephrotic syndrome lowers albumin and may make the tubule retain Na+ directly, nephritic syndrome causes edema, hypertension, and low urine output, and hyperaldosteronism retains Na+ independently of renin and presents mainly as resistant hypertension with hypokalemia. Finally, IV fluid given faster than it can be excreted causes overload, especially in HF, CKD, cirrhosis, low albumin, and oliguric AKI.

Clinical presentation and complications

Hypervolemia causes pulmonary edema, with dyspnea, orthopnea, crackles, hypoxia, and pink frothy sputum, from raised pulmonary capillary pressure in left-sided HF, AKI, CKD, or after IV fluid, with an S3 in HF. HF also causes pleural effusions. JVD, with a JVP above 3 cm over the sternal angle and hepatojugular reflux, reflects right-sided or biventricular HF or overload from CKD or AKI. Ascites, with distension, shifting dullness, and a fluid thrill, is mostly a complication of cirrhosis but also occurs in HF and nephrotic syndrome. Pitting edema collects in the ankles and shins, or the sacrum in bedbound patients, and in the face and eyelids in nephrotic syndrome. Weight gain and hypertension accompany CKD, AKI, glomerular disease, and hyperaldosteronism, and dilutional hyponatremia occurs in HF and cirrhosis, where ADH retains water. Prerenal AKI that fluid does not correct takes two forms: cardiorenal syndrome in HF, where low output and high venous pressure lower GFR, and hepatorenal syndrome in cirrhosis, where the afferent arterioles constrict severely, a process sepsis and NSAIDs can trigger.

Diagnosis

Volume status is assessed by combining the history, vital signs, examination, weight trend, and fluid balance, as set out in the review of , which also explains why urine Na+ is low in HF, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome despite overload.

Examination, oxygen saturation, CXR, and lung ultrasound show whether fluid is in the lungs, and the next step is to find the retaining organ. BNP, released by stretched ventricles, makes HF unlikely below 100 pg/mL and likely above 500 pg/mL, and echo shows heart function; LFTs, albumin, ultrasound, and an ascitic tap assess cirrhosis; and creatinine with its trend, urinalysis, and urine protein assess CKD, AKI, and glomerular disease. Hypervolemia is treated with Na+ restriction to about 2 g a day, treatment of the cause, and diuretics: a loop diuretic first, which blocks NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb, then a thiazide such as metolazone for sequential nephron blockade, a potassium-sparing diuretic to add natriuresis and offset K+ loss, and acetazolamide to add diuresis and correct a diuretic alkalosis. Cirrhosis is the exception to starting with a loop diuretic: spironolactone comes first, because aldosterone drives the retention, with furosemide added in a 100:40 mg ratio, alongside Na+ restriction and abstinence from alcohol; tense ascites is drained by large-volume paracentesis with albumin, and fluid is restricted only when Na+ is low. Hypovolemia and hypervolemia are a loss and a gain of salt and water in the ECF, and plasma Na+ can remain normal in both because Na+ moves with water. Neither has a single reliable sign, so volume status is judged by combining the history, vital signs, examination, weight, and fluid balance, and tests then find the cause: bleeding and the urine Na+ in hypovolemia, and the lungs and the retaining organ in hypervolemia. Key points

  • No single sign is reliable; in older adults sudden weight loss is the most specific sign of hypovolemia, and in children hypotension appears late.
  • Urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L means the kidney is retaining Na+: after extra-renal loss in hypovolemia, and in HF, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome despite overload.
  • FENa below 1% suggests prerenal AKI, but diuretics raise it, so FEUrea below 35% is used instead.
  • A passive leg raise with echo is the most accurate test of fluid responsiveness.
  • About 250 mL of each litre of NS or LR stays in plasma, and only about 1/12 of D5W, so hypotonic fluids are not used for resuscitation.

Comparison

Hypovolemia presents with thirst, postural dizziness, tachycardia, orthostatic and then supine hypotension, cool skin with slow capillary refill and dry mucous membranes, a low JVP, falling weight, and little concentrated urine. Hypervolemia presents with dyspnea, orthopnea, pitting edema, crackles, effusions, ascites, a raised JVP in HF and kidney overload, and rising weight; BP is often normal, raised in kidney disease, and low in advanced HF or cirrhosis. Diagnosis

In hypovolemia, bleeding is sought first with hemoglobin, coagulation, and crossmatch. Otherwise, a urine Na+ below 20 mmol/L shows extra-renal loss or third spacing, and above 20 mmol/L a renal loss. Hyponatremia: water, ADH, and the pace of correction

Hyponatremia is an excess of water relative to Na+. Because Na+ cannot move without water but water can move without Na+, a low plasma Na+ almost always reflects water retained by ADH, taken in faster than the kidney can excrete it, or left behind when the kidney cannot dilute urine, while total body Na+ may be low, normal, or high. Serum osmolality first separates true, hypotonic hyponatremia from hypertonic hyponatremia and pseudohyponatremia; urine osmolality then shows whether ADH is acting, and volume status with urine Na+ locates the cause. Here, we review the renal handling of water, the causes of hypovolemic, euvolemic, and hypervolemic hyponatremia, its effects on the brain, its diagnosis, and its treatment, in which the speed of correction matters as much as its direction, and consider its relationship to disorders of volume, ADH, aldosterone, and the thyroid and adrenal glands.

Key points

  • Water is retained when ADH is released for low effective volume, when it is released inappropriately, when intake exceeds what the kidney can excrete, or when the kidney cannot dilute or excrete urine.
  • Serum osmolality comes first: high in hyperglycemia or after mannitol, normal in pseudohyponatremia, and low in true hyponatremia.
  • Urine osmolality below 100 mOsm/kg means ADH is suppressed, as in primary polydipsia and low solute intake; above 100, urine Na+ below 30 mmol/L means the kidney senses low effective volume, and above 30 points to diuretics, mineralocorticoid deficiency, cerebral salt wasting, SIADH, hypothyroidism, secondary adrenal insufficiency, or kidney failure.
  • SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion that requires hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency to be ruled out.
  • Acute hyponatremia causes cerebral edema and herniation; chronic hyponatremia causes few symptoms because the brain adapts, which is why correcting it faster than 6–8 mmol/L in 24 hours risks osmotic demyelination.
  • Severe symptoms are treated with boluses of 3% saline to raise Na+ by 4–6 mmol/L; otherwise, NS treats hypovolemic forms, fluid restriction, urea, salt tablets with a loop diuretic, or vaptans treat SIADH, and water and salt restriction with loop diuretics treat hypervolemic forms.

Pathophysiology

When Na+ moves with water, the volume of the ECF changes but its Na+ concentration does not, as in hypovolemia and hypervolemia; when water moves alone, the concentration changes, falling when water is gained. Hyponatremia is therefore an excess of water relative to Na+, and total body Na+ can be low, normal, or high, giving its hypovolemic, euvolemic, and hypervolemic forms.

The proximal tubule and descending limb reabsorb about 70% and 20% of filtered water along with or after solute, so they cannot make free water. The thick ascending limb, through NKCC2, and the distal tubule, through NCC, remove Na+ without water and make dilute fluid, and NKCC2 also builds the medullary gradient that later concentrates urine. ADH, released when plasma is concentrated or when effective volume falls even though plasma is dilute, inserts aquaporin-2 so the collecting duct reabsorbs water down that gradient. Because urine can be diluted only to about 50 mOsm/kg, the water excreted is limited by the solute excreted, mostly urea and salt, of which a normal diet provides 600–900 mOsm a day.

Water is retained in four ways. ADH released appropriately for low effective volume, with urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg, causes hyponatremia in hypovolemia and in HF and cirrhosis, where RAAS retains salt and water together and ADH adds the extra water. ADH released without a volume or osmotic trigger, also with urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg, causes SIADH, and hypothyroidism and cortisol deficiency behave the same way. When intake exceeds excretion, ADH is suppressed and urine osmolality is below 100 mOsm/kg, as in low solute intake and primary polydipsia. Finally, the kidney cannot dilute urine when thiazides act at the diluting segment, or excrete water in advanced CKD and AKI.

Etiology and mechanisms

Hypovolemic hyponatremia. Loop diuretics lose Na+ and water, and the volume loss releases ADH, but they cause hyponatremia less often than thiazides, because blocking NKCC2 also weakens the medullary gradient that ADH needs to concentrate urine. Thiazides block NCC in the distal tubule, a diluting segment, so urine cannot be made dilute while the intact gradient lets ADH keep concentrating it; hyponatremia usually appears within the first 2–3 weeks of treatment. In primary adrenal insufficiency, loss of aldosterone and cortisol causes Na+ wasting with hyperkalemia, salt craving, and skin pigmentation; hypoaldosteronism acts similarly, and ACEi and ARBs are an uncommon cause of low Na+. In cerebral salt wasting, after SAH or other brain injury, the kidney wastes Na+, possibly through BNP or reduced sympathetic drive, leaving the patient hypovolemic, unlike SIADH. Extra-renal causes are vomiting, in which nausea itself also releases ADH, diarrhea, sweating with salt replaced by water alone, burns, bleeding, and third spacing in pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, and low albumin.

Euvolemic hyponatremia. In SIADH, ADH is released or acts without a volume or osmotic trigger, from CNS disorders, lung disease, small cell lung cancer through ectopic ADH, drugs such as SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide, and MDMA, pain, nausea, and surgery. Hypothyroidism raises ADH through low output, usually only with marked thyroid failure. Cortisol normally inhibits ADH release, so in secondary adrenal insufficiency, cortisol deficiency releases ADH, while aldosterone is preserved by RAAS, so there is neither Na+ wasting nor hyperkalemia. Both must be excluded before SIADH is diagnosed. Tea and toast diets and beer potomania provide too little protein and salt, so at 50 mOsm/kg the kidney can excrete only a few liters of water a day, and beer's carbohydrate further limits protein breakdown and urea. Primary polydipsia exceeds what normal kidneys can excrete, about 18 L a day, and is most often psychogenic, particularly in schizophrenia; exercise-associated hyponatremia combines water intake with ADH released during exercise.

Hypervolemic hyponatremia. In HF, low output, and in cirrhosis, splanchnic vasodilation, underfill the arteries, so RAAS retains Na+ and water and ADH, released for low effective volume, retains extra water. In advanced CKD and AKI, too few working nephrons filter too little water to excrete intake, whatever ADH does, and nephrotic syndrome can also cause hypervolemic hyponatremia.

Clinical presentation and complications

Symptoms depend on how fast Na+ fell more than on how low it is. When plasma becomes dilute, water moves into brain cells, and an acute fall, within 48 hours, causes cerebral edema with headache, nausea, confusion, and seizures; raised ICP can lead to uncal herniation, coma, and death. Within about 48 hours, brain cells shed osmolytes and lose the extra water, so chronic hyponatremia causes few symptoms, but these adapted cells then lose water quickly if Na+ is corrected too fast. Mild hyponatremia, 130–135 mmol/L, causes fatigue, weakness, poor attention and memory, gait problems, falls, fractures, and osteoporosis; moderate hyponatremia, 125–129 mmol/L, adds drowsiness, cramps, nausea, and vomiting; and severe hyponatremia, below 125 mmol/L, causes confusion, somnolence, seizures, decreased consciousness, and cardiorespiratory collapse.

Osmotic demyelination syndrome follows overly rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia and damages pontine white matter, often in two phases: the encephalopathy improves as Na+ rises, then 3–5 days later dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriparesis, or locked-in syndrome appear, usually irreversibly. The risk is highest with chronic hyponatremia, Na+ below 120 mmol/L, alcohol use, malnutrition, hypokalemia, and liver disease. Untreated severe hyponatremia can also cause rhabdomyolysis.

Diagnosis

The workup, set out in the review of , first confirms with serum osmolality that the hyponatremia is hypotonic, excluding hypertonic hyponatremia and pseudohyponatremia, then reads the urine osmolality, volume status, and urine Na+.

Urine osmolality below 100 mOsm/kg points to primary polydipsia and low solute intake. With a higher urine osmolality, urine Na+ below 30 mmol/L points to extra-renal loss and third spacing, HF, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome, and above 30 mmol/L to diuretics, which make urine Na+ unreliable while they act, mineralocorticoid deficiency, with high K+, low glucose, and pigmentation in primary adrenal insufficiency, cerebral salt wasting after brain injury, SIADH, hypothyroidism, with high TSH and low thyroxine, secondary adrenal insufficiency, with low cortisol and ACTH but normal K+, and kidney failure. SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion, made only after hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, diuretics, and heart, liver, and kidney disease are excluded. Further tests include TSH and cortisol, with ACTH if secondary adrenal insufficiency is suspected; glucose, lipids, protein, and protein electrophoresis; creatinine, LFTs, and albumin; and CXR or CT of the chest and CT of the head to find the cause of SIADH.

Management

Treatment depends on symptoms, how fast Na+ fell, and volume status, and in chronic hyponatremia Na+ is raised by no more than 6–8 mmol/L in 24 hours. Severe symptoms, such as seizures, obtundation, or delirium, are treated with boluses of 3% saline to raise Na+ by 4–6 mmol/L, enough to relieve cerebral edema whatever the starting value, after which correction slows to stay within the limit, with frequent Na+ checks; D5W or desmopressin brings Na+ back down if it overshoots.

Hypovolemic hyponatremia is treated with NS, which restores volume and removes the stimulus for ADH, along with treatment of the cause; once volume is restored, ADH switches off and a water diuresis can raise Na+ too fast. Diuretics are stopped; primary adrenal insufficiency is treated with NS, hydrocortisone, and fludrocortisone; and cerebral salt wasting with NS, adding 3% saline if Na+ is very low. SIADH is treated by addressing its cause and restricting fluid, then with oral urea, which adds solute that carries water out, salt tablets with a loop diuretic, or a vaptan; NS can worsen SIADH, because the kidney excretes the salt in concentrated urine and keeps the water. Hypothyroidism is treated with levothyroxine and secondary adrenal insufficiency with hydrocortisone. In low solute intake, solute and food are restored gradually, because the risk of overcorrection is very high once new solute lets the kidney excrete the extra water, and primary polydipsia is treated by restricting water, after which Na+ can rise quickly. Hypervolemic hyponatremia is treated with water and salt restriction, a loop diuretic, which weakens the medullary gradient so that more free water is lost, and treatment of the cause, with dialysis in advanced kidney failure.

The mechanisms, uses, and risks of each treatment are compared in the review of ; vaptans are not used in cirrhosis, and tolvaptan is hepatotoxic.

It is diagnosed only after hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency are excluded, is treated with fluid restriction rather than saline, and is distinguished from cerebral salt wasting, which has the same urine findings but is hypovolemic and treated with saline.

and . Volume and Na+ concentration are separate: Na+ content sets the volume, and water sets the concentration. Hypovolemia causes hyponatremia when ADH, released for the low volume, retains the water that replaces the losses, and hypervolemia in HF and cirrhosis causes it when ADH adds water to the salt and water RAAS retains. Restoring volume in the first switches ADH off and can overcorrect Na+, whereas the second needs water restriction and diuretics.

and endocrine disorders. Hypernatremia: a water deficit that thirst normally prevents

Hypernatremia is a deficit of water relative to Na+, from water lost faster than it is replaced, ADH missing or ignored, or Na+ gained. Because a rise in osmolality drives powerful thirst, it develops mainly in people who cannot sense thirst or reach water: infants, older adults, and those with neurologic impairment or in hospital. Urine osmolality separates diabetes insipidus from renal and extra-renal water loss and Na+ gain, and treatment restores volume, replaces the water deficit slowly enough to avoid cerebral edema, and addresses the cause. Here, we review its mechanisms, causes, presentation, diagnosis, and treatment, and consider its relationship to diabetes insipidus, hyponatremia, volume disorders, and aldosterone excess.

Key points

  • Thirst, triggered at an osmolality of about 295 mOsm/kg, is the main defense, so hypernatremia almost always needs impaired thirst or no access to water.
  • Hypovolemic hypernatremia follows renal losses (osmotic diuresis, loop diuretics, kidney disease) or extra-renal losses with more water than Na+ (sweating, burns, vomiting, diarrhea); isotonic losses such as bleeding do not raise Na+.
  • Euvolemic hypernatremia follows pure water loss: insensible losses, central or nephrogenic DI, and too little intake; hypervolemic hypernatremia follows Na+ gain, usually from hypertonic saline or NaHCO3.
  • Urine osmolality below 300 mOsm/kg points to DI, 300–600 to osmotic diuresis, loop diuretics, kidney disease, or partial DI, and above 600 to extra-renal loss, low intake, or Na+ gain, which urine Na+ then separates.
  • Chronic hypernatremia is lowered by no more than 10–12 mmol/L in 24 hours, with the water deficit replaced over 48–72 hours, to avoid cerebral edema.
  • Central DI is treated with desmopressin, and nephrogenic DI by removing its cause and with a thiazide and a low-salt, low-protein diet.

Pathophysiology

When plasma Na+ and osmolality rise, hypothalamic osmoreceptors drive thirst, at about 295 mOsm/kg, and the posterior pituitary releases ADH, which inserts aquaporin-2 in the collecting duct so water returns to plasma and urine becomes concentrated. Hypernatremia therefore arises when water is lost faster than it is replaced, through the kidney or through the skin, gut, and lungs; when ADH is missing, in central DI, or ignored, in nephrogenic DI; or when Na+ is gained faster than the kidney excretes it. Because thirst is so powerful, it almost always also needs impaired thirst, infancy, old age, altered mental state, intubation, or no access to water; with intact thirst and access, even DI usually leaves Na+ near normal, at the cost of polyuria and polydipsia. Urine osmolality reflects the mechanism: below 300 mOsm/kg when ADH is missing or ignored, 300–600 with renal water loss, and above 600 with extra-renal loss, low intake, or Na+ gain.

Etiology and mechanisms

Hypovolemic hypernatremia. Water and Na+ are both lost, water more than Na+. Renal causes are osmotic diuresis, in which glucose in DKA and HHS, mannitol, or urea after an obstruction is relieved holds water in the tubule; loop diuretics, which weaken the medullary gradient so urine cannot be concentrated, whereas thiazides instead cause hyponatremia; and intrinsic kidney disease. Extra-renal causes are sweating from exercise, fever, or heat, since sweat has less Na+ than plasma; burns, with large evaporative losses; prolonged vomiting or NG drainage not replaced with water; and diarrhea from gastroenteritis, especially in infants given too little water. Isotonic losses such as bleeding do not raise Na+.

Euvolemic hypernatremia. Pure water loss comes mostly from cells, so the ECF is little changed. Insensible losses rise with fever, fast breathing, and heat. Central DI, too little ADH, is idiopathic in 30–50% and also follows head trauma, pituitary surgery, craniopharyngioma, and infiltrative disease such as sarcoidosis. In nephrogenic DI, the collecting duct ignores ADH, from inherited X-linked forms in children and lithium and hypercalcemia in adults, as well as severe hypokalemia, foscarnet, and demeclocycline. Too little water intake follows impaired thirst from hypothalamic damage, ineffective breastfeeding in infants, and older age, neurologic impairment, altered mental state, intubation, or lack of access to water.

Hypervolemic hypernatremia. Na+ is gained, usually in hospital from 3% saline or IV NaHCO3, and otherwise from improperly mixed infant formula, salt tablets, NaHCO3 ingestion, or seawater. Hyperaldosteronism retains Na+ but usually raises it only to the high-normal range, because thirst and ADH still act.

Clinical presentation and complications

Symptoms come from the brain and are most marked when Na+ rises quickly or exceeds about 160 mmol/L. As plasma becomes concentrated, water leaves brain cells and the brain shrinks, causing irritability and agitation, then lethargy, somnolence, coma, and seizures, with increased tone, brisk reflexes, and myoclonus. Alert patients are intensely thirsty unless thirst is impaired, infants have a high-pitched cry, the skin can feel doughy or velvety, and hypovolemic forms cause orthostatic hypotension and tachycardia, although dehydration is easily underestimated in children because water moves out of cells into the ECF. DI causes polyuria above 3 L a day with polydipsia. The most serious complication is intracranial bleeding, as the shrinking brain tears bridging veins and causes subarachnoid or subdural hemorrhage, and dural sinus thrombosis can occur. Over about 48 hours, brain cells gain solutes and regain water, so lowering Na+ too fast in chronic hypernatremia floods them and causes cerebral edema, seizures, and permanent brain damage. Infants, older adults, and anyone who depends on others for water are most at risk.

Diagnosis

The cause is usually clear from the history and examination, including losses, intake and access to water, drugs such as lithium and diuretics, and Na+ given; urine osmolality and urine Na+ confirm it, and the water deficit guides replacement, as set out in the review of . When DI is suspected, water deprivation followed by desmopressin separates central DI, in which urine osmolality rises, by over 100% when complete, from nephrogenic DI, in which it barely changes, and copeptin, a stable fragment released with ADH, is easier to measure than ADH itself. Glucose, Ca2+, K+, a lithium level, creatinine, urine volume, and renin and aldosterone complete the workup.

Management

Treatment addresses the cause, restores volume first with NS or LR if the patient is shocked, and then replaces free water, by mouth or feeding tube when possible and otherwise as D5W or half-normal saline, the fluids compared in the review of . Chronic hypernatremia is lowered by no more than 10–12 mmol/L in 24 hours, about 0.5 mmol/L an hour, with Na+ checked every 2–4 hours, whereas acute hypernatremia, under 48 hours, can be corrected faster; seizures during correction signal cerebral edema and mean the hypotonic fluid is stopped.

Osmotic diuresis is treated with fluids and insulin in DKA and HHS or by stopping mannitol, loop diuretics are stopped or reduced, and extra-renal losses are treated with isotonic fluid if volume is depleted and then hypotonic fluid. Insensible losses and low intake need free water and access to water. Central DI is treated with desmopressin by mouth or nose, at the risk of water intoxication and hyponatremia. Nephrogenic DI is treated by stopping lithium where possible and correcting Ca2+ and K+, with a thiazide, which causes mild volume loss so that more water is reabsorbed proximally and less reaches the collecting duct, a low-salt, low-protein diet, and NSAIDs. Na+ gain is treated by stopping the source and giving D5W with a loop diuretic to remove the Na+, with dialysis when the kidneys fail or the water needed would overload the patient, and hyperaldosteronism by treating the aldosterone excess.

In hyponatremia, the brain swells and fast correction causes osmotic demyelination; in hypernatremia, the brain shrinks and fast correction causes cerebral edema. Desmopressin for central DI can overshoot into hyponatremia, and thiazides lower Na+, which is why they help nephrogenic DI.

Disorders of plasma sodium: tonicity, the urine, and the pace of correction

Plasma Na+ reflects water rather than total body Na+: hyponatremia means too much water for the Na+ in the body and hypernatremia too little. Both disorders are approached by asking what the kidney is doing with water, using serum and urine osmolality, volume status, and urine Na+, and both are corrected at a limited rate once the brain has adapted, because rapid correction causes osmotic demyelination or cerebral edema. Here, we compare the two disorders, describe the workup of each, and review the fluids and drugs that raise or lower plasma Na+.

Key points

  • Serum osmolality separates true, hypotonic hyponatremia from hypertonic hyponatremia and pseudohyponatremia.
  • In hyponatremia, urine osmolality below 100 mOsm/kg means ADH is suppressed, as in primary polydipsia and low solute intake.
  • Urine Na+ below 30 mmol/L in hyponatremia means the kidney is holding Na+ because it senses low effective volume.
  • In hypernatremia, urine osmolality below 300 mOsm/kg suggests DI, 300–600 a renal water loss, and above 600 a loss elsewhere or Na+ gained.
  • Water deficit = total body water × (Na+ ÷ 140 − 1), replaced over 48–72 hours with ongoing losses.
  • Chronic hyponatremia is raised by no more than 6–8 mmol/L in 24 hours, and chronic hypernatremia lowered by no more than 10–12 mmol/L.

Comparison

Because Na+ cannot move without water but water can move without Na+, a change in plasma Na+ concentration reflects a change in water. In hyponatremia, plasma becomes dilute, water enters brain cells, and cerebral edema follows; in hypernatremia, water leaves brain cells and the brain shrinks, so bridging veins can tear. Over about 48 hours, brain cells adapt by shedding or gaining solutes, which is why chronic disorders cause fewer symptoms and why correcting them too fast causes osmotic demyelination syndrome after hyponatremia and cerebral edema after hypernatremia.

Diagnosis

In hyponatremia, serum osmolality, 2 × Na+ + glucose + urea, normally 275–295 mOsm/kg, is checked first: it is high when glucose, mannitol, or radiocontrast draw water out of cells, normal in pseudohyponatremia from very high lipids or proteins, and low in true hyponatremia. Urine osmolality below 100 mOsm/kg then shows that ADH is suppressed and intake outruns excretion, while a higher value shows that ADH is acting. Volume status and urine Na+ complete the workup: urine Na+ below 30 mmol/L means the kidney is holding Na+, after extra-renal loss or in HF and cirrhosis, while a higher value points to renal loss, SIADH, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, or kidney failure.

In hypernatremia, the cause is usually clear from the history and volume status. Urine osmolality below 300 mOsm/kg means ADH is missing or ignored, as in DI; 300–600 mOsm/kg means a solute, a loop diuretic, kidney disease, or partial DI; and above 600 mOsm/kg means the kidney is concentrating, so water has been lost elsewhere or not taken in, or Na+ has been gained, which urine Na+ separates. The water deficit, total body water × (Na+ ÷ 140 − 1), guides replacement.

Management

Low Na+ is raised with 3% saline for severe symptoms, NS for hypovolemic forms, and water restriction, salt restriction, loop diuretics, vaptans, oral urea, or salt tablets according to the cause; desmopressin or D5W bring the Na+ back down if correction is too fast. High Na+ is lowered with water by mouth or tube where possible, otherwise D5W or ½NS, after NS or LR to restore volume in shock; desmopressin treats central DI, a thiazide nephrogenic DI, and a loop diuretic with D5W removes gained Na+. Na+ is checked often during correction.
