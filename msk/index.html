Osteoarthritis

Pathophysiology

Overload or a weakened joint makes breakdown outpace repair in cartilage, bone, synovium, ligaments, and capsule, so the whole joint remodels and slowly fails; inflammation is low-grade and driven by the damage, not the cause of it.

  1. Abnormal load or jointFrom a cause below
  2. Chondrocytes turn catabolicThey release aggrecanases and MMPs
  3. Matrix is lostAggrecan first, then type II collagen
  4. Debris inflames the synoviumCytokines return to the cartilage and repeat the cycle
  5. Bone respondsSclerosis, osteophytes, and cysts
  6. Pain and stiffnessFrom every structure with nerves, not from cartilage

What happens to the joint

  • ACartilageEarly: ADAMTS-4 and ADAMTS-5 cleave aggrecan, which resists compression, so the surface frays. Late: MMP-13 and other MMPs break down type II collagen, so fissures reach bone, which is left bare
  • BSubchondral boneThickens (sclerosis) under load; cysts form where bone marrow lesions break down, in advanced disease
  • COsteophytesTGF-β makes cartilage grow at the joint margin and ossify, widening and stiffening the joint; enthesophytes form at tendon insertions
  • DSynovium and fluidMacrophage-led synovitis releases IL-6, IL-8, IL-17, and TNF-α; hyaluronic acid and lubricin fall, so the fluid lubricates less
  • ECapsule, ligaments, and meniscusCapsule fibrosis (TGF-β) limits movement; altered or torn ligaments and meniscus make the joint unstable and concentrate load
  • FVessels and nervesGrow into subchondral bone and synovium; NGF sensitizes them, causing hyperalgesia and allodynia

The inflammatory cycle

  1. Matrix fragmentsAct as danger signals
  2. Synovial macrophagesRelease IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α
  3. ChondrocytesMake more aggrecanases and MMPs
  4. More fragmentsThe cycle continues

Why it hurts

  • Subchondral bone and periosteum
  • Synovium
  • Joint capsule
  • Ligaments
  • Outer third of the meniscus
  • Muscle and other soft tissue

Note: Cartilage has no nerves, so pain matches x-ray damage poorly: a worn joint may be painless, and a painful one may look normal.

Etiology

  • Primary: risk factors, no single cause
  • 1AgeUsually after 50; cartilage repairs less well with age
  • 2Sex and genesAbout 2 women to 1 man; inherited, above all hand OA in families
  • 3ObesityExtra load, plus adipokines and cytokines from fat, so even hand OA is more common
  • 4InjuryACL or meniscal tear, or a fracture into the joint: post-traumatic OA, often in younger people
  • 5OccupationRepeated kneeling, squatting, or heavy lifting; ordinary recreational exercise is not a cause
  • 6Alignment and muscleVarus loads the medial knee and valgus the lateral; weak quadriceps leave the knee less protected
  • Secondary: a disease of the joint
  • 7Other arthritisRA, psoriatic arthritis, septic arthritis, gout, and CPPD
  • 8Bone and joint shapeAVN, Paget disease of bone, hip dysplasia, and osteochondritis dissecans
  • 9Metabolic and endocrineHemochromatosis, Wilson disease, ochronosis (alkaptonuria), and acromegaly
  • 10Blood and connective tissueHemophilia and sickle cell disease; Ehlers-Danlos and Marfan syndromes

Note: Obesity, injury, and occupation (3–5) matter most in practice because they can be changed.

Symptoms and complications

  1. EarlySharp, predictable pain with heavy use
  2. MiddleMore constant pain that limits daily activities; locking
  3. AdvancedDull pain at rest and at night, with bursts of severe pain

The pattern

  • PainWorse with activity, better with rest; gradual onset
  • StiffnessIn the morning for less than 30 minutes, and after sitting still (gelling)
  • MovementRestricted, with crepitus and joint line tenderness
  • SwellingHard and bony; any effusion is small and cool
  • FunctionGiving way, locking, muscle weakness, and difficulty with stairs, dressing, or grip
  • Systemic featuresNone: no fever, weight loss, or rash

Where it strikes

  • ADIP jointsHeberden nodes: bony swellings
  • BPIP jointsBouchard nodes; the joint space is harder to feel
  • CThumb base (CMC-1)Squaring of the thumb base; MCP-1 hyperextends to make up for lost movement; weak pinch
  • DHipGroin pain, sometimes felt in the thigh or knee; internal rotation is lost early
  • EKneeOften medial, so varus; crepitus, quadriceps wasting, and an effusion or Baker cyst. Patellofemoral OA hurts on stairs and rising from a chair
  • FSpineLower cervical and lumbar; osteophytes can narrow the nerve root exits, causing radiculopathy
  • GBig toe (MTP-1)Pain on push-off; hallux valgus

Note: OA is usually symmetrical, though one joint may lead; MCP, wrist, elbow, or ankle disease suggests a secondary cause or another arthritis.

Erosive hand OA

An aggressive, inflammatory form in older women, with painful, swollen DIP and PIP joints and central erosions that give a gull-wing shape on x-ray; RA erodes the margins instead.

Complications

  • Deformity and instabilityVarus or valgus knee; angled fingers
  • Muscle wasting and fallsWeak quadriceps and a painful gait
  • Baker cystCan rupture into the calf and mimic DVT
  • RadiculopathyFrom spinal osteophytes
  • Low mood and poor sleepPain, inactivity, and weight gain feed each other
  • DisabilityLoss of work and independence; joint replacement

Diagnosis

OA is a clinical diagnosis: age over 45, pain with activity, and morning stiffness of less than 30 minutes or none need no imaging or bloods unless something does not fit.

  1. HistoryPain, stiffness, giving way, and locking
  2. Whole personFunction, mood, sleep, work, beliefs, and support
  3. ExaminationBony enlargement, crepitus, and range of movement; look at the joint above
  4. Red flagsInfection, inflammation, fracture, or tumor
  5. X-rayOnly if it would change the plan

Find the cause

  • 1AgeNone; under 45, look for a secondary cause (4, 7–10)
  • 2Sex and genesFamily history; hand examination
  • 3ObesityBMI and waist; blood pressure, glucose, and lipids
  • 4InjuryPast injury or surgery; stability tests
  • 5OccupationWork and sport history
  • 6Alignment and muscleStanding alignment, gait, and quadriceps strength
  • 7Other arthritisAspiration; RF and anti-CCP; chondrocalcinosis on x-ray suggests CPPD
  • 8Bone and joint shapeX-ray of the hip and knee; MRI for AVN
  • 9Metabolic and endocrineMCP-2 and MCP-3 disease: ferritin and transferrin saturation for hemochromatosis
  • 10Blood and connective tissueCoagulation studies or hemoglobin electrophoresis; hypermobility and family history

Note: A normal x-ray does not exclude OA, and x-ray changes do not prove that OA is the cause of the pain.

Treatment

No treatment yet reverses OA, so the aim is less pain and better function: start with education, exercise, and weight loss for everyone, add drugs when needed, and keep surgery for failure of both.

  1. EveryoneEducation, exercise, and weight loss
  2. AddTopical NSAIDs, aids, and braces
  3. ThenOral NSAIDs at the lowest dose, for the shortest time
  4. FlareA corticosteroid injection
  5. End stageJoint replacement

Gout and Pseudogout

Pathophysiology

Crystals form in and around joints, of urate when its level stays high (gout) and of CPP when cartilage makes too much PPi (pseudogout), and macrophages that swallow them release IL-1β, causing sudden neutrophilic arthritis.

  1. SupersaturationUrate above about 0.40 mmol/L, or excess PPi
  2. Crystals formSlowly, over months to years
  3. Crystals shedBy a trigger, into the joint
  4. Inflammasome firesNLRP3 and caspase-1 release IL-1β
  5. FlareNeutrophils flood in
  6. ResolutionNETs trap crystals, even untreated

Making and clearing urate

  • AXanthine oxidaseMakes xanthine, then urate; blocked by allopurinol and febuxostat
  • BKidneyAbout 90% of filtered urate is reabsorbed by URAT1 and GLUT9; probenecid blocks URAT1. Underexcretion causes most gout
  • CGutABCG2; matters more when the kidney fails
  • DNo uricaseLost in human evolution; pegloticase and rasburicase replace it

From crystal to flare

  • Signal 1Crystals engage TLRs, Fc receptors, and integrins: NF-κB makes pro-IL-1β
  • Signal 2Swallowed crystals rupture the phagosome: NLRP3 activates caspase-1, which frees IL-1β
  • AmplificationIL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α draw in neutrophils, which release enzymes and oxidants
  • ShutdownNETs pack crystals and digest cytokines; TGF-β rises; protein coats change
  • TophusPacked crystals ringed by macrophages, giant cells, and fibrous tissue, which erode bone through osteoclasts

How CPP forms

  • SourceChondrocytes export ATP and PPi (ANKH); ENPP1 turns ATP into PPi
  • NormallyAlkaline phosphatase breaks PPi down, and needs Mg2+
  • In diseasePPi builds up and binds Ca2+ in cartilage and fibrocartilage; aging, OA, iron, high Ca2+, and low Mg2+ favor it

Etiology

  • Gout
  • 1Kidney underexcretionCKD, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, dehydration, and ketosis or lactate competing for transport; lead nephropathy
  • 2DrugsThiazide and loop diuretics, low-dose aspirin, cyclosporine, tacrolimus, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol
  • 3OverproductionPsoriasis, hemolysis, myeloproliferative disease, TLS, obesity; HGPRT deficiency (Lesch-Nyhan syndrome), PRPP synthetase overactivity, and von Gierke disease
  • 4Diet and alcoholMeat, seafood, beer and spirits, and fructose drinks; dairy, cherries, and weight loss protect
  • 5Genes, sex, and ageURAT1, GLUT9, and ABCG2 variants; men, and women after menopause, because estrogen promotes excretion
  • 6Flare triggersAlcohol, feasting or fasting, dehydration, trauma, surgery, acute illness, diuretics, vaccination, and starting ULT
  • Calcium pyrophosphate (pseudogout)
  • 7Age and OAThe main risks: rare under 60, and often in joints already damaged
  • 8Metabolic diseaseHemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia (Gitelman syndrome), and hypophosphatasia
  • 9FamilialANKH mutations: early, many joints
  • 10Flare triggersSurgery (above all parathyroidectomy), acute illness, trauma, and diuretics

Note: CPP disease before 60 is metabolic or familial until proved otherwise.

Symptoms and complications

  1. HyperuricemiaSilent, for years
  2. FlarePeaks within 24 hours; gone in 3–14 days
  3. IntercriticalWell, but crystals and low-grade inflammation persist
  4. More flaresAbout 60% within 1 year; more joints
  5. TophaceousUsually 10 years or more untreated

Where they strike

  • HMTP-1 (podagra)First site in about half of gout, and affected in most eventually
  • IKnee and wristThe usual sites of CPP disease, with the MCP joints
  • JOdontoid pegCrowned dens syndrome: acute neck pain, stiffness, and fever, mimicking meningitis

The gout flare

  • OnsetSudden, often at night or early morning
  • PainSevere: cannot bear a sheet or weight on it
  • SkinRed, hot, and shiny beyond the joint, then peels
  • PatternOne joint at first, usually in the leg; later several
  • SystemicFever and high WBC are possible, so it can look septic

Diagnosis

Crystals in joint fluid make the diagnosis of either disease, and the fluid must also be cultured, because crystals do not exclude infection.

  1. Hot jointSeptic until proved otherwise
  2. AspirateCells, Gram stain, culture, and crystals
  3. Name the crystalShape and birefringence
  4. ImageIf no fluid, or to show damage
  5. Find the causeUrate and kidney, or the CPP screen

Crystals under polarized light

  • KMSUNeedle-shaped and strongly negative: yellow when parallel to the slow axis
  • LCPPRhomboid or rod, and weakly positive: blue when parallel; easily missed

  • FTophus and erosionPunched-out, with an overhanging edge and preserved joint space until late; x-ray is normal early. DECT colors urate
  • GChondrocalcinosisThin line in hyaline cartilage, meniscus, wrist (triangular fibrocartilage), or pubic symphysis; x-ray misses over half

Treatment

Treat the flare quickly with whichever anti-inflammatory is safest for the patient; then, in gout, lower urate to target for life so the crystals dissolve, and in CPP disease, where none can be dissolved, treat the cause and prevent flares.

  1. Exclude infectionBefore any corticosteroid in the joint
  2. Treat the flareWithin 24 hours; rest and ice
  3. Decide on ULTGout only
  4. Start lowWith flare prophylaxis
  5. Treat to targetTitrate, then lifelong

Rheumatoid Arthritis

Pathophysiology

In genetically susceptible people, mucosal injury breaks tolerance to citrullinated proteins, and the autoimmune synovitis that follows grows a pannus that destroys cartilage, bone, and ligament.

  1. Mucosa injuredSmoke, gums, or gut
  2. Proteins citrullinatedBy PAD enzymes
  3. ACPA and RFYears before symptoms
  4. SynovitisA second hit reaches the joint
  5. PannusInvades cartilage and bone
  6. DeformityLigaments fail

How it builds

  • ACPABind citrullinated fibrinogen and others, and drive macrophages through TLR4 and Fc receptors to make TNF-α; activate complement
  • RFUsually IgM against the Fc of IgG: immune complexes and complement
  • T cellsMeet citrullinated peptides on HLA-DRB1; make IFN-γ and IL-17
  • Why systemicIL-6 drives CRP, anemia, and platelets; TNF-α drives weight loss and vessel disease

What happens to the joint

  • ASynoviumType A (macrophage-like) and type B (fibroblast-like) cells multiply; lymphocytes and plasma cells gather
  • BPannusInvading synovium, with giant cells; held by IFN-γ, IL-3, and GM-CSF
  • CMarginal erosionRANKL and TNF-α make osteoclasts, first where bone is bare
  • DCartilageThins evenly: MMPs up, TIMPs down
  • EFluidNeutrophils, and rice bodies of fibrin and dead cells
  • FCapsule and ligamentsStretch, so joints subluxate

Destructive yet sustained

  1. Pannus outgrows its bloodHypoxia
  2. HIF-1 activeHelped by IL-1 and TNF-α
  3. VEGF and MMPsLeaky new vessels; matrix cut
  4. GlycolysisLactate fuels it, like a tumor
  5. More cytokinesThe loop repeats

Why the lining does not die

  • Survival signalsBcl-2 (by IL-15), Akt (by TGF-β), NF-κB, STAT3, and FLIP increased
  • p53Mutated and nonfunctional, so apoptosis fails

Septic Arthritis

Pathophysiology

Bacteria reach the synovium, usually by blood, and the neutrophils they draw destroy cartilage within days, so a hot joint is an emergency.

  1. Bacteremia or entrySynovium has no basement membrane
  2. AdhesionStaphylococcal adhesins bind joint proteins
  3. Neutrophils flood inEnzymes and cytokines
  4. Pressure risesBlood supply squeezed
  5. Cartilage lostWithin days; then bone

How it gets in, and what it does

  • ABloodMost: from skin, lung, urine, heart valve, or line
  • BDirectInjection, arthroscopy, bite, or wound
  • CNearbyOsteomyelitis, where metaphysis lies inside the capsule (hip, shoulder)
  • DPusNeutrophils, usually above 50000/µL
  • ECartilageBacterial toxins, neutrophil proteases, and MMPs; cartilage cannot repair
  • FBone endHigh pressure causes avascular necrosis, above all at a child's hip

Why damage goes on after ABX

  • Dead bacteriaStill drive cytokines
  • Neutrophil debrisKeeps enzymes in the joint
  • SoDrainage matters as much as ABX

Note: A joint already damaged, above all by RA, is the likeliest to be infected and the easiest to mistake for a flare.

Etiology

  • Common
  • 1Staphylococcus aureusThe most common at nearly every age; MRSA by region, health care, and injected drugs
  • 2StreptococciGroups A, B, C, and G, and pneumococcus
  • 3Gram-negative bacilliOlder, immunosuppressed, urinary source; Pseudomonas with injected drugs or punctures; Salmonella in sickle cell disease
  • 4GonococcusYoung and sexually active; terminal complement deficiency (C5–C9)
  • Special
  • 5Children's organismsGroup B streptococci in newborns; Kingella kingae under 3 years
  • 6Prosthesis or surgeryEarly: Staphylococcus aureus. Delayed: coagulase-negative. Late: from blood
  • 7Slow infectionsTB, fungi, Lyme disease, and Brucella: weeks to months

Who is at risk

  • Age over 80
  • Diabetes
  • RA or damaged joint
  • Prosthesis
  • Recent joint surgery or injection
  • Skin infection or ulcer
  • Injected drugs
  • HIV or immunosuppression
  • Dialysis
  • Children

Treatment

Psoriatic arthritis is treated to remission, with the drug chosen by the worst domain; reactive arthritis usually needs only NSAIDs while it settles, with ABX for Chlamydia.

  1. MildNSAIDs; inject a joint
  2. Psoriatic, activeMethotrexate early
  3. Not at targetBiological by domain
  4. Reactive, lastingSulfasalazine
  5. RefractoryTNF inhibitor

Take care

  • Oral corticosteroid in psoriasis: flare on withdrawal
  • Hydroxychloroquine may worsen skin
  • IL-17 inhibitor: candida, IBD flare
  • TB and hepatitis before biologicals
  • JAK inhibitor: clots, heart, cancer
  • Long ABX: generally not recommended in chronic reactive arthritis, and no role in psoriatic

Diagnosis

Exclude infection first in any hot joint by aspirating it, then count joints, date the onset, and test only for what the pattern suggests.

  1. Joint or not?Bursa, tendon, or referred pain
  2. Hot?Aspirate today
  3. How many?One, few, or many
  4. How long?6 weeks divides
  5. Inflammatory?Stiffness, CRP
  6. Elsewhere?Skin, eyes, gut, genitals

Red flags: same day

  • Hot joint with fever
  • Prosthetic joint
  • Immunosuppressed
  • Child not walking
  • Neck pain with weakness in RA
  • Red eye with lost vision
  • Fracture or tumor signs

Note: Arthritis lasting under 6 weeks is often viral or reactive and may settle, but synovitis beyond that needs referral, because treatment within 3 months prevents damage.

Treatment

Treat infection within hours, relieve pain while the cause is found, and start a disease-modifying drug early in persistent inflammatory arthritis.

  1. HoursSeptic: drain and ABX
  2. DaysCrystal: colchicine, NSAID, or corticosteroid
  3. WeeksViral and reactive: NSAID, watch
  4. MonthsRA and psoriatic: DMARD
  5. YearsOA: exercise, weight, replacement
